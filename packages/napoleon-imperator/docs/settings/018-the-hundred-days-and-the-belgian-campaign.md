# The Hundred Days and the Belgian Campaign

This file records the political and military reconstitution of Napoleon's power after his return from Elba, the invasion of Belgium, and the two actions of 16 June 1815 — Ligny and Quatre-Bras — that set up Waterloo, at the standard `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` sets for the run-up to a decisive battle, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`. The escape from Elba itself is already recorded at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-first-abdication-elba`; Waterloo's own terrain, order of battle, chronology, and casualties are at `settings/019-waterloo.md`. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Army reconstitution {#hundred-days-army-reconstitution}

**Standing: established by research.** On returning to Paris 20 March 1815, Napoleon inherited a regular army of 56,000 soldiers, of which 46,000 were immediately campaign-ready. He counted on returning prisoners of war from Russia, Germany, Britain, and Spain to furnish, in the framing of a widely cited secondary account, "a trained, veteran and patriotic army far larger than that which had won renown in the years before 1814." By late May the total force under arms had reached roughly 198,000, with a further 66,000 training in depots but not yet deployable; by June, forces were organized into the Armée du Nord alongside separate corps screening the eastern frontiers against Austria and Russia, whose own armies were still mobilizing.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Hundred Days," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days.

## The Acte additionnel and the outlawry of Napoleon {#hundred-days-political-frame}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact wording of the Congress of Vienna's outlawry declaration not independently verified in this research pass.** On 22 April 1815, drafted with the liberal theorist Benjamin Constant, the Acte additionnel gave France a hereditary Chamber of Peers and an elected Chamber of Representatives, extended the franchise, and guaranteed press freedom; the plebiscite that ratified it drew only 1,532,527 votes, less than half the turnout of the Consulate-era plebiscites, a measure of how thin Napoleon's popular mandate actually was on his return. On 13 March 1815 the Congress of Vienna, then still in session, declared Napoleon an outlaw — this canon has not independently verified the exact wording of that declaration, commonly rendered as branding him "the enemy and disturber of the tranquillity of the world," in this research pass, and treats the substance of the outlawry as established while flagging the precise phrasing as unconfirmed. On 25 March, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and Britain committed to field 150,000 men each against him, formally activating the Seventh Coalition. Wellington's and Blücher's armies were ready and concentrated in Belgium by June, well ahead of the larger Austrian and Russian armies under Schwarzenberg and Barclay de Tolly, whose own invasion was not planned until 1 July; Napoleon's strategic logic in striking first was explicit — the British and Prussian armies were widely dispersed and might be defeated in detail before the rest of the Coalition could combine, and a quick victory might bring the whole Coalition to negotiate rather than fight on.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Hundred Days," as above.

## The crossing at Charleroi {#hundred-days-charleroi-crossing}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact French strength given as a range.** On 15 June 1815 Napoleon's Armée du Nord — commonly cited at 128,000 (Chesney, 1868), with other figures running 124,000–126,000 with roughly 350 guns — crossed the frontier at Thuin near Charleroi, securing what a widely cited account calls Napoleon's favored "central position," at the junction between Wellington's army to the north-west and Blücher's Prussians to the north-east, aiming to prevent the two from combining. Wellington reacted slowly: he did not learn of Charleroi's fall until three in the afternoon, because a message from his intelligence chief, Colquhoun Grant, was delayed by General Dörnberg. Napoleon placed Marshal Ney in command of the French left wing and ordered him to secure the crossroads of Quatre-Bras; Ney's scouts reached the crossroads that evening, but the actual attack was deferred to the following day — a delay much criticized afterward and recorded at `settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-quatre-bras`.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Waterloo campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign, which attributes the 128,000-strong Armée du Nord figure there to Colville Chesney, *Waterloo Lectures* (1868), p. 51 — this canon has not opened Chesney's book directly and repeats the figure as Wikipedia's synthesis.

## Command friction: Berthier's absence and Soult as chief of staff {#hundred-days-command-friction}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact manner of Berthier's death disputed among accident, suicide, and murder, per `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-berthier`.** Berthier, chief of staff to Napoleon from 1796 through the first abdication of 1814, refused to rejoin him for the Hundred Days and withdrew instead to Bamberg in Bavaria; he died there 1 June 1815, falling from an upstairs window of the Neue Residenz with a sill four feet from the floor, "making an accident seem unlikely" — this canon's existing standing on the cause, disputed among accident, suicide (at the sight of Russian troops marching to invade France), and assassination, is unchanged and recorded in full at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-berthier`. His absence left Marshal Soult as chief of staff for the only time in his career, a role historians widely judge him to have filled less capably than his record as a field commander would suggest; a modern military historian's judgment, cited here as a named characterization rather than this canon's own verdict, is that Soult "lacked his predecessor Marshal Louis-Alexandre Berthier's administrative skills." The clearest documented consequence is Soult's written order to Grouchy on 18 June, instructing him to move against the Anglo-allied left flank to block Prussian reinforcement — a source this canon cites calls the wording "almost impenetrable nonsense," and Grouchy, reading it as directing him against the Prussian rearguard specifically, marched on Wavre rather than toward the sound of Waterloo's guns (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-grouchy-detachment`).

Sources: Wikipedia, "Louis-Alexandre Berthier," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Alexandre_Berthier; "Jean-de-Dieu Soult," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-de-Dieu_Soult, which attributes the "impenetrable nonsense" characterization of Soult's order there to Bernard Cornwell, *Waterloo: The History of Four Days, Three Armies and Three Battles* (2014) — a work of narrative history rather than an archival monograph, which this canon has not opened directly and cites only as Wikipedia's own quotation of it; `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-berthier`, established by research.

## Ligny {#hundred-days-ligny}

**Standing: established by research.** Fought 16 June 1815 on the Scheldt–Meuse watershed, the battlefield centered on the Ligny brook, which meanders northeast through the village of Ligny toward Sombreffe, with the fortified villages of Ligny, Saint-Amand, and Wagnelée strung along it and grain fields described as "high as a man" elsewhere on the field; Blücher's headquarters was the windmill at Brye, Napoleon's the windmill of Naveau near Fleurus. Napoleon's Armée du Nord, roughly 62,882 with 210 guns, faced Blücher's Armee vom Niederrhein, roughly 83,417 with 224 guns, a third of whose infantry were untrained militia and recent Saxon or Rhenish conscripts of doubtful reliability. Napoleon delayed his attack until he heard cannon fire from Quatre-Bras. Fighting see-sawed through the day at Saint-Amand (a French counterattack by Steinmetz's Prussians recaptured it after Jagow's initial retreat, and French General Girard was mortally wounded) and at Ligny village itself, where Gérard's Pécheux division took the church but came under fire from three sides, losing twenty officers and five hundred men. Around 17:00 d'Erlon's I Corps appeared on the field, apparently poised to flank the Prussians decisively, but conflicting orders between Napoleon and Ney sent it into a costly about-face without engaging in either battle that day — the same command failure recorded at `settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-quatre-bras`. Blücher, learning that Wellington could not reinforce him, launched a final counterattack around 19:00, repelled by Imperial Guard cavalry; a combined French assault by the Guard, Gérard's corps, and Milhaud's cavalry, opened by a "crashing salvo of sixty guns" around 19:45, broke the Prussian center by roughly 20:30. Leading one of the charges in person, the seventy-two-year-old Blücher's horse was shot and fell on him; he was rescued in a semi-conscious condition and evacuated, and Gneisenau assumed temporary command. French casualties ran 8,300–12,000 killed, wounded, or captured; Prussian casualties ran to 16,000 killed or wounded plus a further 8,000 deserters, captured, or missing, and twenty-one guns lost.

The defeat was not decisive because the Prussian army retreated in squares, in good order despite being surrounded, repelling further attacks as it went. Gneisenau, in temporary command, ordered the retreat north toward Tilly and Wavre — keeping the army in contact with Wellington — rather than east toward the Prussian supply line; Blücher, on recovering, endorsed the choice, reasoning that the defeat had reduced his force by only about a sixth and that with nearly 100,000 men he could still turn Wellington's coming battle into a victory. By 18 June the reconstituted Prussian army reached Waterloo and did exactly that (`settings/019-waterloo.md`).

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Ligny," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ligny.

## Quatre-Bras {#hundred-days-quatre-bras}

**Standing: established by research.** Fought simultaneously with Ligny on 16 June, the action turned on a single crossroads: if the French held it, they could prevent Wellington's forces moving south-east along the Nivelles–Namur road to support the Prussians. Ney commanded the French left, 18,000–24,000 strong; Wellington had only some 8,000 troops present initially, under the Prince of Orange, reinforced to 32,000–36,000 by the day's end. Ney took no serious steps to capture the crossroads until past noon, though he could have done so with relative ease earlier in the day. The outcome was a genuine tactical draw that read as a strategic French success regardless: Wellington held the field at dusk, but Ney had prevented him from marching to Blücher's aid at Ligny, which mattered more than the ground itself. Casualties ran to 4,000–4,400 French against 4,800–5,600 Allied.

The day's most consequential single failure was d'Erlon's I Corps, which spent it marching and countermarching on the Old Roman Road between the two battlefields without engaging at either. Around a quarter past an unrecorded hour, Ney learned that I Corps, without his order or knowledge, had moved east to assist at Ligny, and sent orders for it to return at once; fifteen minutes later, at 17:30, he received an unclear order from Napoleon to seize Quatre-Bras and then turn east to crush Blücher. The corps marched back and forth between the two fights, arriving at neither in time to matter. Historians treat d'Erlon as blameless for the failure — Napoleon himself did not hold him to account — and record the judgment that "if the I Corps had engaged in either battle the outcome of the campaign might have been different."

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Quatre Bras," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Quatre_Bras; "Jean-Baptiste Drouet, Comte d'Erlon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet,_Comte_d%27Erlon, which attributes the two verdicts quoted above to the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed. (1911), and to A. F. Becke (1911), p. 377 — this canon located but did not independently reach the 1911 *Britannica*'s own d'Erlon entry via Wikisource in this research pass, and has not opened Becke directly; both verdicts are repeated as Wikipedia reports them.

## Grouchy's detachment {#hundred-days-grouchy-detachment}

**Standing: established by research, with the historians' verdict genuinely split between a traditional reading that dates Grouchy's failure to 17 June and a more sympathetic reading that emphasizes the ambiguity of Soult's written order.** On 17 June, Napoleon moved the bulk of his army in pursuit of Wellington and detached Marshal Grouchy with the right wing — 33,000 men and eighty guns — to pursue the retreating Prussians, with written and verbal orders to march on Wavre and engage them there; the orders were both ambiguous and late in arriving. On the morning of 18 June, Napoleon's further orders reached Grouchy around six o'clock and his corps began moving at eight; by noon the guns of Waterloo were audible. Gérard and other corps commanders urged Grouchy to "march to the sound of the guns," but this ran contrary to Napoleon's standing instruction — that Grouchy act as "the sword against the Prussians' back, driving them through Wavre" and rejoin Napoleon only afterward — so Grouchy declined, not yet understanding, as neither he nor Napoleon then understood, that the Prussian army was no longer either routed or disorganized. A second order repeating the same instructions arrived around four in the afternoon, closing off any late course-correction. Grouchy's own later account cites a further, chilling consideration: Ney had been rebuked by Napoleon only two days earlier for exceeding his orders at Quatre-Bras, discouraging exactly the kind of improvisation "march to the sound of the guns" would have required.

Grouchy opened the Battle of Wavre at four in the afternoon, Vandamme unlimbering three batteries and pushing Habert's division to force the bridges in a vicious street fight; his 33,000 faced only Thielemann's Prussian III Corps of 17,000, and the fighting was indecisive despite French numerical superiority — a French tactical win by day's end, the Prussians in retreat, but at the cost that mattered: Thielemann's 17,000 had tied down Grouchy's entire 33,000, troops that could otherwise have reached Waterloo, while giving Blücher time to march some 50,000 troops to Wellington's aid there instead. The traditional historians' verdict, embedded in the standard campaign narrative, dates Grouchy's failure earlier than the ambiguous-orders episode of the 18th: he "was slow in taking up the pursuit after Ligny," letting Blücher fall back largely unmolested, regroup, and execute the flank march that decided Waterloo. Grouchy himself, both immediately and across the following decades, argued in print that he had followed his orders correctly and was scapegoated for a defeat not of his making — publishing *Observations sur la relation de la campagne de 1815 par le général de Gourgaud* (1818) and the multi-part *Fragments Historiques Relatifs à la Campagne de 1815 et à la Bataille de Waterloo* (1829–1830); this canon has not been able to retrieve direct quotation from either work, only their existence and titles, and records his defense as a documented fact of his later life rather than as a settled historiographical verdict. Grouchy had been made a Marshal of the Empire only on 5 April 1815, against the explicit recommendation of Marshal Davout, then Minister of War — a detail this canon treats as evidence that at least one of his own peers doubted his fitness for independent command from the outset, without treating that doubt as proof of the Waterloo failure itself.
