**Status:** Source-verified. The decisive battle was fought on 2 December 1805 near Slavkov u Brna, then Austerlitz, in Moravia. The French army had occupied Vienna after the capitulation of Ulm. Russian and Austrian forces were converging in Moravia while Napoleon sought to force a decision before the coalition could consolidate its separate national commands and threaten his communications toward Vienna.

The date is 11 Frimaire Year XIV in the French Republican calendar. The French official bulletin dated 12 Frimaire and published the next day describes the battle after the fact; it is evidence of the French report and its self-presentation, not a neutral casualty ledger. The battlefield setting therefore begins with an active campaign state: soldiers have marched, roads carry recent traffic, staff officers possess reports of Ulm and Vienna, and neither army has a complete view of the other. A scene set before first contact must preserve that accumulated fatigue and incomplete information.

This owner fixes the temporal boundary. Events after the battle, including the armistice and Treaty of Pressburg, belong to later settings owners. The formal storyline may use the achieved battlefield victory as a transition point, but no settings fact here schedules that reveal.

**Status:** Source-verified in disposition; local camp detail is bounded reconstruction. During the night of 1-2 December, French forces reduced their visible occupation of the Pratzen Heights and placed the principal striking formations behind lower ground west of the ridge. This movement supported the appearance that the French centre and right were vulnerable. It did not mean that the ridge or the surrounding roads were empty: sentries, staff officers, guns, horses, fires, and formed units remained part of the night position.

The night movement imposes material limits. A gun cannot be shifted without teams and a usable route; infantry cannot change position silently at parade-ground speed; orders must find a bivouac in darkness; and men who march or stand through a cold night begin the morning with less reserve. Fires illuminate some faces and hide others in smoke. A courier must identify a road, sentry, or staff group before delivering a message. No later scene may place the entire French army in a fully visible, fully formed line before daylight merely because the map shows its nominal position.

The withdrawal has two separate consequences. For the coalition observer, an apparently weak centre makes the southern turning plan credible. For Napoleon's own army, the same posture requires discipline: the screen near the Goldbach villages must resist without revealing the mass waiting behind it, while Soult's divisions remain available to climb when the coalition has committed. The setting fixes this operational bargain but does not convert it into a scripted deception dialogue.

**Status:** Contested in exact clock readings; source-verified in broad weather sequence. Cold mist covered the low ground during the opening movement while higher ground brightened first. Segur places the decisive clearing before eight o'clock and describes the sun breaking through; Thiébault places the marshals together before eight and describes Napoleon waiting while the fog concealed the French; the official 30th Bulletin avoids the memoir dialogue and says that Napoleon ordered the attack at the first rays of the sun. These accounts support an early-morning interval, not a defensible minute.

The canon therefore uses the terms before eight, early morning, and after the first rays unless a dated order supplies another bound. It does not adopt an unsupported 07:30 or 08:30 clock anchor. The mist is not a magical curtain: village roofs, hedges, reverse slopes, smoke, and the curvature of the ground continue to hide local formations after sunrise. A commander on a height can see movement above a fold without reading the strength or identity of every column below.

Weather also affects bodies and equipment. Cold dampness stiffens clothing and reins; frozen or wet ground changes the movement of caissons and cavalry; smoke from black-powder volleys can restore concealment after the natural mist lifts. A later scenario may choose a precise moment inside the documented interval only as a clearly marked work decision, never as a fabricated quotation from a witness. Soult's reported answer is itself disputed: Segur gives direct speech, Thiébault indirect speech, and the official bulletin no exchange. The reliable fact is the sequence of waiting, observation, and attack.

**Status:** Source-verified in position and information limits. Zuran is a low rise northwest of the Pratzen ridge and served as an early French command position. Battlefield documentation places Napoleon near the rise and records the relation between his observation point, the ridge, and the roads toward the southern villages. From Zuran he could perceive the ridge and the large movement toward Telnitz and Sokolnitz; he could not see every battalion behind a village wall, on a reverse slope, or inside the mist.

Information reached him through Berthier's staff, aides-de-camp, mounted couriers, scouts, prisoners, local observation, and reports from corps commanders. Each channel had a different age and reliability. A messenger from Telnitz could report immediate pressure there but could not know whether the central coalition column had already descended from the Pratzen. A visible mass could be identified as movement without revealing its commander or effective strength. Napoleon's advantage was comparison: separate reports and visible changes agreed that the coalition was committing south and leaving the centre exposed.

The command post also has a physical workflow. A commander's decision can therefore precede a written order at one point while another unit still acts on an older instruction. This setting fixes the channels and their limits; the scenario layer must stage the order's relay and receipt rather than invent an instantaneous battlefield network.

**Status:** Source-verified. The Pratzen ridge runs roughly north-south for about 5 km and rises about 40 m above adjacent low ground. Staré Vinohrady is near 290 m absolute elevation; the Prace and Pratzen sector approaches 325 m. The ridge controls observation and lateral connection. The Goldbach stream and Bosenitz brook drain the southern low ground. Telnitz and Sokolnitz sit near the southern approach; Kobelnitz and Puntowitz lie farther north; the Satschan and Menitz ponds lie south near Augezd and Saint Anthony chapel. The Brno-Olomouc road passes the Santon on the French left.

These features impose different movement costs. A bridge or ford concentrates infantry and guns. A village street, garden wall, or chateau enclosure narrows a column and prevents a whole formation from deploying at once. A gun team needs a road wide enough for horses and caisson; infantry can cross a field but loses interval; wounded and prisoners compete with reserves for the same route. A formation on the ridge can see exposed movement below, while a unit in a hollow or behind a roofline can disappear from view. Sunrise increases visibility without creating an overhead view.

The features also have separate operational consequences. The Pratzen enables observation and threatens the coalition's central connection. The Goldbach and Bosenitz crossings delay the southern attack. Telnitz fixes the French right; Sokolnitz and its chateau consume time and frontage; the Santon anchors the French left; the ponds constrain the later retreat. The setting therefore rejects generic battlefield scenery: every named place has a route, sight-line, or choke function that a downstream writer can stage without re-researching the field.

**Status:** Source-verified in the named places and broad control sequence; contested in exact clocks, strength, and retrospective explanations. Sokolnitz is not one object. The village is a cluster of streets, walls, gardens, roofs, and lanes beside the Goldbach approaches. Its so-called Castle is an eighteenth-century manor house, not a medieval fortress. North of it lies the pheasantry, whose east wall looks up toward the Pratzen slopes; the granary is a separate strongpoint. Each can be held while another changes hands, and a report saying “Sokolnitz taken” cannot be read as proof that the manor, pheasantry, and every lane were simultaneously secure.

The southern approach is constrained by the Goldbach defile and by roads that compress columns before they can deploy. Stutterheim's Austrian account places Langeron's Second and Przybyszewski's Third Columns descending toward Sokolnitz while two French battalions from Legrand resist; French guns between Sokolnitz and Kobelnitz fire into the village, and the two Russian columns cross upon one another after entering. The account's sequence is useful for frontage, congestion, and artillery effect, but not as an omniscient clock: it is a participant's retrospective official narrative. The village street cannot hold a whole column abreast. Files bunch at gates and bridges; officers lose sight of the rear; caissons wait behind infantry; smoke and the low mist conceal whether a lane is empty, occupied, or merely quiet.

The manor's control sequence must remain separate and explicit. The Russian 7th Jaeger and Galicia Musketeers first take the house. The French 2nd Battalion of the 26th Légère retakes it in a counterattack. Russian troops then retake the manor, after which the French 33rd Ligne attacks from the northeast and recaptures it. Further Russian attacks fail, leaving the manor as a French bulwark. This northeast route belongs to the 33rd Ligne's manor action; it must not be merged with the later French counterattack that looks northeast from the village's west edge. The granary is a strongpoint during the fighting and later serves as a pen for Russian prisoners, so its control has a material afterlife even when the surrounding streets remain contested.

At about 10:15, the French 48th and 111th Ligne counterattack into and through the village. The battlefield view from the west edge looks northeast along their advance, across the village centre toward the Pratzen. “About 10:15” is a source-bounded label, not permission to invent a minute-by-minute timetable or dialogue. The units' movement must show what a counterattack can actually do: men leave a covered edge, cross a lane or opening under fire, drive defenders from a wall or house, and become vulnerable while reforming among buildings. A regiment that reaches the next street does not thereby own the manor or pheasantry. The pheasantry's east wall is held by the French 18th and 75th Ligne. Repaired sections are said to be embrasures for six French cannon; that “six” is a traditional, contested field interpretation, not an audited surviving gun inventory. Cannon there command approaches, but smoke, recoil, ammunition handling, damaged wheels, and the need to keep crews behind masonry limit continuous fire.

The physical fight accumulates costs. Muskets fired through apertures lose visibility as black-powder smoke settles between walls. Artillery can break a roofline or fill a lane with splinters without clearing every room. Wounded men, prisoners, replacement cartridges, stretcher parties, and runners all compete with attacking files for the same narrow passage. A surrendered group may be moved toward the granary while an unsearched garden still fires into the road. French and Russian officers receive local reports late and with uncertain nouns: “the castle,” “the village,” and “the pheasantry” may identify different positions to different senders. Later scenes must preserve those information limits rather than give either commander a map-like view of every control change.

The Goldbach is a tactical boundary, not a decorative stream. Its crossings and the approaches through Telnitz and Sokolnitz regulate when guns can come forward and when a retreat becomes a queue. A unit pushed out of a house may still possess the road behind it; a battery across the water may support a wall without seeing the men behind that wall. The scenario must therefore track frontage, ammunition, and viable passage separately from nominal possession. “French control” means a usable chain of positions and reports, not that every Russian soldier has vanished from the gardens, cellars, or lanes.

The source record carries national and temporal bias. Peterson's critical battlefield tour identifies the physical objects and photographed lines of advance, including the manor, granary, 48th/111th route, 33rd route, and pheasantry wall, but its captions are a modern field interpretation. Stutterheim, an Austrian participant writing the coalition's detailed account, emphasizes defiles, column entanglement, and failures of concert. Napoleon's 30th Bulletin is a French victory report: it fixes French command placements and the broad morning turn, yet magnifies certainty, enemy losses, and the cleanliness of the triumph. Ségur's later memoir supplies useful movement and sight-line testimony but is retrospective. The canon therefore preserves named formations, terrain mechanics, and control transitions while treating exact clocks, casualty totals, and claims of deliberate omniscient design as contested.

Przhebishevsky's own report adds a different limit: his Third Column crossed ploughed fields, needed pioneers to clear artillery routes, fought from roughly seven in the morning until four, received no information despite dispatches, and finally ran out of cartridges. It is a captured commander's self-defence, dated 1806, so its seven-hour endurance and claims of steadfastness are not neutral totals; nevertheless, its stonewall, marsh, canister, route, ammunition, and communication details make the Sokolnitz mechanics concrete. The 10:15 caption and the report's broad hours can coexist only as bounded, differently situated records, never as a fabricated synchronized clock.

**Status:** Source-verified for the written order, the named formations, and the existence of a staged arrival; contested for the exact road, clock meaning, distance-time folklore, and the size of each arriving increment. This setting owns the movement system that brings Davout's III Corps onto the southern French right. It does not turn “Davout arrived” into a single instant or place every unit listed in an order of battle at Telnitz or Sokolnitz.

The dispatch chain begins with an order, not with a battlefield sighting. Napoleon's 1 December orders first direct Davout to assemble his troops at the abbey of Raigern. The general dispositions written at the bivouac at 8:30 p.m. then specify that Davout, with Friant's division and General Bourcier's dragoon division, is to leave Raigern at 5:00 a.m. and gain the right of Soult; Soult is to dispose of Gudin's division when it arrives. The same document fixes a 7:30 meeting near Napoleon for further orders and gives Davout an escadron and a half of the 21st Dragoons to send to the bivouac. These are instructions and intended dependencies. They do not prove when Davout received them, when a courier found each brigade, or when the last caisson cleared the abbey.

The place names require a controlled translation. The order says Raigern; Stutterheim's coalition account says the convent of Reygern; a later French narrative writes Raygern, while modern references may identify Gross Raigern or Rajhrad. Here these names refer to the same operational staging area around the abbey, not to separate successive bivouacs. The route is bounded from the Vienna direction through Nikolsburg to that staging area, then east and northeast toward the French right and the lower Goldbach approaches. The order gives the destination relationally—“the right of Marshal Soult”—rather than a turn-by-turn road book. It therefore cannot support an invented map sequence through every hamlet, bridge, or lane between Reygern and Telnitz.

One later critical account supplies a complete distance-time pairing and must be kept intact as one source's claim: the order reached Friant on 29 November at 8:00 p.m.; the division marched at 11:00 p.m.; it covered 72 km to Nikolsburg in twenty-four hours, reached Raygern at 7:00 p.m. on 1 December, and left at 5:00 a.m. on 2 December. The same account calculates 112 km from Vienna in forty-four hours, with thirty-six hours of movement and eight hours of rest, and says only about 3,300 of Friant's men kept the pace while others followed in small groups. This is not the same claim as the familiar 110–140 km in 36–50 hours folklore. The canon adopts neither folklore combination: any scene using the 112/44/36/8 figures must label them as this account's reconstruction, while another source's 140 km or 48-hour figure cannot be silently paired with them.

The march is a sequence of road heads and lagging necessities. At night the leading infantry can keep a guide, but darkness separates files at turns and makes a wrong branch costly. Horses lose condition before the infantry's feet do; artillery teams and ammunition wagons need a passable road and cannot simply appear beside a deployed battalion. Men who fall out, repair shoes, seek water, or wait for a bridge are not evidence that the whole division has arrived. At Reygern, the formation can shorten its column, issue cartridges, and assign an advance; on the final approach, the road head can reach the Goldbach while guns, baggage, staff records, and stragglers remain behind. Under fire, a brigade may arrive in successive echelons, form behind a hedge or village, and counterattack before the remainder has closed up.

The named order of battle fixes identities but not arrival order. Nafziger's table lists III Corps under Davout with Friant's division: Kister's brigade contains the 15th Légère and 33rd; Lochet's contains the 48th and 111th; Heudelet's contains the 108th; the division has artillery, nine guns, and train. The same table lists Bourcier's dragoon division and its regiments. Those rows are a roster and strength reference. They do not place the 15th, 33rd, 108th, or an individual dragoon regiment at a named village without a narrative sequence. The setting therefore permits the order's “Friant and Bourcier” and the separately attested 48th and 111th action, while leaving each brigade's lead, artillery arrival, and cavalry commitment to a sourced downstream treatment.

The clocks describe different events. The written order fixes departure at 5:00. Sigler's critical study says Friant's leading elements began arriving at about 06:00, while its broader account describes Davout's buildup continuing toward 13:00. Stutterheim, an Austrian retrospective, places 4,000 men from Davout at the front near 09:00; the later French relation likewise says Davout came from Raigern with 4,000 at about nine. A Fondation Napoleon summary calls the III Corps “in action” at 08:30. These statements can coexist only if “arrival” means different things: road-head or leading elements, first action, a larger 4,000-man increment, or later buildup. None proves that the entire III Corps was formed at 06:00, 08:30, or 09:00.

The command consequence is local and asymmetric. Legrand can report that Telnitz or Sokolnitz is under pressure and ask for help; Davout can commit the men already in hand without knowing whether the next brigade will clear the road. Kienmayer and Dokhturov see resistance and fresh French pressure, but cannot infer the full strength, route, or exhaustion of the arriving corps. Napoleon receives reports, dispatches, and visible changes from separate sectors; he can recognize that the right has not collapsed while still lacking a reliable count of men actually behind each wall. A scenario must show couriers finding headquarters, orders being repeated, and formations closing under smoke and fire. The French official narrative celebrates a perfectly timed rescue; the Austrian account stresses the surprise and the coalition's numerical superiority; the later critical articles often elevate the march into a heroic “forced march.” The canon retains the order, route bounds, staged arrival, fatigue, and command uncertainty while treating exact synchronization and total-completion claims as contested.

**Status:** Source-verified in the 1 December written disposition and the existence of a morning decision at the French command post; contested in the exact oral words, clock, and boundary between readiness, order, receipt, and first movement. This H2 owns the decision-to-execution timing system for Soult's centre-right. It fixes Saint-Hilaire and Vandamme as the divisions prepared for the initial central operation while Legrand remains the southern holding division; the detailed assaults and their successive objectives belong to later H2s.

The contemporaneous order is a readiness condition, not a claim that the attack began at seven. Berthier's 1 December 8:30 p.m. disposition instructs Soult to place all three divisions beyond the ravine by 07:00, ready for a forward movement by echelon with the right wing leading, and requires Soult personally to report near Napoleon at 07:30. It also says that the divisions must remain in the indicated dispositions until further order. The written text therefore contains four distinct states: a division is positioned; it is ready; its commander reports; and a later command releases movement. A downstream scene may show staff officers checking intervals, roads, guns, and the ability of each division to step off, but it may not treat “ready at 07:00” as an attested assault time.

At Zuran, the command problem is one of observation and relay. Napoleon, Berthier, Soult, aides, and the marshals share a rise from which the broad southern movement can be seen, while folds, villages, smoke, and the remaining mist hide the strength and exact progress of individual columns. A report that the coalition has left the Pratzen heights describes an observed change, not an overhead map. It reaches the emperor through a grand guard, staff officer, aide, or marshal and carries an age, source, and uncertainty. The contemporaneous order establishes the staff chain—Berthier issues by Napoleon's order and Soult gives the divisional orders—but it does not preserve a verbatim written “attack now” message. The most defensible execution is therefore a command decision communicated orally or through short staff relays, followed by Soult's return to the flats and his own divisional transmission; the exact wording remains unknown.

The famous conversation has several layers of tradition. Sigler's critical study, drawing on Thiébault, reports that Napoleon asked how long Soult needed to reach the heights, that Soult answered no more than twenty minutes, and that Napoleon waited another quarter hour before saying to go. The same study says the morning mist and campfire smoke still concealed Saint-Hilaire and Vandamme when Soult returned. Ségur's memoir gives a related but not identical scene, while the French 30th Bulletin suppresses the exchange and says that the emperor waited for the horizon to clear, gave orders at the first rays, and that the signal was immediately followed by the opening cannonade. These are not interchangeable clocks or quotations. The canon treats “twenty minutes,” “another quarter hour,” “first rays,” and the later 08:00 or 08:30 summaries as source-labelled traditions, never as one synchronized transcript. No later prose may put an exact sentence into Napoleon's or Soult's mouth unless it marks the memoir source and its retrospective status.

The apparent clock conflict becomes executable when each timestamp is assigned a different event. 07:00 is planned divisional readiness; 07:30 is the scheduled marshal's report; “first rays” is the bulletin's visibility threshold; 08:00 is Sigler's critical reconstruction that the last of the first three coalition columns had cleared the Pratzeberg and begun descending; 08:30 is a later critical timeline's movement or action marker; and “go” is a release received and acted on by subordinate commanders, not necessarily the instant the first files moved. Stutterheim's separate observation records the combined army moving at 07:00, the fourth column being held until toward 08:00, and the third column only just leaving the Pratzen near 09:00. Saint-Hilaire and Vandamme must still form from their assigned positions, clear friendly intervals, and avoid masking one another's artillery. Legrand's division remains on the lower Goldbach as the southern screen while the two assault divisions become available. The order can be received at headquarters before a colonel has repeated it to every battalion, and a battalion can hear drums or a mounted aide before its rear companies see the signal.

Terrain and transmission make simultaneity impossible. A voice or drumbeat can travel beyond the visible part of a line, but distance, wind, ravines, folds, smoke, musketry, artillery, and overlapping formations can weaken, distort, delay, or mask its useful meaning. A unit may hear an indistinct beat or shout without knowing whether it came from its own commander, a neighboring formation, or the general battle; this setting does not assume that drums were in fact the attack signal unless a source says so. A courier must find a brigade commander, dismount, deliver the instruction, and return or be replaced. Smoke can hide a staff flag; a ravine can block a rider's route while sound echoes across it; artillery teams need clearance before infantry columns cross their front. Closed columns preserve shock but lengthen the interval between head and rear. Friendly troops on the lower ground can mask the approach of a division, while a rise exposes the leading files before the rest has closed. A scenario may therefore show an order received, a formation dressing its ranks, an aide reporting a gap, and only then a bounded step-off. It must not manufacture a written order whose survival the sources do not attest.

Napoleon's decision remains a risk, not an omniscient trap. If the coalition centre had stayed on the heights, Saint-Hilaire and Vandamme would have faced a stronger, visible position while Legrand's thin division continued to absorb pressure below; the scheduled echelon could have become a dangerous exposure. Stutterheim's coalition account records the seven-o'clock movement and the widening intervals as the columns approached the valleys, while the French bulletin retrospectively presents the first rays and the orders as a clean, decisive sequence. Ségur and Thiébault preserve vivid command recollections but write after the fact; Sigler synthesizes these testimonies into a useful tactical narrative, not a contemporaneous time sheet. The setting therefore retains the written intent, observation reports, relay mechanics, formation limits, and competing clock traditions, while refusing the myth that every French unit knew the plan at the same instant.

**Status:** Source-verified in the division's named command relationships, the Pratz–Pratzeberg geometry, and the broad first-contact sequence; contested in exact clocks, the later disposition of Varé's brigade, and memoir dialogue. This setting owns Saint-Hilaire's initial approach and the immediate fight around Pratz village and the Pratzeberg. Vandamme's independent axis toward Stare Vinohrady, the later struggle for that height, and the Imperial Guard's fighting belong to downstream settings owners.

The space is a saddle and two distinct objectives, not one generic hill. Pratz village lies deeply in a ravine whose branch of the Goldbach runs near Puntowitz; Pratzeberg is the higher crest beyond it. Sigler's critical reconstruction places Saint-Hilaire's approximately 6,800-man division on a three-kilometre front east of Puntowitz and Kobelnitz, with the division intended to clear the Pratzen Heights and serve as the army's pivot. The initial right-hand axis is therefore toward Pratz and the Pratzeberg, not across Vandamme's separate leftward route. A battalion entering the village is below the crest and does not yet hold the observation ground above it. A unit on the crest can see the ravine and the village roofs, but cannot read every company hidden behind the fold.

The nominal organization is clear, but the day's actual employment must not be flattened. Morand's advanced guard was the two-battalion 10e Léger, approximately 1,500 men in Sigler's reconstruction, under Colonel Pierre Pouzet. Thiébault's first brigade comprised the 14e and 36e Ligne, each represented by two battalions. Varé's nominal second brigade comprised the 43e and 55e Ligne. Sigler assigns that brigade to the division's left, with an objective at Stare Vinohrady and cooperation with Vandamme. That is a boundary and coordination assignment, not evidence that both regiments formed behind Thiébault in the Pratz assault. The order of battle may name Varé under Saint-Hilaire while the actual operation places the brigade on Vandamme's side or in a changing supporting role. Until a direct report resolves the timing and control, downstream writing must label Varé's relationship `Contested` and must not put the 43e or 55e into a Pratz scene merely because the nominal table lists them in the division.

Formation gives the approach its physical sequence. Sigler describes the 10e Léger deployed in line, angling southeast toward Pratzeberg, with Thiébault's brigade following up the slope in battalion attack columns. The critical study says a regiment's first battalion could lead in line while the second followed in closed column, preserving fire and shock but creating a visible interval between head and rear. The exact order was reportedly modified in practice, so the scenario may show the 10e opening frontage and the 14e/36e retaining depth without claiming a universal parade formation. Saint-Hilaire accompanies the advanced guard; his staff must keep contact with Morand while ensuring that Thiébault's rear does not overrun the leading line.

At approximately 08:30 in Sigler's reconstruction, Morand's 10e turns toward the Pratzeberg and Thiébault follows the rising ground. This timestamp describes the columns' movement, not the first shot and not the later crest fight. After crossing the 240-metre contour, Thiébault sends the left-hand 1/14, commanded by Colonel Jacques-François-Marc Mazas, toward Pratz. The battalion deploys into company lines without a forward skirmish screen and reaches the edge of the ravine. The ravine's branch stream separates the French companies from the village; its depth and banks break momentum, while the village hides the opposing battalion until it fires. A downstream scene can therefore stage the sudden volley, the broken leading battalion, and the two accompanying French guns left temporarily isolated, but must not assign a precise minute or invented shouted exchange.

The Russian response is also layered. Sigler's account says Major Carl Toll, riding ahead of the fourth column at about 08:00, first observed Saint-Hilaire's movement near Pratz and reported to Lieutenant-General Miloradovich. The troops immediately available were three weak battalions—two Novgorod Musketeer battalions and one Apsheron battalion—plus a few hussars and two guns, with one battalion retained, one deployed south of the village, and one along the ravine bank. The same critical account then describes an Apsheron reserve reinforced by three Little Russian Grenadier battalions making repeated attacks against the isolated guns. These details are a reconstruction of the local response, not permission to treat every Russian unit in the fourth column as already present at Pratz. Stutterheim's coalition account instead describes the centre's formation and the French masses appearing in the bottom near Pratzen; its near-09:00 observation belongs to a different point in the movement sequence.

Thiébault's counterattack follows a material problem, not a magic recovery. In the memoir-derived account, the 2/14 rallies while the 36e moves on the village; the battalion deploys as it runs, and Thiébault leads the immediate effort across the ravine. The 36e then forms against the Russian battalions south of Pratz, recovering the two guns and breaking the local attack. This is the edge of the setting's first phase: the French have restored a connection between the 10e and the 14e/36e, but they have not thereby completed the later central battle. Smoke, broken intervals, wounded officers, and the need to move guns by hand make the counterattack a ragged local re-formation rather than a clean reset.

Kutuzov's wound and words must remain a tradition map. Sigler reports that he attempted to rally the troops and was slightly wounded by a musket ball, while the story of a physician and a remark about where the army was truly hurt comes through memoir and later critical narration. Thiers's retrospective history describes a cheek wound and places the centre's encounter on pages 311–314, while Stutterheim reports the surprise of the French appearance without supplying the same dialogue. The French bulletin reduces this human uncertainty to the first-rays order and a victorious forward movement. The canon therefore permits a visible wound, confusion, and delayed command response only with source-labelled standing; it does not establish a verbatim Kutuzov quotation or an exact shooter.

Stare Vinohrady, also rendered Stari-Winobradi or “Old Vineyards,” is the high point on the northern end of the Pratzen position. Sigler places it at approximately 290 metres, while the Pratzenberg rises higher to the southeast. The height is not interchangeable with Pratz village: a force can pass the village and still be below the crest, or hold the crest while its rear remains exposed toward the ravine. From Stare Vinohrady a commander can observe the lower Goldbach approaches and the French left, but a fold in the slope hides files and guns. Vandamme commands Soult's second division. Nafziger's order of battle lists its brigades and regiments as the 24e Légère with the 4e Ligne, the 28e and 46e Ligne, and the 57e Ligne, with division artillery and train. A downstream scene must preserve the division's internal intervals, artillery road, and contact flank instead of turning the order of battle into a static painted line.

The first approach is shaped by visibility and interval. Sigler's critical reconstruction says Vandamme was massed in front of Jirzikowsitz while Saint-Hilaire formed farther south and east. After the release, Vandamme advances on the left of Saint-Hilaire, with the height and the connection to Bernadotte governing its direction. Stutterheim's coalition account sees a massive French column in the bottom in front of Pratzen, then describes French masses advancing slowly, opening by degrees, and using inequalities of ground to cover movement. The two accounts answer different questions: Sigler reconstructs the French formation and axis; Stutterheim records what the coalition could see while its centre was still organizing. The opposing position is layered. Stutterheim places the fourth coalition column under Kollowrath and Miloradovich near the Pratzen sector, with Russian battalions and Austrian brigades attempting to hold the height as the French appeared. These accounts establish a physical problem: coalition units moving south must reverse direction, climb, and form while French infantry approaches from below. A unit that hears firing behind it may face about before its artillery or neighbouring brigade can align. The high point is therefore defended by fragments arriving through separate command channels, not by a perfectly synchronized counter-line.

The French formation must also pay for the climb. Infantry in closed columns can preserve momentum on a slope but presents a dense target and needs room to deploy at the crest. Guns that support the advance must negotiate the same approaches as caissons and limbers; if the lead battalions reach the high ground first, their artillery may still be below or masked by friendly files. The crest exposes the head of the division to musketry and counterattack, while reverse folds protect the next battalions from direct view. A scenario may show officers dressing intervals, artillery crews seeking a line of fire, and runners trying to maintain contact with Bernadotte, but it must not supply an unsupported ammunition total or pretend that a map symbol equals a deployed battery.

Peterson's battlefield record distinguishes the view toward Pratzen village from the view across the crest and records that Saint-Hilaire later received the coalition counterattack on the height. That modern field caption is useful for orientation but is not a contemporaneous report. The French 30th Bulletin compresses the movement into an official victory sequence in which Soult's attack cuts the enemy's right and the rest follows; Thiers's later French history gives Vandamme a determined role in turning the height and names individual regiments, but its patriotic narrative and hindsight demand qualification. Ségur and Thiébault provide vivid recollections of formation and danger, while Stutterheim presents the coalition's surprise and attempts to recover the key ground. The canon retains the height's geometry, Vandamme's left axis, the contact requirement, and the cost of deploying under fire; it leaves exact clock readings, complete brigade placement, and the later Guard battle to their own owners.

**Status:** Source-verified for the French hook's broad geometry, the arrival of Kamensky's brigade and Austrian troops, the use of reserve artillery, and a later French advance; contested in exact clocks, spoken words, casualty totals, and the reliability of memoir descriptions of individual gestures. This setting owns the fight that turns Saint-Hilaire's initially exposed advance into a bent defensive line on the height. It does not own the later Imperial Guard cavalry action or the southern encirclement.

The French hold on the Pratzen is a problem of frontage, not a triumphant flag planted on an empty summit. After the 10e Léger reaches the Pratzeberg and the 1/14 rallies behind it, Thiébault's remaining battalions must connect the village side to the crest while watching troops approaching from the east and rear. Sigler's reconstruction calls the resulting shape a *crochet*, or hook: a line extending from slightly west of the crest eastward, then bending north toward Pratzen village and anchoring on the church. The line's two arms answer different threats. The southern and western face meets Kamensky's Russian brigade; the northern arm and its bent flank face the Austrian brigades that have emerged from the coalition movement. The hook is therefore a temporary geometry produced by danger, not a pre-existing parade formation.

Kamensky's arrival is governed by congestion. Sigler says an Austrian cavalry formation found itself in the wrong position and cut through the second coalition column to return, delaying Kamensky while his brigade was descending toward the lower Goldbach. When Kamensky saw French infantry on the Pratzeberg, he turned his men about and marched back uphill. This is not an instantaneous reversal: officers must transmit the change down a column, files must clear cavalry and guns, and the rear battalions must close before they can support the leading one. A scene may show the head of the brigade appearing first, its rear still caught in the traffic below, and French observers mistaking a partial arrival for the whole formation. The source supports approximately 4,000 men in Kamensky's brigade, but not a precise distribution at every contour line.

Kamensky's first pressure exposes the French interval. He sends a battalion toward the French left, nearly outflanking the 10e. Saint-Hilaire brings the rallied 1/14 at a run from the rear and places it in line on the right of the 10e. Thiébault brings the 1/36, 2/36, and 1/14 still under his direct control toward the left. These movements cannot be portrayed as a clean exchange of diagrams. The battalions have just crossed a ravine, suffered a volley, recovered guns, and moved uphill; officers are finding men by color, drum, voice, and visible standards, while smoke makes neighbouring uniforms hard to identify. A battalion reaching the line may be short of its original officers and may dress on a surviving flank rather than on a surveyed alignment.

The second danger comes from the east. Thiébault's memoir-derived account describes troops approaching the Pratzeberg from the east and rear, one of them calling that they were Bavarians. Saint-Hilaire, Thiébault, and Morand use spyglasses and an officer's contact with Kamensky's troops to identify the newcomers as Austrian brigades under Jurczek and Rottermund, within Kollowrath's column. The story is valuable as a recognition problem: uniforms, distance, and smoke make nationality uncertain at the exact moment a commander must decide whether to fire. It is not a license to reproduce a verbatim call as established fact. The canon labels the alleged call and the conversation memoir testimony, while retaining the physical consequence—French troops must refuse a flank until the approaching formation is identified.

Thiébault's solution assigns tasks by angle. The 1/36 pivots on the left of the 10e; the 2/36 forms toward the new eastern arrivals; the 2/14 remains in attack-column formation as a compact mass that can meet cavalry or a surrounding corps without disrupting the line. The resulting hook is thin. Sigler estimates roughly 800 metres along the first arm and another 800 down toward Pratzen, with battalion frontage and necessary intervals consuming much of the available ground. The exact numbers are critical reconstruction, not a survey of every file. What is executable is the relationship: the French line faces more than one direction, preserves a reserve-like column on the exposed flank, and cannot freely push forward without opening a seam.

Artillery changes the hook from a line of infantry into a temporary battery position. Sigler records half of Saint-Hilaire's divisional artillery with Morand and the other half with Thiébault: two eight-pounders and a howitzer for the advanced guard, two four-pounders and a howitzer for the brigade. The two four-pounders were the pieces isolated near Pratz and later recovered. Chef de bataillon Fontenay then brings six twelve-pound guns from the corps reserve. Thiébault places three-gun sections on each flank of the 36e and masks them with infantry, loading round shot and grapeshot for close range. Fontenay's objection that such work could damage the pieces comes from memoir tradition; the setting may show the crews facing a short, dangerous engagement, but it must not convert the claimed thirty- or forty-toise range into a universally measured distance or invent a number of rounds.

The Austrian assault is materially mismatched yet dangerous. Sigler gives sixteen Austrian battalions, more than 8,000 men, approaching the three French battalions and guns, approximately 2,600 men. He also characterizes many Austrian battalions as depot formations or inexperienced recruits. That assessment is a French-leaning critical synthesis and must be balanced against Stutterheim's coalition account, which emphasizes the French generals' skill in using folds of ground and concealed movement. Neither source means that inexperienced troops were incapable of pressure. A mass moving uphill can still close a gap, overwhelm a gun crew, or force a thin line to fire before it has dressed its ranks. The scene should show the Austrians' approach narrowing through terrain, French muskets held until a useful range, and artillery crews waiting for the infantry mask to withdraw.

The hook survives through movement, not immobility. When the Austrian attack falters and Kamensky's advance slows, Thiébault turns his troops south to align with Morand and then advances down the Pratzeberg, driving Kamensky downhill and taking guns and flags according to the memoir-derived account. The setting records the direction and tactical consequence while treating the captured-gun and flag counts as source claims requiring a label. Kutuzov, Weyrother, Kollowrath, and Miloradovich do not share one view of the line. Sigler says senior coalition commanders rally another attack; Stutterheim records the French use of ground and the coalition's difficulty coordinating the columns. A downstream manuscript must give each headquarters only the reports and visual fragments available from its location.

The later counterstroke exposes the cost of victory. Saint-Hilaire, Thiébault, and Morand reportedly consider withdrawing to a more defensible position when the next attack pushes the French toward the crest. Pouzet's remembered refusal and the subsequent half-hour of fighting are memoir material, not a clock-certified transcript. The stable canon is that the French line was pressed, senior officers and horses were exposed, and the division's continuation depended on cohesion at close range. Saint-Hilaire, Mazas, and de Lamotte are reported wounded or killed at different points in the surviving narratives; the exact sequence and casualty attribution must remain source-labelled. A messenger from Berthier's headquarters eventually checks the division's state, but that visit does not make the division omnisciently supported. The French hold is a local success achieved while higher command contact remains intermittent.

**Status:** Source-verified for the Russian Guard's reserve role, its movement toward Blasowitz, the early engagement with French light troops and infantry, and a later cavalry contest; contested in exact charge timing, formations, casualty totals, and the polished heroism of retrospective descriptions. This setting owns the Guard's intervention on the French northern-centre flank as a distinct operation. It does not merge that fight with the Pratzen infantry hook or with the later southern retreat through the frozen ponds.

The Guard begins as a reserve on paper and becomes a first-line formation through the movement of other columns. Stutterheim says the Grand Duke Constantine's corps of Guards was intended to form the reserve of the allied right and left the heights in front of Austerlitz for Blasowitz and Krug. The intended role presumes time and space to observe the main battle. In practice, the Guard reaches ground already touched by Rivaud's skirmishers and Murat's light cavalry. A reserve that arrives under fire must choose between forming in depth, occupying a village, and attacking the troops who have already found its flank. The setting treats this transition as a command problem rather than as a preplanned decisive charge.

Blasowitz is a positional hinge. It lies north of the main Pratzen axis and near the routes linking Bernadotte, Murat, and Lannes. A battalion of Guard light infantry occupies the village in Stutterheim's account, while the Guard's larger formations try to establish themselves on the heights. The village offers walls, lanes, and a recognizable rally point but also restricts a cavalry deployment. The heights offer observation and room to dress ranks, yet their crest exposes men to French skirmish fire and guns. A scenario must distinguish the battalion in the village from the Guard cavalry beyond it, and both from the infantry formations moving on the causeway.

Prince John of Liechtenstein's cavalry complicates the reserve's deployment. The original disposition placed his cavalry on Bagration's left to command the plain before Schlapanitz. Stutterheim says the column had been posted behind the third coalition column and was impeded by infantry crossing its route as they descended from the heights. Liechtenstein sends ten squadrons under Uwarov to Bagration's left, where the Elisabethgrad Hussars form; Constantine's Hulans become the head of the remaining cavalry column. The names and reported detachment are source-specific. They establish traffic, separation, and a partial arrival, not a simultaneous mass of every squadron at Blasowitz.

The first Russian cavalry charge is a failure of interval as much as courage. Stutterheim says the Hulans under General Essen deploy before the rest of the line is ready and rush the French light cavalry without support. The French horsemen retire through infantry intervals, and the pursuing Hulans enter the battalions' fire. Caffarelli's division forms on one side and Rivaud's on the other, placing the Russian cavalry between fires. The account reports more than 400 losses and Essen's severe wounding and later death; these figures and sequence belong to a coalition retrospective and must be presented as such. The material mechanics are stable: cavalry that follows a retiring screen into a formed infantry line loses room to turn, meets musketry from multiple directions, and cannot count on the second line if its own formation has not closed.

The French intervals matter. Murat's light cavalry cannot be portrayed as an isolated cloud that routs the Guard by itself. Stutterheim places French infantry under Bernadotte and Lannes in support, with cavalry retreating through gaps and formed troops receiving the pursuing Hulans. The gaps must be wide enough for a squadron to pass but narrow enough that horses emerging from them meet a fresh line. Smoke, standards, and the noise of several regiments make it possible for a Russian officer to know that French troops are in front without knowing which formation waits beyond the first interval. A unit may pause to dress or charge on incomplete information; the story must not grant a bird's-eye view of the entire northern field.

The Guard infantry then faces the French advance in a separate exchange. Stutterheim describes Constantine leaving the heights, advancing in line, and ordering a bayonet charge that drives back French skirmishers. A sharp cannonade follows; when the Grand Duke approaches French infantry that has deployed, Bessières's Guard cavalry charges from intervals and drives the unsupported Russian line back. The account's phrase that the Russian infantry was “without support” is an interpretation of the moment, not proof that no neighbouring troops were within hearing. The executable state is that the Russian line has advanced beyond the protection of its original crest, French artillery has punished the approach, and cavalry emerges at the instant the Russian commander expects to meet infantry.

The Russian Horse Guards' counter-charge restores local space. Stutterheim reports the regiment attacking the French flank, checking and routing French cavalry, then striking French infantry that has moved up to support it. The regiment is credited with capturing a French eagle. This is a named coalition claim, not a neutral inventory; the canon permits a captured standard only with attribution and does not attach an unsupported battalion or bearer. The Guard corps then retires, rallies on the heights it had originally left, and moves toward Austerlitz by Krzenowitz. The later encounter with French horse grenadiers under Rapp is similarly bounded: the French cavalry arrives as reinforcement, the Horse Guards and Guard hussars attack, and the Russian Guard withdraws without being continuously pursued. The fight has local reversals rather than one clean national victory.

Command information is asymmetric. Constantine is physically near his Guard and can see the village and immediate cavalry; Liechtenstein is managing a cavalry column whose route is blocked; Bagration is trying to maintain his own left and cannot absorb every broken squadron. Murat and Bessières can exploit intervals but receive no instantaneous report of every Russian reserve movement. The French official bulletin presents the centre and northern action as part of an ordered victory, while Stutterheim's account defends or explains coalition deployments through obstacles, impetuosity, and delayed support. Later French narratives may celebrate the Guard charge as proof of tactical superiority; Russian and coalition traditions emphasize the courage of the Horse Guards and the cost of attacking without support. The canon keeps the crossing routes, villages, intervals, and rally points visible while refusing to rank every participant's moral interpretation as fact.

The Guard's battlefield state must also include horses and ammunition. A squadron that has charged through infantry intervals must reform before it can charge again; wounded horses block lanes, loose animals alarm formed ranks, and officers must find surviving trumpeters or standards. Artillery limbers cannot follow a cavalry thrust through a village street, so a battery supporting the next phase may remain on the height while the cavalry fights below. Infantry that has advanced by bayonet may have fired only a few volleys but still be exhausted from climbing and dressing under shot. A scenario can therefore show the Guard rallying, French cavalry reforming, and both sides claiming a momentary advantage without manufacturing a synchronized “second charge.”

It does not rewrite the Guard's distinct intervention at Blasowitz or the southern collapse.

The northern field is a containment system of roads, villages, and open ground. Stutterheim places Bernadotte's corps across the Girschikowitz route, Murat's cavalry between Girschikowitz and Krug, and Lannes's corps to the left of Murat along the causeway. The names can appear in variant spellings—Girzikowitz/Jirzikovice, Blasowitz/Blazovice, Krug, Holubitz, Posorsitz, and Kovalowitz—but the setting treats them as linked positions, not interchangeable labels. A unit moving from the Pratzen sector toward the northern road must cross or skirt the same spaces in which cavalry seeks room to deploy. A village that is “occupied” may contain only light infantry or a detachment, while a nearby height carries the actual battery and observation post.

Prince Bagration's task is not simply to attack Lannes. He advances from Posorsitz and attempts to occupy the heights of Dwaroschna while keeping his left connected to Uwarov's cavalry. Stutterheim says Bagration places three battalions of light infantry under General Ulanius in Krug and Holubitz. The source is a coalition retrospective and uses the villages as operational anchors; it does not tell us that every house was searched or that the three battalions occupied both settlements in equal strength. The canon preserves a thin infantry screen, local strongpoints, and a route by which Bagration can observe or delay the French advance.

Lannes's arrival closes the interval. Stutterheim says Lannes moves in column on Bagration's left and on the right of Uwarov's cavalry, stopping the coalition right's advance. The French corps may therefore be shown as a deep formation whose head reaches the contested ground before the rear has fully dressed. Caffarelli and Suchet are named on Lannes's right and left in the coalition account, but the exact battalion sequence remains outside the source's resolution. The 27e Ligne protects a commanding height between Lesch and Kovalowitz, to the left of the Brunn causeway, with eighteen guns. This battery is a defensive reserve and retreat safeguard, not evidence that all French artillery was concentrated there. The number eighteen belongs to Stutterheim's report and should be attributed when used.

The battery's position creates a physical threat to Bagration's left. Heavy cannon fire forces the coalition commander to reinforce that side and send most of his cavalry to Uwarov, according to Stutterheim. A battery on a height can see a road and village approaches but cannot fire through its own infantry or over every fold. Limbers and ammunition wagons remain behind the gun line; if the position changes, crews must limber under threat and find a road not blocked by retiring infantry. A scenario must show the battery's crews choosing lanes, the 27e protecting the pieces, and the French infantry using the guns as an anchor rather than treating the height as a magical wall.

Uwarov's cavalry becomes a mobile screen for a threatened flank. Stutterheim gives him approximately thirty squadrons after Bagration commits much of his cavalry to the left. Ulanius's troops are driven from Krug and Holubitz while the French columns continue their advance, protected in part by Murat's cavalry. The exact composition and strength are source claims that should not be silently harmonized with a modern order of battle. What the geography requires is a sequence of cavalry moving through or around villages, seeking a flank, and using charges to slow an infantry column whose own artillery and skirmishers are not yet fully deployed.

The cavalry exchanges are not a single “charge.” Stutterheim describes several charges by Russian and French horse. Ulanius succeeds in checking the French progress, while Bagration holds at Posorsitz for a long period before withdrawing toward Rausnitz as the Russian Guard leaves the heights behind Blasowitz. Each exchange has a different tactical state: a screening squadron can retire through infantry, a formed cavalry line can strike exposed flanks, and a tired horse regiment may be held back to cover a road. The canon leaves the order of individual squadrons and the exact minutes contested; it requires scenes to track whether horses are formed, scattered, or reforming behind a village.

Command is divided by distance. Murat can direct cavalry near the causeway but cannot see the Pratzen hook; Lannes can coordinate Caffarelli, Suchet, and the battery only through staff officers and visible signals; Bernadotte's left is near the Guard and must maintain its own alignment. Bagration receives reports from Ulanius and Uwarov, but the villages and cannon smoke conceal whether a French column has halted or merely dipped behind a fold. A retreating battalion can carry a rumor that the height is lost long before a staff officer verifies it. The scenario must use messengers, standards, riders, and delayed reports rather than granting either marshal a continuous overhead view.

The northern wing's success is measured by what it prevents. Bagration's forces do not need to be annihilated for the French centre to gain time; they need to be contained so that a coherent mass cannot wheel south onto the Pratzen. Conversely, a French advance that takes Krug or Holubitz but leaves its flank open to Uwarov has not secured the road. The source narratives disagree about emphasis: Stutterheim presents the northern combat as a sequence of cavalry checks, village occupations, and Bagration's disciplined retreat; the French bulletin compresses the action into the army's general success and gives French formations a cleaner coordination than smoke and distance permit; later histories often celebrate Lannes and Murat while treating Bagration's delaying work as a footnote. The canon retains both functions—French pressure and coalition containment—without turning the northern fight into a mere backdrop.

By evening, Stutterheim places Bagration behind Austerlitz and Uwarov protecting the retreat, while the high road to Wischau is left open and baggage is subsequently captured. Those are end-state claims from a coalition account, not an exact inventory of every wagon or a promise that no French patrol reached the road earlier. The setting may use the open road, halted batteries, and exhausted cavalry as the material bridge to the aftermath, but it must reserve the detailed baggage seizure and night movement for the later retreat settings. The battle's northern logic ends when Bagration can no longer threaten the French centre and Lannes can hold the road without exposing the army's left.

**Status:** Source-verified for the French turn from Pratzen toward Augezd, the coalition retreat through the southern villages and between the lakes, the role of Dochturov, Kienmayer, and covering cavalry, and the material danger of the dike; contested in the number of men lost on the ice, the exact condition of the lakes, the sequence of every surrender, and later victory-myth explanations. This setting owns the immediate southern end-state of the battle. The armistice, treaty, and political aftermath belong to later settings.

The southern collapse begins as a change of facing. Stutterheim describes French troops on the right wing turning their backs on Austerlitz to attack the remains of the allied left. The French who had taken the heights now leave the morning axis and move toward the chapel above Augezd and the villages around the lakes. This does not mean that every French regiment wheels at once. Vandamme's division, described as the extreme right on the Pratzen heights, moves gradually toward the chapel as the crest is secured; artillery and rear battalions follow through roads still occupied by wounded, prisoners, and abandoned equipment. A unit that faces south may expose its original rear to Pratzen until neighbouring formations close the gap.

Augezd is an approach node, not the lake itself. Stutterheim says Buxhoevden's troops pass through the village amid confusion and that prisoners are taken in or around it, while other fugitives reach the lake. The setting must distinguish the village streets, the chapel height, the water's edge, and the narrow dike between lakes. A report that “Augezd fell” therefore cannot be read as proof that the entire left wing is trapped. Some battalions can pass the village, some halt to reform, and others turn toward the water because the road behind them is blocked. French artillery arriving later changes the calculation; before the guns clear the routes, infantry and cavalry may contest the same exits.

The lake story requires source discipline. Stutterheim says the lake was frozen but not sufficiently so to prevent many fugitives from perishing in it. The account is a coalition retrospective written to explain the physical disaster and cannot supply an audited count, temperature, or universal condition of the ice. Napoleon's official bulletins and later French retellings turn the frozen ponds into a vivid symbol of annihilation, while modern battlefield interpretation must distinguish verified terrain from dramatized numbers. The canon permits ice cracking under clustered men, horses, guns, and carts, and permits drowning or exposure where a source attests it; it forbids an invented casualty total or a cinematic command to march onto the lake as though the danger were known in advance.

Dochturov's problem is route width. Stutterheim says the first column's centre and rear collect between Tellnitz and the lake, but the retreat can be made only across a narrow dike that admits no more than two men abreast. A narrow crossing transforms a large formation into a queue. Front ranks cannot accelerate without leaving the rear exposed; artillery limbers and caissons block infantry; wounded men and prisoners compete for the same passage. Officers may try to restore order in the open plain, but the visible column behind them can look larger or smaller depending on fog, smoke, and folds. A scenario should track the dike as a capacity constraint with a head, middle, and tail, not as a single line that everyone crosses simultaneously.

Kienmayer's hussars secure the route before the French can cut it. Stutterheim says Kienmayer is sent forward with the Hesse-Homburg Hussars to observe the point between Satschan and Ottnitz. The assignment is protective and informational: cavalry watches the road and reports whether French troops are approaching around the lake; it does not guarantee that the dike is clear or that the infantry can move at the same speed. Austrian cavalry under O'Reilly and the Szeckler Hussars, with Cossacks in the broader covering screen, support Dochturov's retreat in the plain between Augezd and Sokolnitz. Their presence buys time through threat and charge, but horses cannot hold an exposed hill indefinitely against grape-shot.

The French response develops in stages. After taking Augezd, French artillery reaches the lake edge and fires on Austrian cavalry protecting the retreat. Stutterheim reports the French moving two dragoon regiments toward the height, then turning by the left when they see the Russian infantry supported; the dragoons post at the head of Vandamme's division. This is a useful caution against a simple pursuit: cavalry may refuse an attack when infantry support is still intact, then resume pressure after artillery and fresh formations arrive. The setting permits French guns to sweep a flank and force a cavalry displacement, but the exact battery identity, range, and number of discharges remain source-dependent.

The retreating infantry is exhausted and uneven. Stutterheim says Dochturov briefly restores order and then concentrates on getting away; later the column forms a body of at least 8,000 men near Neuhoff, with darkness approaching. These are broad coalition estimates. They do not prove that every man belongs to the first column or that the formation is combat-ready. The canon requires fatigue, wet roads, abandoned guns, missing officers, and men who have lost contact with their regiments. Rear-guard cavalry can be brave and effective while still failing to prevent prisoners or artillery from being captured. A battalion behind a ditch at Tellnitz may delay pursuit for the time required for the column's head to clear, even if the battalion itself is later forced to retreat.

The physical end of the battle is therefore not a single massacre but a sequence of changing anchors: Pratzen crest, chapel height, Augezd streets, lake edge, dike, and the heights beyond Neuhoff. Each anchor buys or consumes time. French infantry and artillery must move over roads damaged by vehicles; coalition cavalry must choose between charging and preserving the retreat; Russian officers must decide whether to rally a battalion or send it through the choke point. Source bias is visible in the conclusion. Stutterheim openly warns that minute details are difficult to obtain and that two observers rarely see the same object alike; his account nevertheless defends coalition conduct and proposes alternative movements. The French bulletin presents the collapse as the inevitable consequence of the Pratzen decision. Later memoirs heighten the ice and the drama of the surrender. The canon preserves the dike, the lakes, the villages, the cavalry screen, and the late artillery while marking exact losses, ice conditions, and omniscient causal claims as contested.

This H2 ends at the operational battlefield boundary. It does not narrate the negotiated armistice, the diplomatic exchange, or the Treaty of Pressburg. Those events require different sources, actors, and political stakes. It does establish the material state those later settings inherit: French formations hold the heights and approaches, coalition units have crossed or are attempting to cross the southern exits, guns and prisoners are separated from their original commands, and reports arriving after dark describe a victory whose human and material cost is still being counted.

**Status:** Source-verified for the need to separate killed, wounded, prisoners, missing, abandoned guns, and stragglers; contested in all grand totals and in the rhetorical cleanliness of French and coalition summaries. This setting owns the first accounting state after the firing recedes, when commanders are still receiving fragmentary returns. It does not own the armistice, the diplomatic settlement, or the later public memory of the battle.

The first report is not the final ledger. A regiment can report that it has lost a gun while the piece is being dragged back by a neighboring battery; a battalion can list men as missing when they are wounded in a village or held as prisoners near a granary; a brigade can count present files before stragglers rejoin. The battlefield's physical compartments—houses, ravines, dikes, woods, roads, and artillery parks—delay verification. The canon therefore keeps five states separate: killed, wounded, captured, missing or separated, and temporarily unaccounted for. “Losses” without a category is not an executable fact.

Przhebishevsky's report demonstrates the problem from the coalition side. It describes the Third Column's movement, artillery routes, stone walls, dispatches that did not arrive in time, and ammunition depletion, then presents the commander's conduct under pressure. The report is a Russian commander's self-defence written after capture, and its broad hours and claims of endurance serve an argument. Yet its practical details reveal what a headquarters could know: a battery can be lost before its dispatch reaches the column commander; cartridges can be exhausted while a neighboring formation still believes support exists; a road can be blocked by wounded and vehicles. Later scenes may use these constraints but must identify the report's national and retrospective position.

Stutterheim's concluding account explicitly warns that minute particulars are difficult to obtain and that observers rarely see the same object from the same point. His refusal to calculate a universal casualty total is itself evidence about the reporting problem. The account nevertheless supplies local numbers—such as reported prisoners, wounded, or battalions in a retreating body—within its narrative. Those numbers must retain their locator and status. They cannot be added across paragraphs as though all used identical definitions, nor can a French official bulletin's enemy losses be subtracted from a coalition return to produce a spurious balance.

The French bulletin has a different purpose. It announces a victory to the emperor's government and public, names French formations, and presents the Pratzen movement and southern collapse as a coherent sequence. Its authority is strongest for what the French command wanted officially recorded—orders, commanders, the broad result, and the vocabulary of triumph—not for neutral enemy totals or the private uncertainty of a brigade officer. A scene set at French headquarters may show aides compiling captured colors, guns, and prisoners from separate reports, but it must not make Napoleon know the final count while firing continues. The bulletin's polished certainty is a later layer over reports that were incomplete when first delivered.

Prisoners are material work, not an abstract number. Captured men must be disarmed, searched for officers and papers, directed away from active lanes, and guarded by troops who might otherwise reinforce the line. The Sokolnitz granary can hold prisoners in the local sequence, while a village street remains dangerous because a house or garden has not been searched. Officers separated from their regiments may be questioned before an interpreter is available. French and Russian terms for a castle, village, column, or battery may refer to different objects. The canon permits a report to state that prisoners were taken at a named place only when its source does so; it does not invent a mass surrender dialogue or a precise queue length.

Wounded men create an equally visible but ambiguous trail. A wounded general may remain near his formation long enough to influence a final rally, while wounded files are carried toward a church, barn, or improvised dressing point. Horses shot under officers, broken limbers, and abandoned muskets show where a formation passed but cannot by themselves establish when it withdrew. Medical orderlies need safe routes and water; an artillery team may stop firing to move a stretcher across a gun position. A screenplay-level scenario should preserve the conflict between evacuation and pursuit: a unit can be victorious on the crest while its wounded still occupy the lane behind it.

The French army also has to reconcile identity and presence. Nafziger's order of battle fixes regiments and paper artillery, but a return after combat must distinguish the men who began the day, those who joined late, attached batteries, and formations that fought in a neighboring sector. The 48th and 111th Ligne are independently attested in the Sokolnitz counterattack; their presence there must not be silently used to place all of Friant's division in the same village. Similarly, Varé's nominal Saint-Hilaire assignment cannot be converted into a Pratzen casualty total when the brigade's actual employment remains contested. The evidence graph later needs lineage from each return to the setting that establishes the unit's actual action.

Commanders receive a changing picture. Napoleon can infer that the centre has broken through from visible possession of the heights and from reports from Soult, but he still needs separate returns for the northern cavalry and Davout's right. Soult may know that the Pratzen crest is held while lacking the state of every southern village. Davout can report that the coalition left is retreating while his own brigades count ammunition and prisoners. On the coalition side, Kutuzov's wound and movement between commands limit his direct observation; Buxhoevden, Dochturov, Kienmayer, and the column commanders issue local orders that may not reach the emperors before the retreat has changed direction. A later memoir that assigns one mind a complete view is treated as retrospective compression.

The first-night accounting has four documentary layers. There are immediate oral reports from aides and regimental officers; written returns compiled when roads and headquarters permit; official bulletins shaped for political communication; and later memoirs that impose a dramatic arc. Each layer can preserve a different truth. An oral report may accurately say “the guns are gone” while misnaming the battery; a written return may correct the unit but omit men still separated; a bulletin may state the result without the uncertainty; a memoir may remember the smoke and fear but relocate the clock. Later settings must cite the layer used for each claim rather than treating “the French account” or “the Russian account” as a single voice.

This accounting state closes the battle setting without declaring the cost settled. The victory peak is operationally visible—French control of the decisive heights, coalition retreat, captured material, and disconnected columns—but the human ledger remains provisional. That distinction is essential for the novel's opening: the young emperor's triumphant battlefield image can be vivid and immediate, while the narrative still allows wounded men, prisoners, missing officers, and contradictory reports to occupy the same historical moment. The later return to Napoleon's childhood inherits a victory remembered as complete and a record that remains materially unfinished.
