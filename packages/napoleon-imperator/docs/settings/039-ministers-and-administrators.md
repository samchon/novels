# Ministers and Administrators

This file owns the civil offices that translate imperial decisions into law, money, policing, and daily administration.

**Status:** Work canon. A ministry has a legal remit, budget, staff, archives, and rivals; it is not identical with the emperor's intention.

Orders cross ministries, prefectures, courts, police, army staffs, and households. Settings must identify which office can decide, which can execute, and which can delay or report. A decree may be clear in Paris and ambiguous at a frontier.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Prefects connect central policy to municipalities, taxation, conscription, policing, roads, and public information.

A prefect cannot make a decree true without clerks, registers, guards, money, and local cooperation. Reports travel upward through incentives and fear. Later scenes must distinguish the central instruction, the prefect's interpretation, the mayor's action, and the population's response.

**Status:** Work canon. Security is produced through informers, files, patrols, censorship, arrests, prisons, and judicial or extrajudicial authority.

An intelligence report is not an event and an arrest is not proof of guilt. Settings must identify the source, chain of custody, legal authority, physical site, and consequence of a report. Police may know a rumor without knowing its truth, while fear changes what citizens say publicly.

**Status:** Source-critical. Regime change can preserve offices and procedures while changing whose rights, property, and speech the system protects.

Courts require files, judges, clerks, witnesses, police, and time. A legal code is implemented unevenly, and an imperial command may conflict with local custom or judicial procedure. Later documents must identify the jurisdiction, text, date, and enforcement path before calling an act legal or illegal.

**Status:** Work canon. Files accumulate, wait, move, and lose context; administrative power is limited by time and attention.

A minister receives summaries rather than every fact. A missing enclosure, untranslated letter, or delayed register can change policy. Settings must show who prepares a brief, what evidence is omitted, who signs, and how a subordinate learns of the decision.
