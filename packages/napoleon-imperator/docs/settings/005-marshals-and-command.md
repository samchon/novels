# Marshals and Command Relations

This file gives figure-level units for the marshals and senior commanders central to the 1805 campaign and the work's broader military cast, and relation-level units for the bonds among them and with Napoleon, per `principles/settings.md#figures-and-relations`. Corps-level organizational facts (sizes, ranks, the marshalate's creation) are at `settings/008-grande-armee.md`; battle-day deployment and action are at `settings/012-austerlitz.md`.

## Marshal Louis-Alexandre Berthier {#marshal-berthier}

**Standing: established by research, with several vivid anecdotes flagged as secondhand.** Born 20 November 1753, Versailles (died 1 June 1815, Bamberg, circumstances disputed among accident, suicide, and murder). Son of a royal topographical engineer, appointed topographical engineer himself at age twelve, wounded twice serving under Rochambeau and Lafayette in the American War of Independence — a formed technical professional before the Revolution, not a self-made revolutionary officer. Chief of staff of the Army of Italy from 1796 and of the Grande Armée from 1805, a role he never left until 1814. Physically strong and athletic in youth; by 1805, a small, portly man who bit his nails visibly when a decision troubled him, silent and stern to strangers but privately generous to struggling officers and returning émigrés. His defining function is not independent command but the precise, exhausting transmission of Napoleon's orders — a mobile filing and mapping system compact enough for one campaign coach, through which all army correspondence passed and which he personally signed. Soldiers called him "Napoleon's wife," a joke about constant proximity and uncomplaining absorption of Napoleon's temper rather than about intimacy of address; this canon establishes no instance of Napoleon using the informal register with him. Since May 1796 he has carried an enduring devotion to Giuseppina Visconti, a married Milanese noblewoman, an attachment the army treats as a standing joke rather than a scandal; full detail is under its own entry below.

Sources: R. P. Dunn-Pattison, *Napoleon's Marshals* (1909), full text at [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34400/34400-h/34400-h.htm) (read directly), for the physical description, the "Napoleon's wife" soldiers' nickname, and the Chief of Staff function; Britannica, "Louis-Alexandre Berthier"; napoleon.org (Franck Favier interview).

## Marshal Jean Lannes {#marshal-lannes}

**Standing: established by research, with the reported deathbed rebuke to Napoleon flagged as disputed legend.** Born 10 April 1769, Lectoure, Gascony (died 31 May 1809, Vienna, of wounds from Aspern-Essling). Genuinely low-born — son of a small landowner or dyer, apprenticed to a dyer before enlisting — and rose entirely on demonstrated courage: colonel by 1793, brigade commander by 1795, general of brigade by 1796, all before thirty. Short, blunt, outspoken, and profane, capable of real rage, yet held in Napoleon's highest personal esteem and the only marshal historians consistently attest was permitted to address him with the informal "tu," a friendship forged at Arcole and Lodi and hardened in Egypt. Napoleon's own summary of him — "I found him a pygmy and left him a giant" — captures the trajectory. Do not write him as a genial rogue: he suppressed the Cairo revolt and dealt harshly with Italian partisans. His reported deathbed rebuke of Napoleon at Aspern-Essling — accusing him of reckless ambition — is treated by Fondation Napoléon historians as a case study in later legend formation rather than documented fact, and this canon marks it accordingly.

Sources: [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Jean Lannes"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_lannes.html); [frenchempire.net — "Jean Lannes"](https://www.frenchempire.net/biographies/lannes/); napoleon.org (on the deathbed-quote legend as later legend formation, title only accessible).

## Marshal Joachim Murat {#marshal-murat}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 25 March 1767, Labastide-Murat, Lot (executed by firing squad, 13 October 1815, Pizzo Calabro). Son of a prosperous innkeeper and postmaster who also served as steward for the Talleyrand family, giving young Joachim early proximity to nobility he did not possess; intended for the priesthood, he abandoned seminary in 1787 for the army. Vain, ambitious, and impetuous by near-universal contemporary account, flamboyantly dressed (scarlet, gold-embroidered pantaloons, ostrich and heron plumes, a diamond-hilted sword), and physically striking — blue-eyed, athletic, an exceptional horseman. He personally forced the deputies out of the Orangerie at Brumaire, physically enabling the coup that made his later career possible (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-brumaire`). Married Caroline Bonaparte (civil ceremony 20 January 1800, religious 4 January 1802) after pursuing the match against rival suitors including Lannes; Napoleon initially favored other candidates and was persuaded by Joséphine. Commands the Cavalry Reserve at Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`). His defining flaw, per Dunn-Pattison, is an inability to accept that he is being deceived by a flattering counterpart — a vanity that made the November 1805 Hollabrunn armistice possible (see the relation below) and that recurs across his career.

Sources: [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Joachim Murat, 1767–1815"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_murat.html); [Britannica — "Joachim Murat"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joachim-Murat); R. P. Dunn-Pattison, *Napoleon's Marshals* (1909), digitized at [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34400/34400-h/34400-h.htm), for the vanity-and-flattery characterization, read directly.

## Marshal Louis-Nicolas Davout {#marshal-davout}

**Standing: established by research, with one battlefield detail (four horses shot under him at Austerlitz) singly sourced and flagged.** Born 10 May 1770, Annoux, Burgundy (died 1 June 1823, Paris) — the youngest of the marshals. From impoverished minor nobility with a family cavalry tradition; educated at Brienne (a different period from Napoleon's own attendance) and the École Militaire; dismissed from the regular army in 1791 for radical politics, then immediately elected colonel of a volunteer battalion. He altered the spelling of his name from the noble "d'Avout" to the plainer "Davout" to distance himself from his origins. First met Napoleon in Paris, at rue de la Victoire, on 28 March 1798, shortly before they departed together for Egypt, and made a poor first impression on him; the poor terms continued through the Egyptian campaign but did not originate there. The relationship transformed by 1800–04, and Napoleon gave him III Corps in 1805 before he had proven himself in independent command — a genuine mark of trust that this canon treats as being at its peak in the Austerlitz period. Known as "the Iron Marshal" for absolute discipline: he forbade unauthorized foraging and secured authority to punish marauders who robbed civilians, was distrustful and authoritarian toward his own officers yet paternally attentive to ordinary soldiers' shoes, food, and medical care. Balding despite his youth and wearing spectacles — unusual for a senior commander of the era — he looked, per contemporary description, more like a monk or bookseller than a battlefield leader, indifferent to presentation and personally awkward in society, yet wrote affectionately to his wife, Louise Aimée Julie Leclerc (married 9 November 1801, sister of General Leclerc, Pauline Bonaparte's late husband; the contract was signed by Napoleon and Joséphine themselves).

Sources: [napoleon.org — "DAVOUT, Louis Nicolas (10 May 1770 – 1 June 1823)"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/davout-louis-nicolas/); [napoleon.org — "The Iron Marshal: A Biography of Louis N. Davout"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-iron-marshal-a-biography-of-louis-n-davout/); [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Louis-Nicolas Davout"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_davout.html). This canon has not opened A. G. Macdonell's *Napoleon and His Marshals* (1934) and therefore does not cite it.

## Marshal Jean-de-Dieu Soult {#marshal-soult}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact primary document for the Napoleon attribution below still unpinned and a countervailing critical Wellington remark flagged as unverified.** Born 29 March 1769, Saint-Amans-la-Bastide, Tarn (died 26 November 1851). Son of a country notary; enlisted as a private at sixteen after his father's 1785 death to support his mother, a purely economic motive rather than inherited military tradition. Rose fast — sergeant 1791, captain 1793, brigadier after Fleurus 1794, Marshal 1804. Nicknamed "Bras de Fer" ("Iron Arm") by his own troops for brutal discipline at Boulogne; walks with a permanent limp from a leg wound suffered at Montecreto during the Siege of Genoa (13 April 1800) — shot in the leg, then robbed and held prisoner for weeks before being freed after Marengo — which several sources connect to a documented reluctance to expose himself personally at the front thereafter. Ambitious and acquisitive by near-universal consensus — he later earns the mocking nickname "King Nicolas" for reputedly aspiring to the Portuguese throne while commanding there, and as military governor of Andalusia (1810–14) systematically looted Spanish religious art (over 180 paintings). The "best tactician in Europe" line commonly and incorrectly attributed to Wellington is, per convergent sourcing, actually Napoleon's own verdict on Soult after Austerlitz — rendered in French as "le premier manœuvrier de l'Europe" ("the foremost maneuverer/tactician in Europe") — not a Wellington remark at all; this canon corrects the misattribution accordingly, while noting the exact primary document (letter, bulletin, or reported remark) behind Napoleon's phrase has not itself been traced. This canon also could not independently verify the countervailing critical Wellington remark it previously asserted as "reliably sourced" ("never seemed to know how to handle troops after a battle had begun") — the underlying *Quarterly Review* citation for it could not be confirmed and is dropped; a character may repeat that line only as rumor, never as narrated fact.

Sources: [Napoléon & Empire — "Soult, Jean-de-Dieu"](https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/soult.php), directly confirming the "best tactician in Europe" / "premier manœuvrier de l'Europe" phrase as Napoleon's own post-Austerlitz verdict, not Wellington's; Wikipedia, "Jean-de-Dieu Soult," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-de-Dieu_Soult (the Genoa wound of 13 April 1800 at Montecreto and its lasting effect on his battlefield conduct; the "King Nicolas" nickname; the Andalusian art looting); "Soult, Nicolas Jean de Dieu," *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed. (1911), via Wikisource, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclopædia_Britannica/Soult,_Nicolas_Jean_de_Dieu. The three concur on 13 April 1800 for the Genoa wound, against the 13 May 1800 date this canon previously carried from a single hobbyist site. The countervailing critical Wellington quotation about Soult could not be traced to a verifiable primary source in this research pass; the *Quarterly Review* vol. 257 (1931) p. 362 citation previously given for it could not be confirmed and is not retained.

## Marshal Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte {#marshal-bernadotte}

**Standing: established by research, with the "Mort aux Rois" tattoo legend flagged as disputed — likely a later theatrical invention rather than a documented fact.** Born 26 January 1763, Pau, Béarn (died 8 March 1844, Stockholm, as King Charles XIV John of Sweden) — the oldest of the corps commanders at Austerlitz. Son of a local prosecutor; after his father's 1780 death enlisted as a private in the royal army and rose as a career NCO for a full decade before the Revolution accelerated his advance — sergeant-major by 1788, colonel by 1794, general of division by October 1794. A reputed Jacobin sympathizer — a documented 1797 newspaper statement has him declaring himself "a republican both by principle and conviction" who would fight all royalists "to my dying hour" — tall, handsome, and vain about his dress, with real demonstrated battlefield leadership but a documented pattern of self-interested political flexibility that recurs across his career. Governor of Hanover from 1804 and one of the original 1804 marshalate, he commands I Corps at Austerlitz, held in central reserve; multiple assessments agree he did not distinguish himself there, in contrast to Davout's and Soult's roles the same day. On 3 October 1805 his corps's march through the neutral Prussian enclave of Ansbach, en route to the Danube, provoked genuine Prussian fury and stands as the diplomatically live 1805-period episode for his conduct — the more severe insubordination charges against him belong to the 1806 Jena–Auerstedt campaign and postdate this canon's primary window. His engagement, from December 1805, to family ties detailed in the relation below runs beneath his entire career.

Sources: The Napoleon Series, John Cook, "Bernadotte 1806 – Is There a Case for the Defence?," https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles-campaigns/bernadotte-1806-is-there-a-case-for-the-defence/; napoleon.org, "BERNADOTTE, Jean-Baptiste-Jules," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/bernadotte-jean-baptiste-jules/ (governor of Hanover 1804, the original 1804 Marshalate, his corps at Ulm and Austerlitz in 1805, and the verdict that he was "one of Napoleon's least aggressive commanders"); Swedish Wikipedia, "Karl XIV Johan," https://sv.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_XIV_Johan, on the tattoo legend — characterized there as "probably a myth" ("troligtvis en myt"), likely originating in an 1833 French stage play, *Le Camarade de lit*, rather than in any documented tattoo, and separately corroborating the 1797 republican newspaper statement quoted above; this canon's previous citation to faktoider.nu is dropped, that site having gone dark (the URL now 404s and the domain redirects to a parked-hosting page).

## General Jean Rapp {#general-rapp}

**Standing: established by research.** Jean Rapp (1771–1821), one of Napoleon's personal aides-de-camp, renowned for courage and repeated wounds, later the author of a valuable memoir. At Austerlitz he led a charge of Imperial Guard cavalry, including the Guard Mamelukes, against the Russian Imperial Guard's counterattack on the Pratzen; despite taking several saber wounds he broke the Russian cavalry and personally captured Prince Nikolai Repnin-Volkonsky, colonel of the Russian Chevalier Gardes — an episode corroborated across sources and commemorated in a painting by Baron Gérard commissioned by Napoleon.

Sources: [napoleon.org — "Rapp, Jean"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/rapp-jean/) (the charge at the head of the Guard Mameluks and Mounted Chasseurs/Grenadiers of the Guard, breaking the Chevalier Guards; promotion to général de division 24 December 1805); [Wikipedia — "Nikolai Repnin-Volkonsky"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Repnin-Volkonsky); [Wikipedia — "Battle of Austerlitz, 2 December 1805 (Gérard)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Austerlitz,_2_December_1805_(G%C3%A9rard)) for the painting commission.

## Marshal Auguste de Marmont {#marshal-marmont}

**Standing: established by research.** Auguste Frédéric Louis Viesse de Marmont (1774–1852), one of the original eighteen marshals of 1804, met Napoleon at the Siege of Toulon (1793, `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-toulon`) and became one of his longest-standing personal connections, serving as aide-de-camp through Italy and Egypt. In 1805 he commanded II Corps but was assigned to guard the Alpine approaches and southern flank against Austrian intervention from Italy, and so was not present at Austerlitz. For later reference: his 5 April 1814 defection, surrendering his corps to the Allies outside Paris without informing his own men, coined the French verb "raguser" (from his later ducal title, Duke of Ragusa) meaning to betray.

Sources: [napoleon.org — "MARMONT, Auguste-Frédéric-Louis Viesse de"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/marmont-auguste-frederic-louis-viesse-de/); [historyofwar.org — "Auguste Frédéric Louis Viesse de Marmont, Duc de Raguse"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_marmont.html) (the 1805 II Corps assignment guarding the Alpine/Italian flank, absent from Austerlitz; the 5 April 1814 defection and the coinage "raguser").

## Marshal Michel Ney {#marshal-ney}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact wording of his "iron cage" boast and his refusal-of-blindfold last words each attested only as popular paraphrase rather than a single fixed verbatim record.** Born 10 January 1769, Saarlouis (executed 7 December 1815, Paris). Son of a barrel-cooper; enlisted as a private in 1787 and rose entirely on demonstrated battlefield courage rather than patronage, already a marshal of 1804 alongside Berthier, Lannes, and Murat. Napoleon reportedly called him "the bravest of the brave" after his conduct commanding the rearguard during the 1812 retreat — cut off from the main army after the second action at Krasnoi, he led the last survivors across the frozen Dnieper at Gusinoe to rejoin Napoleon, and is reputedly, with the appropriate hedge this canon preserves from its own sources, the last imperial soldier to leave Russian soil (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-retreat`). His judgment away from direct combat proved far less reliable than his personal courage: as one of the marshals who confronted Napoleon at Fontainebleau in April 1814, telling him the army would not march on Paris — "the army will obey its chiefs" — he helped force the first abdication (`settings/017-the-collapse-and-first-abdication.md#collapse-fontainebleau-abdication`); publicly loyal to the restored Louis XVIII by 1815, he boasted he would bring Napoleon back to Paris "in an iron cage," then met him in person at Auxerre on 18 March and defected back within the hour, in a scene one eyewitness recorded with "his eyes... full of tears." At Quatre-Bras and Waterloo his combat leadership remained genuine but his operational judgment failed at scale: he delayed the Quatre-Bras attack past the point it could have easily succeeded, lost control of d'Erlon's I Corps, and at Waterloo launched a series of massed but unsupported cavalry charges that could not break formed infantry squares (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-quatre-bras`; `settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-chronology`). Arrested for treason 3 August 1815, tried before the Chamber of Peers 4–6 December and convicted 137–17, he was executed the following day near the Luxembourg Gardens, refusing a blindfold and giving the order to fire himself; the exact wording of his final words is attested in at least two overlapping but non-identical popular traditions this canon does not adopt as certainly verbatim, though both agree on his refusal of a blindfold and his own command to fire.

S. Pope (1999), not independently opened by this canon; Wikipedia, "Michel Ney," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ney, which attributes the "the army will obey its chiefs" exchange there to David Gates, *The Napoleonic Wars 1803–1815* (2003), p. 259 — not opened directly by this canon, the same standing already noted at `settings/017-the-collapse-and-first-abdication.md#collapse-fontainebleau-abdication` — and "Hundred Days," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Days; French Senate, "Vol de l'Aigle et Cent jours," senat.fr, for the 18 March Auxerre meeting date; lestafette.unblog.fr, a period memoir excerpt quoted for the eyewitness detail of the Auxerre reunion, treated as a secondary transcription this canon has not independently verified against the original; frenchempire.net, "Michel Ney," for the "iron cage" boast and the Waterloo battlefield remarks, neither independently cross-verified against a primary source.

## Marshal Emmanuel de Grouchy {#marshal-grouchy}

**Standing: established by research, with a specific "capable but unimaginative" characterization from a named historian not located in this research pass.** Born 23 October 1766 (died 29 May 1847). A cavalry officer from 1781, "a cavalry commander of considerable distinction throughout the Revolutionary and Napoleonic eras," made a Marshal of the Empire only on 5 April 1815, during the Hundred Days — against the explicit recommendation of Marshal Davout, then Minister of War, a detail this canon treats as evidence that at least one of his own peers doubted his fitness for independent command from the very start of his marshalate, without treating that doubt as proof of what followed. Detached with the army's right wing on 17 June 1815 to pursue Blücher's retreating Prussians, he followed the route his written orders specified even after hearing Waterloo's cannon fire the next day, winning a tactical victory over Thielemann's Prussian rearguard at Wavre while the rest of Blücher's army marched, unengaged, to decide the battle he had been sent to prevent (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-grouchy-detachment`). He went into American exile at Philadelphia after the second Restoration, received amnesty in November 1819, returned to France in May 1820, and was restored to the marshalate and the peerage only in 1831–1832, under Louis-Philippe, following the July Revolution. He spent much of the rest of his life defending his Waterloo conduct in print — *Observations sur la relation de la campagne de 1815 par le général de Gourgaud* (1818) and the multi-part *Fragments Historiques Relatifs à la Campagne de 1815 et à la Bataille de Waterloo* (1829–1830) — arguing he had followed his orders correctly and was made the campaign's scapegoat; this canon has located only the existence and titles of these works, not their content, and records his self-defense as a documented biographical fact rather than as a settled historiographical verdict on the question itself.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Emmanuel de Grouchy, marquis de Grouchy," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_de_Grouchy,_marquis_de_Grouchy; "Battle of Wavre," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wavre; "Waterloo campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign.

## Relation: Napoleon and Berthier {#relation-napoleon-berthier}

**Standing: established by research.** Formal, not fraternal — no attestation exists of Napoleon using the informal register with him, in explicit contrast to Lannes. The bond's distinctiveness is duration and total dependency rather than intimacy: Napoleon called him "the man who has served me longest and has never failed me," and rewarded him accordingly (sovereign principality of Neuchâtel, 1806; Prince of Wagram, 1809). Berthier obeyed instantly and completely even when the order cost him personally — forced in 1808, at 54, into a dynastic marriage with Princess Marie Élisabeth of Bavaria-Birkenfeld despite his ongoing devotion to Giuseppina Visconti, he complied without recorded protest of the kind that would have been unthinkable from him. The bond's dark coda, for later reference: Berthier accepted the 1814 Bourbon Restoration and refused to rejoin Napoleon for the Hundred Days, and after Waterloo Napoleon is reported to have said, "If Berthier had been there, I would not have met this misfortune."

Sources: napoleon.org (Franck Favier interview, on Berthier's total obedience and its cost); R. P. Dunn-Pattison, *Napoleon's Marshals* (1909), digitized at [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34400/34400-h/34400-h.htm), confirming the 1808 Bavaria-Birkenfeld marriage and Marie Élisabeth's own affection for Visconti, read directly.

## Relation: Napoleon and Lannes {#relation-napoleon-lannes}

**Standing: established by research.** The one marshal-level friendship this canon establishes as carrying genuine mutual informality, forged at Arcole and Lodi and hardened in the shared hardship of Egypt. Napoleon tolerated from Lannes a bluntness and profanity he tolerated from no other marshal, and after Austerlitz let Lannes's furious, unauthorized leave-taking (provoked by feeling the campaign bulletin had slighted V Corps) go entirely unpunished — a measure of the latitude the friendship bought that this canon establishes as unique among the marshalate. At Aspern-Essling in May 1809, when Lannes was mortally wounded, Napoleon knelt at the stretcher and wept, his coat stained with Lannes's blood, and was later found alone in his tent, immobile and silent, with tears falling into his soup; his public statement afterward — "I have lost the most distinguished general in my army, my companion in arms for sixteen years, and my best friend" — is the clearest documented statement of Napoleon's capacity for personal grief in this canon.

Sources: [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Jean Lannes"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_lannes.html); [frenchempire.net — "Jean Lannes"](https://www.frenchempire.net/biographies/lannes/), for the Arcole/Egypt friendship, the informal "tu," and Napoleon's grief and public statement on Lannes's death.

## Relation: Napoleon and Murat {#relation-napoleon-murat}

**Standing: established by research.** A relationship of family obligation, real reliance on Murat's cavalry generalship, and periodic, genuine fury at his vanity-driven errors. The clearest documented instance is Napoleon's dictated rebuke after Murat's unauthorized three-day armistice with Bagration's rearguard at Hollabrunn/Schöngrabern (15–16 November 1805), granted because Murat mistook Bagration's force for Kutuzov's main army and believed a flattering claim that peace negotiations were underway — allowing the main Russian army to escape a trap Napoleon had built. Napoleon's dictated response of 16 November 1805 survives in the published *Correspondance* and this canon treats its wording as available for direct use, in this translation of the French text: "It is impossible for me to find words to express my dissatisfaction. You command only my vanguard and you have no right to make an armistice without my order; you are making me lose the fruit of a campaign. Break the armistice at once and march on the enemy." The letter goes on to call the Tsar's aide-de-camp a scoundrel, to observe that an officer without powers is nothing, and to tie the humiliation directly to the Vienna bridge: "the Austrians let themselves be tricked over the passage of the Vienna bridge, and you let yourself be tricked by an aide-de-camp of the Emperor." The widely reprinted English version of this rebuke opens with "My cousin" — that salutation does not appear in the published French text, where the letter begins directly with the expression of displeasure, and this canon does not use it. Murat broke the truce within hours of receiving it, but the main Russian army was already gone. The sequence is sharper than it first appears: just two days *before* Hollabrunn, on 13 November 1805, Murat had run an almost identical false-armistice bluff himself, alongside Lannes, to seize the Vienna Danube bridge intact (`settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-tabor-bridge`) — the same vanity that let a flattering counterpart deceive him at Hollabrunn made him, when his own ego was flattered rather than crossed, a skilled deceiver in turn at Tabor, and he did not recognize the identical trick coming back at him within forty-eight hours.

Sources: the French text of the letter to Murat, 16 November 1805, as published in the *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier* (1858–1870) and reproduced at histoire-empire.org, "Correspondance de Napoléon — Novembre 1805," https://www.histoire-empire.org/correspondance_de_napoleon/1805/novembre_02.htm — this canon has opened that page and transcribed the wording from it, including confirming the absence of the "Mon cousin" salutation carried by circulating English versions; historyofwar.org, "Combat of Hollabrunn, 15–16 November 1805," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/combat_hollabrunn_1805.html; `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-tabor-bridge`.

## Relation: Napoleon and Davout {#relation-napoleon-davout}

**Standing: established by research.** Early relations were actively hostile: the two men met in Paris on 28 March 1798, shortly before departing together for Egypt (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-davout`), and Davout made a poor first impression that hardened rather than originated during the Egyptian campaign — contemporaries reported Napoleon disliked him there for associating with officers hostile to him, and Davout's personal untidiness particularly displeased him. The relationship transformed by 1800–04, and by December 1805 stands at its peak of trust: Napoleon gave him III Corps before independent-command proof, and Davout's forced march to Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`) repays that trust decisively. The bond never becomes a friendship in the register Lannes enjoys — a later historian's verdict, that Napoleon "had needed and used Davout, without the latter being a close friend or confidant," is a fair summary this canon adopts for characterization — and sours only later (1811–12) when Murat and Jérôme poison Napoleon against him over suspected Polish ambitions, outside this canon's primary window.

Sources: [napoleon.org — "The Iron Marshal: A Biography of Louis N. Davout"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-iron-marshal-a-biography-of-louis-n-davout/); [napoleon.org — "DAVOUT, Louis Nicolas"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/davout-louis-nicolas/), for the Egypt-era friction and its transformation by 1800–04.

## Relation: Napoleon and Bernadotte {#relation-napoleon-bernadotte}

**Standing: established by research.** A relationship complicated from its origin by a double family tie: Napoleon had been engaged to Désirée Clary (21 April 1795, broken off 6 September 1795 when he met Joséphine) before she married Bernadotte on 17 August 1798, and Désirée's sister Julie Clary had already married Joseph Bonaparte in 1794 — binding the two men as in-laws twice over while their professional relationship stayed openly cool. Napoleon's regard fluctuated: at points he trusted Bernadotte enormously (reportedly considering him a likely successor had he died during the Marengo campaign), at others found him actively difficult to work with, and Bernadotte's habitual questioning and slow execution of orders eroded trust as a persistent trait across their shared career rather than a single incident. From St. Helena, Napoleon's final, measured verdict — that Bernadotte "never promised or declared an intention to stay true... I can therefore accuse him of ingratitude, but not of treason" — is the fairest documented summary and this canon treats it as the correct register for the relationship even in its earlier, 1805-period form.

## Relation: Lannes and Murat {#relation-lannes-murat}

**Standing: established by research, with the depth of the rivalry flagged as likely inflated by an unreliable memoir source.** Popular accounts and Laure Junot's notoriously gossipy memoirs present a bitter feud, but surviving letters show real, mutual warmth alongside genuine professional jealousy — both men used the informal "tu" with each other, Murat stayed as Lannes's houseguest for weeks in July 1808, and Marbot's memoirs suggest Napoleon deliberately praised each to the other, meaning some of the rivalry narrative may be Napoleon's own manufacture rather than the men's independent feeling — though that last suggestion carries only the weight Marbot carries, which per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-marbot` is low, Elting dismissing his tales as "howling cock-and-bull inventions." This canon therefore offers the Napoleon-as-instigator reading as a possibility a scene may entertain, never as an established cause. The clearest genuine point of friction is Caroline Bonaparte, whose hand both men pursued before Murat won it (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-caroline`). At Austerlitz the two cooperate effectively on the French left against Bagration despite this tension, which this canon treats as evidence the rivalry, while real, did not override professional discipline under Napoleon's command.

Sources: [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Joachim Murat"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_murat.html); [historyofwar.org — "Marshal Jean Lannes"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_lannes.html); `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-marbot` (on Marbot's reliability generally).

## Marshal Berthier's household: the Visconti attachment {#berthier-visconti}

**Standing: established by research, with two specific anecdotes — the desert shrine and the exact wording of his outburst — not yet decided, in that no source this canon has read attests either one.** In May 1796, at a reception in Milan, Berthier fell instantly and lastingly for Giuseppina "Visconti," already married; the attachment persisted essentially for the rest of his life despite Napoleon's disapproval and became, per multiple sources, a standing joke in the army. He carried her portrait on campaign, including to Egypt. Forced by Napoleon in 1808, at 54, into a dynastic marriage to Princess Marie Élisabeth of Bavaria-Birkenfeld, he obeyed instantly, as he obeyed everything; two weeks after the wedding, Madame Visconti's own husband died, and Berthier's reported outburst — "To what a miserable condition I am reduced! With a little more constancy, Madame Visconti might have been my wife!" — has no source this canon has been able to locate: it does not appear in Dunn-Pattison's 1909 text, which this canon has read directly and which was the citation previously given for it. The line should be treated as unattributed popular tradition, usable as characterization but never as a verbatim-certain quotation, until a source for it is actually found and read. Rather than rupture, the household absorbed Visconti, with his young wife reportedly conceiving an affection for her — an arrangement Wikipedia notes "infuriated the Emperor."

Sources: R. P. Dunn-Pattison, *Napoleon's Marshals* (1909), per `settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-dunn-pattison`, confirming the 1808 Bavaria-Birkenfeld marriage and Marie Élisabeth's own affection for Madame Visconti; that same unit records this canon's direct reading failing to locate either the outburst or the "desert shrine" anecdote in the text, which had previously been cited for both, and no substitute citation is offered here rather than one this canon has not read. Wikipedia, "Louis-Alexandre Berthier," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Alexandre_Berthier, for the household arrangement after 1808.

## Relation: Napoleon and Ney {#relation-napoleon-ney}

**Standing: established by research.** His side: genuine, near-unconditional reliance on Ney's combat leadership under direct pressure — the 1812 rearguard command is the clearest instance — combined with a documented willingness to give him operational latitude, independent command of the left wing at Quatre-Bras and Waterloo, that his record as a subordinate commander rather than a battlefield fighter did not fully justify. Ney's side: total, repeatedly demonstrated personal courage, but a loyalty that proved more attached to Napoleon's physical presence than to any prior public commitment he had made in his absence — he denounced Napoleon in the restored Bourbons' service, boasting of an iron cage, and reversed that denunciation within the hour of actually standing in front of him again at Auxerre, in tears (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-ney`). Neither man ever resolved the tension between what each needed from the other: Napoleon needed Ney's courage but not his independent judgment, and gave him independent command anyway when the 1815 campaign required more marshals than Napoleon had marshals he trusted with that judgment; Ney supplied the courage and, under the operational pressure he had never been trusted to manage on his own, could not supply what Quatre-Bras and Waterloo needed instead.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-ney` above and `settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-quatre-bras`, each established by research; the characterization itself is this canon's own reading of that record.
