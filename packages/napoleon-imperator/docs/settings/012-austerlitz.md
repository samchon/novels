# Austerlitz

This file records the terrain, weather, order of battle, chronology, and aftermath of the Battle of Austerlitz (2 December 1805) in enough detail that the work's opening — a storyline decision this file does not itself make, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality` — needs to invent nothing of the battle's setting or mechanics. Diplomatic and operational lead-up is at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md`; figure-level detail for commanders is at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md` and `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md`.

## Terrain {#austerlitz-terrain}

**Standing: established by research.** The battlefield lies roughly 10 km southeast of Brno, around the town of Austerlitz (modern Slavkov u Brna, Moravia, now the Czech Republic). The Pratzen Heights (Pratzeberg), a roughly 5-km ridge and plateau running southeast between the Goldbach and Littawa (Litava) valleys, dominates the field by roughly 40 m of relative relief, cresting at Staré Vinohrady ("Old Vineyards," ~290 m absolute) in the north through a saddle above the village of Prace to Pratzeberg (~325 m) in the south. Napoleon deliberately withdrew his forces from the heights on the night of 1–2 December, ceding this dominant ground to lure the Allies into descending off it to attack his apparently weak right flank near the Goldbach villages; once the Allies committed downhill, the center they had emptied became Soult's objective. The Goldbach stream (Říčka) runs south through a shallow, marshy valley dividing the French position from the ground below the heights, fed by the Bosenitz (Roketnice) brook; its December banks were boggy enough to slow artillery and cavalry, and the villages strung along it — Telnitz (Telnice), Sokolnitz (Sokolnice, with its walled château and pheasantry), Kobelnitz (Kobylnice), Puntowitz (Ponětovice), Bosenitz — channeled infantry into narrow, contested approaches. Zuran Hill (Žuráň), a low rise north of the heights, was Napoleon's forward command post; he overnighted nearby in a straw shelter in a gravel pit and gave Soult the order to storm the Pratzen from there around 8:30 a.m. Satschan (Zatchan) and Menitz ponds — shallow fishponds and marshes south of the villages, near the hamlet of Augezd (Újezd) and St. Anthony's chapel — are the site of the disputed drowning episode recorded below. The Santon, a roughly 210-m knoll on the northern Brno–Olomouc road, was fortified overnight with captured Austrian guns and anchored Lannes's V Corps left. The roads were reportedly nearly indistinguishable from the surrounding frozen fields in the days before the battle, though no snowstorm fell on the day of the battle itself.

## Weather and the sun of Austerlitz {#austerlitz-weather}

**Standing: disputed between sources as to the wording of the Soult exchange and the clock; established by research for the fog, Napoleon's reply, and the later mythologizing.** Dense, cold morning mist covered the low ground where the fighting at Telnitz and Sokolnitz began around 7–8 a.m., while the Pratzen Heights, higher up, caught early light. Both eyewitnesses this canon has read agree that Napoleon meant to use it: Thiébault has him "wishing to turn to advantage a fog which covered the valleys and concealed our troops," and Ségur that the bivouac smoke and vapours, heavier here than elsewhere, "concealed from the eyes of the Russians our centre." He needed it to hold just long enough for the Allies to descend off the plateau.

The exchange with Soult, the canon's most quotable moment, does not survive scrutiny in the form this canon previously gave it, and no downstream unit may put "less than twenty minutes" in Soult's mouth as documented speech. Thiébault, a brigade commander in Saint-Hilaire's division, has the marshals gathered round Napoleon "before 8 o'clock," the exchange coming "as the sun was rising," and reports Soult only in indirect speech, as saying twenty minutes at most would be enough. Ségur, Napoleon's own aide-de-camp, quotes Soult directly — "Ten minutes" — and has the sun break through "not yet eight o'clock," with Vandamme's and Saint-Hilaire's divisions attacking at eight and the whole front engaged by nine. The 30th Bulletin, the official account, mentions no mist at all and no exchange, only that Napoleon waited for the horizon to clear and gave his orders at the sun's first rays. Later summaries put the exchange at about 8:45 and the advance at about 9 a.m., and a further account has the Pratzen already in Soult's hands by nine. What both eyewitnesses do agree on, and what this canon therefore treats as the quotable line, is Napoleon's reply rather than Soult's answer: a further quarter of an hour — "we will wait another quarter of an hour," in Thiébault's version. Thiébault adds that Napoleon withheld the signal until an aide reported, a little prematurely, that the heights were abandoned.

As the divisions began their ascent the mist tore apart and full sun broke over the ridge — the "soleil d'Austerlitz." The mythologizing is real but this canon assigns it accurately: the 30th Bulletin supplies only the framing of a radiant sunrise falling on the coronation anniversary, not an omen, and the personal-omen reading belongs to the memoir tradition and to Napoleon's own later usage. He did invoke it again — on the eve of Borodino, "tomorrow at five o'clock, the sun of Austerlitz," and on the morning itself, "behold the sun of Austerlitz!" — and Ségur, who recorded both, immediately punctures the second: it rose on the Russian side, made the French conspicuous to their fire, and dazzled them. The overall day was cold and crisp once the fog lifted, not snowy; the ground was frozen hard rather than muddy on the day of battle itself, though the approach march in preceding days had been through mud and slush.

## Order of battle: French {#austerlitz-oob-french}

**Standing: established by research, with total strength, the grenadier division's participation, and Davout's march all disputed between sources.** Napoleon held supreme command. Total French strength is variously cited as roughly 67,000, 68,000–73,000 (Britannica), 73,000 with 139 guns (the Duffy and Smith table), or about 75,000 with about 154 guns (austerlitz.org), depending on whether all of Davout's corps, the Guard, and Bernadotte's corps are counted as present at the start versus arriving during the day; guns run 139–157 pieces across sources. A figure of 65,000 is also in circulation, attributed to Chandler; this canon repeats it only as a commonly cited number and attaches no page to it, having not opened that book. Corps and commanders: Soult's IV Corps (~23,600, three infantry divisions — Saint-Hilaire, Vandamme, Legrand — plus light cavalry), the largest corps, delivered the decisive Pratzen assault. Lannes's V Corps (~12,700) anchored the north near the Santon, engaging Bagration. Bernadotte's I Corps (~11,300–13,000, Dupont's division being detached) held initial reserve behind V Corps, later supporting Vandamme. Davout's III Corps arrived in the portion detailed below (roughly 4,300–9,300, sources differing sharply on how much of the corps is counted as engaged), arriving late on the night of 1 December just in time to reinforce Legrand at Telnitz/Sokolnitz. The forced march from Vienna that brought it there is famous and real but its dimensions are not settled: figures in circulation run from roughly 110 km to roughly 140 km, over anything from 36 to 50 hours, and no source this canon has opened states a distance and a duration together on its own authority. This canon flags the arithmetic explicitly rather than letting the range imply more than the sources support: the low end (110 km/50 hr ≈ 53 km/day) sits inside the general forced-march ceiling of roughly 56 km/day this canon states at `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching`, but the high end (140 km/36 hr ≈ 93 km/day) would nearly double that ceiling, a rate no other unit in this canon is credited with sustaining even briefly. A downstream scene may show the march as extraordinary and exceptional — the fastest sustained movement this canon records for any formation — but must not put a precise figure in a character's mouth as canon, and should not combine the widest-distance and shortest-duration figures together without acknowledging that pairing as the least corroborated extreme of the range. Murat's Cavalry Reserve (~7,400, cuirassier divisions under Nansouty and d'Hautpoul plus dragoons) was decisive in the northern cavalry fighting against Bagration and Liechtenstein. Bessières's Imperial Guard (~5,500) was held in reserve, committed only at the crisis point against the Russian Imperial Guard counterattack. The grenadier division (~5,500–5,700) formed an additional reserve, and two things about it are not settled: whether it was engaged at all — Nafziger, following the French general staff's Alombert and Colin, marks it "not in battle," while austerlitz.org and the Duffy/Smith table count it present in reserve — and who commanded it, since Oudinot was still convalescent from the wound he took at Hollabrunn and effective command was shared with, or exercised by, Duroc. A downstream scene may not put Oudinot at its head without acknowledging that. Key division commanders: Saint-Hilaire (Pratzen assault), Vandamme (took Staré Vinohrady, then defended it against the Guard counterattack), Legrand (held the extreme southern flank, bore the heaviest initial Allied attack), Friant (III Corps, retook Sokolnitz), Kellermann (light cavalry, north flank). Napoleon's command post: Zuran Hill early, moving onto the Pratzen itself as the center collapsed.

## Order of battle: Allied {#austerlitz-oob-allied}

**Standing: established by research, with total strength and gun counts disputed between sources.** Tsar Alexander I held nominal supreme command. Total Allied strength is commonly cited as 85,000–90,000 (roughly 70% Russian, 30% Austrian), with 278–334 guns cited across sources; one detailed count gives 113 battalions, 124 squadrons, 298 guns in 35 batteries, and 73,200–85,400 combatants depending on inclusion criteria. General Mikhail Kutuzov (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#kutuzov`) was the actual senior Russian field commander, overruled and reduced to commanding a single column. Emperor Francis (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`) was present but deferred to Alexander. Franz von Weyrother (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#weyrother`) authored the five-column attack disposition, roughly 41,000 men in the plan, meant to sweep the French right and cut communications to Vienna, rolling up Napoleon's line south to north — the exact plan Napoleon had anticipated and exploited. Friedrich Wilhelm von Buxhowden (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#buxhowden`) commanded the Allied left wing, the main southern attack against Telnitz/Sokolnitz, and was isolated and driven onto the ponds in the afternoon. Prince Pyotr Bagration (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#bagration`) commanded the Allied right and advance guard in the north. Grand Duke Constantine Pavlovich (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#constantine-pavlovich`), the Tsar's brother, commanded the Russian Imperial Guard reserve, leading its midday counterattack against Vandamme's division — initially breaking the French 4th Line Regiment and taking its Eagle, the only French standard lost in the battle, before being routed by Bessières's Guard cavalry and Rapp's charge (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#general-rapp`). Prince Johann I of Liechtenstein commanded Allied cavalry, largely in the north, engaged against Murat. Column commanders in the southern/central attack: Dmitry Dokhturov (1st column, ~13,240 infantry/250 cavalry), Louis Alexandre Andrault de Langéron (2nd column, ~11,250 infantry/300 cavalry, ordered the Sokolnitz bombardment), Ignaty Przybyszewski (3rd column, ~7,700 mixed, attacked Sokolnitz château, eventually surrendered after exhausting ammunition), Miloradovich/Kollowrat (4th, central column, ~23,900 infantry, the column broken atop the Pratzen by Soult), Michael von Kienmayer (Austrian advance force, ~7,000, opened the Telnitz fighting).

Sources: The Napoleon Series, "Russian-Austrian Order-of-Battle at Austerlitz: 2 December 1805" (Stephen Millar), https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles-campaigns/russian-austrian-order-of-battle-at-austerlitz-2-december-1805/, for the itemized 113 battalions, 124 squadrons, and 298 guns in 35 batteries, and the 73,200–85,400 combatant range; historyofwar.org, "Battle of Austerlitz, 2 December 1805," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_austerlitz.html; World History Encyclopedia, "Battle of Austerlitz," https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2253/battle-of-austerlitz/, for the commanders (Alexander, Kutuzov, Francis, Weyrother, Buxhowden, Bagration, Constantine) and the "swelled to 90,000" figure.

## Battle chronology {#austerlitz-chronology}

**Standing: established by research for the sequence of events; the clock times are a working reconstruction and are disputed between sources, per `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-weather`.** Night of 1–2 December: the French quietly abandon the Pratzen Heights, shifting the bulk of the army into dead ground west of the Goldbach, leaving only a deliberately thin screen (Legrand's division, later reinforced) near Telnitz/Sokolnitz as bait; Napoleon reads his proclamation (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`) to the army as he moves among the bivouac fires. Roughly 7:00–8:00 a.m.: the Allied attack opens in fog against Telnitz, held by the French 3rd Line Regiment; Kienmayer's Austrians and Dokhturov's column push the French out, Davout's arriving troops counterattack and retake it before being pushed back again; the position see-saws. Roughly 8:00–9:00 a.m.: Langéron's and Przybyszewski's columns assault Sokolnitz village and its walled château, defended by the French 26th Light Regiment and light infantry, with heavy, repeatedly-recaptured fighting. Roughly 8:30 a.m.: Napoleon, from Zuran, watches the Allied columns stripping the Pratzen bare to feed the southern attack. Somewhere between roughly 8:00 and 9:00 a.m.: the Soult exchange and the order to advance, after which Saint-Hilaire's and Vandamme's divisions storm the Pratzen Heights as the mist burns off. This timeline follows the later secondary reconstruction, which puts the exchange at about 8:45 and the advance at about 9; both eyewitnesses put it appreciably earlier, Ségur having the assault begin at eight, and the whole clock for this sequence is disputed per `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-weather`. Times in this entry are a working sequence for scene planning, not established to the quarter-hour. Over the following hour, hard fighting on the heights breaks the Allied 4th Column and isolates Buxhowden's southern force from the rest of the army. Roughly 9:00–11:00 a.m., north flank: Bagration's advance guard and Liechtenstein's cavalry engage Lannes and Murat around the Santon and Blasowitz; Russian Guard uhlans and Chevalier Guard cavalry clash with Kellermann's light cavalry (initially pushed back) and then Murat's massed heavy cavalry, which breaks the Russian horse — a largely separate cavalry battle throughout the morning that does not decide the day. Roughly 11:30 a.m.–1:00 p.m.: Vandamme's division, having taken Staré Vinohrady, is counterattacked by the Russian Imperial Guard under Grand Duke Constantine (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#constantine-pavlovich`) — the last serious Allied bid to retake the heights, breaking the French 4th Line and taking its Eagle before Napoleon commits Bessières's Guard cavalry and Rapp's charge, which breaks the Russian Guard in turn. Midday–early afternoon: with the center secured, Napoleon turns Soult's and Davout's forces south against Buxhowden's isolated left wing. Roughly 2:00–4:00 p.m.: the Allied left wing collapses and is driven south toward the Satschan/Menitz ponds and the Littawa; a French battery of 24 guns near St. Anthony's chapel fires on the retreating mass, some of which tries to cross the frozen ponds (see the disputed episode below). By roughly 4:00–5:00 p.m.: organized Allied resistance ends; the battle's total duration is roughly 8–9 hours, from about 7 a.m. to 4–5 p.m.

Sources: historyofwar.org, "Battle of Austerlitz, 2 December 1805," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_austerlitz.html; The Napoleon Series, "Austerlitz Tour: The Satschan Ponds," https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/virtual/austerlitz/c_satschan.html; Wikipedia, "Battle of Austerlitz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Austerlitz.

## The Satschan pond episode and the Bulletin {#austerlitz-satschan-pond}

**Standing: disputed between sources — a documented instance of Bulletin propaganda contradicted by physical reconstruction.** The 30th Bulletin de la Grande Armée (dated 12 Frimaire Year XIV / 3 December 1805) claimed roughly 20,000 Allied troops drowned when French artillery broke lake ice under fleeing Russian and Austrian columns near the Satschan and Menitz ponds — the Bulletin's own wording, read directly, is "20 mille hommes se jetant dans l'eau et se noyant dans les lacs" ("20,000 men throwing themselves into the water and drowning in the lakes"), which it explicitly compares to Aboukir. Modern investigation is decisive against this figure: only an estimated 5,000 Allied troops were even in that sector of the retreat, making 20,000 drownings mathematically impossible; when Napoleon ordered the ponds drained days after the battle, local testimony gathered by the historian J. Holland Rose from Professor Fournier of Vienna — the Oberamtmann Franz Brutmann and the parish priest of Telnitz among his informants — recovered only "twenty-eight or thirty cannon, 150 corpses of horses, but only two (some say three) human corpses" at Satschan, and nothing at all at Menitz. This canon treats "20,000 drowned" as a documented, deliberately exaggerated Bulletin propaganda figure (per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`) and "two or three bodies recovered on draining" as the established physical reconstruction, traced to Rose's own near-verbatim figure rather than to paraphrase drift; the gap between them is itself available as a scene of the propaganda machine at work, not a contradiction to be silently resolved to one number.

Sources: J. Holland Rose, "The Ice Incident at the Battle of Austerlitz," *English Historical Review* XVII (July 1902), pp. 537–538, reprinted as Appendix VII in *Napoleonic Studies* (London: George Bell &amp; Sons, 1904), pp. 382–385, https://archive.org/stream/napoleonicstudie00roserich/napoleonicstudie00roserich_djvu.txt — read directly; Rose's correspondence with Professor Fournier of Vienna and the local testimony he gathered, giving the recovered-body and recovered-matériel figures above; napoleon.org, "30e Bulletin de la Grande Armée (12 frimaire an XIV – 3 décembre 1805)," https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/30e-bulletin-de-la-grande-armee-12-frimaire-an-xiv-3-decembre-1805/, the Bulletin text itself, read directly. The Napoleon Series's "Satschan Ponds" tour page is not retained as evidentiary support here: read directly, it gives no casualty or recovery figures at all and is cited instead at `#austerlitz-terrain` for terrain only.

## Casualties {#austerlitz-casualties}

**Standing: established by research for the French totals; the Allied total and the captured-materiel figures are disputed between sources, and one previously carried figure is withdrawn as unlocatable.** French casualties: composite modern figures range 8,569–9,288 total, comprising 1,288–1,537 killed, 6,708–6,991 wounded, and 573–760 captured or missing; a commonly rounded figure is roughly 9,000 total. Allied casualties begin at roughly 27,000, of which some 15,000–16,000 killed or wounded and 12,000 captured. The 36,000 upper bound this canon previously carried has no locator in any source it has read and is withdrawn; what supports a figure above 27,000 is instead the Russian archival return, which lets the arithmetic be shown rather than asserted. The Russian report of killed and missing compiled for Kutuzov gives 19,371, against 131 guns lost, with 4,579 men subsequently rejoining the army; a separate artillery-department return gives 160 guns and 1,963 horses, and 11,638 Russian wounded and sick were treated in Austrian and French hospitals. Deducting the returnees and adding the hospitalised yields roughly 26,400 Russians alone, before Austrian losses — which also explains the otherwise puzzling split in the literature between "about 21,000" and "28,000 or more" Russian casualties, since the two figures differ precisely over whether the returnees are subtracted and the hospitalised added. Austrian losses are given as 4,000 by World History Encyclopedia against the 6,000 minimum this canon previously stated, and are treated as 4,000–6,000. Material losses need the same treatment this canon already gives the Satschan drownings: the figure of 185–186 cannon is a French claim, the one inscribed on the Vendôme column, while the Russian archival returns give 131 and 160 and Britannica and Napoléon &amp; Empire alike put the historians' count at about 130 guns actually taken (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`). The 400 ammunition wagons travel with the French claim and are not independently attested. The 45 standards or colours are confirmed. Any downstream use must preserve these ranges rather than silently pick a single figure, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#measurement-conventions`.

## Napoleon's proclamation and the eve of battle {#austerlitz-proclamation}

**Standing: established by research, wording treated as a reliably attested translation rather than a single fixed original.** Napoleon's proclamation to the army, 1 December 1805, is documented across multiple secondary sources with minor translation variance; this canon treats the following as its standard English form, available for direct use per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#language-conventions`: "Soldiers, I myself will direct all your battalions. I shall keep out of the zone of fire if, with your customary bravery, you carry disorder and confusion into the enemy's ranks. But if, even for a moment, victory is uncertain, you will see your Emperor exposing himself to the first blows, because victory must not be in doubt on this day... Let no one break ranks under the pretext of removing the wounded... This victory will finish our campaign, and we shall be able to go into winter quarters, where we shall be joined by new armies being formed in France. Then the peace which I shall make will be worthy of my people, of you, and of me." Contemporary accounts also describe troops improvising torchlight processions on the eve of the coronation anniversary, and Napoleon reportedly telling aides, "This is the most beautiful evening of my entire life" — this specific remark is less firmly sourced than the proclamation itself and should be treated with the same caution as other reported private remarks per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions`.

## Immediate aftermath: armistice and Pressburg {#austerlitz-aftermath}

**Standing: established by research, with two common conflations corrected.** Francis requested and received an immediate ceasefire; the two emperors met in person on 4 December 1805 — Napoléon &amp; Empire dates the meeting to 3 December, against Britannica and the general record — at the Spálený mlýn, the "Burnt Mill," some 16 km south of Austerlitz near Nasedlovice, talking for roughly ninety minutes under a lime tree (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`). A formal armistice was signed 6 December 1805 at Austerlitz castle, permitting Russian forces free withdrawal from Austrian and hostile territory without a separate Franco-Russian treaty. This canon corrects a common conflation: the "Truce/Armistice of Znaim" is a real historical event but belongs to the War of the Fifth Coalition in 1809, after Wagram, not to the 1805 Austerlitz campaign; no downstream unit may use that name for the 1805 ceasefire. The Treaty of Pressburg, signed 26 December 1805, followed: Austria ceded Venetia, Istria (except Trieste), and Dalmatia to Napoleon's Kingdom of Italy; ceded the Tyrol (including Brixen and Trent) to Bavaria; ceded Vorarlberg and further southern German territory to Bavaria, Württemberg, and Baden; recognized the electors of Bavaria and Württemberg as Kings (Article XX) and released Baden, Bavaria, and Württemberg from remaining feudal ties to the Holy Roman Empire, a direct precursor to the Empire's 1806 dissolution; and paid forty million francs "metallic value." That sum is not a general indemnity, as this canon previously described it, but a specific buy-out: the treaty provides it in redemption of contributions already imposed on the occupied hereditary states and not yet collected. Austria's compensation was Salzburg and Berchtesgaden, incorporated as a duchy. Würzburg was not part of it — that is a second conflation this canon corrects: under Articles XXIX and XXXII Napoleon undertook to obtain Würzburg for Archduke Ferdinand, the displaced Elector of Salzburg, as his own establishment, and no downstream unit may list it among Austria's gains. The Third Coalition collapsed; Russia, though defeated, was not a Pressburg signatory and remained formally at war, but strategically disengaged.

XXIII (the Tyrol including Brixen and Trent to Bavaria, with Swabian, Franconian and Bavarian-circle districts to Bavaria, Württemberg and Baden), Arts.
