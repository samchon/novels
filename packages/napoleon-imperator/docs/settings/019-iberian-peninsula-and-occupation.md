This file owns the long-war conditions in Spain and Portugal.

**Status:** Source-supported setting principle. Iberia's mountain chains, poor or seasonal roads, ports, river crossings, and layered jurisdictions make control local and time-dependent.

A French column can occupy a capital and still fail to secure the next bridge, mill, or courier relay. The setting records the authority at each node: French garrison or prefect, Spanish municipality or junta, Portuguese civil office, British naval or army presence, regular field force, or local armed band. “The road is open” must mean open to whom, at what hour, with what escort, and with what food or draft animals. Rain, heat, dust, snow in passes, and darkness change the interval between a patrol and its support. A port can feed an allied army by sea while the French hold an inland town; a town can pay a requisition while surrounding villages refuse guides. Languages and clerical forms matter because a translated order, parish register, and military map do not carry the same authority. Downstream canon therefore gives operations a road head, road tail, messenger route, and local observer rather than treating distance as a single travel number.

**Status:** Work canon grounded in the source-supported geography. A capital, road, bridge, tax office, and port can have different controllers on the same day.

Every movement entry must identify road head and tail, bridge state, escort, food source, courier route, and the authority whose paper permits passage. Distance is operational: a dry-road column can become a bottleneck after rain, while a port can remain open to one navy and closed to another. A garrison's nominal jurisdiction is not the radius it can patrol, feed, and communicate with before darkness. The army may know that a corps crossed a river without knowing whether its baggage, wounded, or replacement animals have followed. A municipal clerk can preserve a tax record while refusing to provide guides; a local priest can transmit news without being a military agent. This distinction lets later scenes show uncertainty as a physical condition—lost time, missing wagons, false route information, and delayed orders—rather than as atmospheric vagueness.

**Status:** Source-supported and contested. Bayonne transferred a dynastic crisis into a French-imposed monarchy, but public ceremony is not durable legitimacy.

The Fondation chronology places Napoleon's offer to Joseph, Joseph's summons to Bayonne, the settlement, and Joseph's assumption of the Spanish throne on separate dates. The new king required a council, decrees, revenue, guards, translators, and commanders while French troops supplied coercive power. His formal entry into Madrid on 20 July 1808 cannot be staged as a simple popular verdict. Joseph's correspondence preserves his fear that he would be an object of terror and hatred; that is evidence of his perception and position, not a census of every Spaniard. A magistrate may copy a decree to keep a tax office working, a priest may pray for a king while resisting a requisition, and an afrancesado may seek reform or survival without sharing French aims. Each scene must identify which document was received, in what language, by which office, and what force could enforce it. Legitimacy remains a contest among institutions and households.

**Status:** Source-critical canon. “Guerrilla” is an operational label with a history, not a transparent identity category; French records may call resistance brigandage.

The road is an information system before it is a battlefield. A courier can bring a report from a village, but the recipient still has to ask who saw the movement, whether the witness benefits from French protection or insurgent trust, and whether weather or darkness makes the route testable. A patrol may seize a guide, confuse local militia with a band, or reach a hamlet after the armed group has dispersed. The 1809 gendarmerie account ties route policing to this insecurity without proving a centrally commanded insurgency. Reprisal requires a chain: report, classification, officer with authority, prisoners or hostages, transport and guards, record, and a population that remembers the act. A burned store, executed prisoner, confiscated mule, or protected village changes future intelligence. Later scenes may invent a bounded encounter inside this loop, not omniscient coordination or a universal civilian motive.

**Status:** Critical setting with a primary parliamentary witness to its controversy. Allied endurance rests on sea access, Portuguese institutions, engineering, local knowledge, and preserving an army while yielding ground.

British shipping and pay do not erase Portuguese labor, local requisition, or civilian cost. A magazine needs a port or road, guards, animals, clerks, and a political arrangement that permits replenishment. A fortified line is useful only if troops can reach it, civilians can be moved or compelled, and the enemy cannot turn it through an unguarded route. A retreat can destroy stores and displace families while preserving the field army. The Convention of Cintra, concluded 30 August 1808, evacuated French troops; the House of Commons debate on 21 February 1809 condemned its consequences and argued that it released a French army for later service. The debate is a British political source, not an impartial operational report, but it exposes transports, prisoners, terms, and strategic time as material issues. Wellington's army must be an informed opponent with constraints, not a weightless antagonist.

**Status:** Source-supported institutional canon. Royal paperwork, military command, and local reception are separate systems that can contradict one another.

Joseph could issue decrees while field commanders controlled the soldiers and resources that made a decree real. His biography records his uneasy relation with Napoleon and the marshals; that conflict prevents “French policy” from becoming one voice. A decree needs a copying office, a seal, a translator, a receiving official, a revenue mechanism, and an armed subordinate if it is to outlast the ceremony. The paper may be obeyed in Madrid, delayed in a province, or quietly altered by a municipal clerk. Property, church privilege, conscription, and family identity give different households different reasons to comply or resist. The setting labels a royal decision, a military order, a local implementation, and a later report separately; none proves that the regime was accepted.

The chronology must also remain visible in the political canon: the Dos de Mayo rising and repression occurred on 2–3 May 1808; Dupont's force was defeated at Bailén on 19 July; and Junot lost at Vimeiro on 21 August. These are not interchangeable “Spanish resistance” beats. Madrid is an urban crisis under French troops, Bailén is a Spanish field victory and capitulation, and Vimeiro brings a British expedition into the Portuguese theatre. The chronology warns that the Madrid death toll cannot be established securely; later scenes must not turn a contested count into a precise crowd statistic.
