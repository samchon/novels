# Saint Helena

This file gives the exile at Longwood the scene-level depth `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-st-helena` and `#later-death-dispute` summarize but do not carry in full, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`. It does not reopen the cause-of-death historiographical dispute, which stays fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`; it supplies the place, the routine, the relationships, and the medical record around that already-settled dispute. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Longwood as a place {#sthelena-longwood}

**Standing: established by research, with one author's claim that Napoleon deliberately staged the rat infestation flagged as a contested minority reading.** Longwood had been the Deputy-Governor's summer country residence, a farm building belonging to the East India Company, enlarged and refurbished before Napoleon's household moved in 10 December 1815 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`). It was low, small, and poorly built: Lieutenant Herbert Clifford's 1817 diary records "the house at Longwood is low and small... the other rooms which we saw were small," and General Gaspard Gourgaud, seeing his own assigned room on 8 November 1815, called it "a veritable cellar" and initially had to live in a rented Jamestown hotel room, then a tent on the grounds, before it was ready. The house was rat-infested enough that termites later led the French government to consider demolishing it in the 1940s, and only the stone front steps survive from the original fabric; one author, writing for napoleon.org, argues Napoleon deliberately exaggerated the problem for propaganda, leaving food and garbage out so that visiting Britons would corroborate his complaints, and connects this to the arsenic-based rat poison in standard period use as the likely innocent source of the arsenic later found in his hair and wallpaper — this canon records that argument as a named author's contested, minority reading, not as settling either the rats' genuineness or the arsenic question already resolved at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`.

Of the house's roughly 1,000 square meters, Napoleon occupied only about 150: a study and bedroom (with a copper bathtub), a dining room, a library of some 3,500 volumes in mesh-doored cases, a salon — where he ultimately died, on a camp bed — and a vestibule. That vestibule had been fitted as a billiard room, but Napoleon disliked billiards and gave the table to his servants; the room instead became his *cabinet des cartes*, where he spread maps and battle sketches while dictating his campaign memoirs (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-memoirs`), and Clifford's account independently confirms it doubled as his exercise space — "a long billiard room in front of the house, where, it is said, he walks up and down at least three hours of the day." The site itself compounded the house's problems: Longwood sits on a plateau roughly 1,800 feet up, some eight kilometers south-east of Jamestown, permanently exposed to the south-east trade winds and regularly shrouded in fog, a genuine climatic liability distinct from the building's own poor construction.

## The daily regime and the garden {#sthelena-daily-regime}

**Standing: established by research.** Clifford's 1817 account gives the established routine: "He rises early, takes a cup of coffee, breakfasts between twelve and one, à la fourchette, and dines about eight; he spends a great deal of time in his bath... walks up and down at least three hours of the day, and when the weather is fine he extends his walk as far as the garden... He employs the remainder of the day in reading and writing. It is still supposed that he is writing his life; indeed, it is certain, and that he has got through his Italian campaigns." He was, per the same account, "more fond of speaking Italian (which is his native language) than French; he understands English; but cannot pronounce it at all" — a preference corroborated separately by his six recorded interviews with Governor Hudson Lowe, all conducted in Italian. Over the years the routine narrowed: he gradually limited his walks by foot, horse, and carriage, eventually stopped leaving the Longwood estate at all, and in his final years spent most of his time in his bedroom or bathroom rather than his study, though he kept a genuine interest in reading to the end. His weight increased markedly during the exile — a June 1820 sketch depicts him as distinctly "potbellied," and a visitor that October described him as "fat as a Chinese pig" — before reversing sharply in his last months, recorded at `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-medical`. Riding had been an earlier pleasure: he once rode past the outer sentinels (who were punished for allowing it) and ascended the island's true summit, Diana's Peak (2,697 feet, per Clifford), by a route "no one had ever attempted on horseback before," reportedly saying, "Where a man could go, a horse might." By 1817 he had not mounted a horse in a year, confined by the escort restriction recorded at `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`.

From 1819 through late 1820, on his doctors' advice as exercise against his weight, Napoleon took up gardening in earnest — a project contemporaries and later chroniclers describe in real physical detail: two circular windbreak walls some eleven to twelve feet high near his bedroom and library, a small fountain fed by lead pipe, rosebushes on stepped terraces, a fish pond and an aviary (the fish and most of the birds died), transplanted oak trees (only one outlived him), and sunken walking paths built specifically so he could walk unobserved by the sentries watching him. Labor came from several hundred Chinese workers already present on the island in an existing labor community. Count Balmain, the Russian commissioner, recorded seeing him at work on 20 January 1820: "He was amusing himself in one of his private flower gardens... He walks a good part of the afternoon in Longwood garden, accompanied by either Counts Montholon or Bertrand." One contemporary account has him working "spade in hand" from five in the morning, and, when laborers laughed at his plain straw hat, having matching hats made as gifts for them all. This canon notes that the garden's granular engineering detail (wall heights, pipe fittings) rests on tourism-history and enthusiast sourcing this canon could not independently verify against the primary accounts (Balmain, Montholon) those sources cite, and treats those specific figures as plausible but secondary-sourced rather than primary-confirmed; the broader fact of the garden project, its 1819–1820 dating, and its medical motivation are established with stronger corroboration.

Sources: napoleon.org, "A visit to Longwood," as above; Wikipedia, "Napoleon's exile to St. Helena," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_exile_to_St._Helena; sainthelenaisland.info, "Napoleon," https://www.sainthelenaisland.info/napoleon.htm, for the Balmain quotation and the October 1820 "fat as a Chinese pig" observation, this canon's confidence in this tourism-history site's sourcing chain flagged above; napoleon-cologne.fr, "Napoleon Bonaparte in the Garden," https://blog.napoleon-cologne.fr/en/napoleon-bonaparte-in-the-garden, similarly flagged.

## The Lowe relationship {#sthelena-lowe-relationship}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact count of face-to-face meetings disputed between sources and the historians' overall verdict on Lowe genuinely split, including within the Fondation Napoléon's own published authors.** Sir Hudson Lowe, appointed governor and arriving on the island 14 April 1816 (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-st-helena`), met Napoleon face to face either five or six times between April and August 1816 depending on the source; where six meetings are given, they run: 14 April (an initial meeting that went reasonably well); 30 April (Napoleon complaining of his treatment); 16–17 May (Napoleon "bitterly complained" and accused Lowe of intending to assassinate him); 19–20 June (a private meeting with a third party present, which went well); 17 July (a two-hour confrontation in which Napoleon listed his grievances in full — the narrowness of his unescorted range, the requirement that all communication pass through the governor, opened letters, his entourage held to the same restrictions, and Longwood's unhealthy siting — and reproached Lowe for "his meticulous adherence to instructions, unworthy of a general officer," and for "bad faith"); and 18 August (a final, stormy meeting over budget cuts, after which Napoleon reportedly called Lowe a butcher and refused all further face-to-face contact). After that last meeting Lowe told his secretary, Major Gorrequer: "to treat with General Bonaparte one must be either a blind admirer of his faculties or a yielding instrument to work with, a mere slave in thought to him." Napoleon's own first impression of Lowe, reported after their first meeting, was that he was "hideous; he has a most villainous countenance."

The regime's specific frictions are more granular than a general "restrictive governor" characterization: the formal presence requirement was not, as popularly reported, an inspection every six hours, but confirmation of Napoleon's presence morning and evening before a signal was made that "all is well," a requirement satisfied indirectly, by his being seen walking out or by an officer's private arrangement with his servants rather than by a literal audience. His unescorted range around Longwood was tightened over the years — Lowe's own biographical essay gives an original figure of roughly twelve miles, while Clifford's 1817 account gives four miles, beyond which an officer had to accompany him, a restriction that by 1817 had already kept him from riding at all for a full year. Lowe refused to address him as "Emperor" or even "General Bonaparte," writing instead "Napoleon Bonaparte" and dropping the "u" from "Buonaparte"; the dispute over his title remained a live flashpoint to the end of his life, not merely a 1816-era irritant — as late as 13 April 1821, a set of Coxe's *Memoirs of John, Duke of Marlborough* that Napoleon had gifted to the 20th Regiment was returned by Lowe, and the officer who had accepted it, Engelbert Lutyens, was removed from his post at Longwood, because Napoleon's valet had handwritten "Empereur" in one volume. Funding is a genuinely mixed record rather than a one-sided grievance: Lowe had earlier voluntarily raised Napoleon's annual subsistence allowance from £8,000 to £12,000 after reviewing the budget and generally granted table requests — including wines and food not even provided to the British officers' mess — but the August 1816 rupture was specifically over real, later cuts to that same spending, imposed from London.

Contemporary and historical judgment of Lowe is genuinely split, including within napoleon.org's own published authors: one essay there frames him sympathetically, arguing the restrictive regulations came from London (Lord Bathurst's colonial office, confirmed by act of Parliament) rather than from Lowe's own initiative, and that Bathurst himself believed "we could not have found a fitter person of his rank in the army willing to accept a situation of so much confinement, responsibility and exclusion from society"; a different verdict, from the historian Peter Hicks writing within the same institution, holds that Lowe's own mismanagement, dishonest reporting, and poor judgment — particularly regarding the physicians O'Meara and Stokoe (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-medical`) — went beyond simply executing instructions. Wellington himself is quoted, privately, calling Lowe "a stupid man" who lacked "education and judgment," while separately, as Prime Minister, telling Parliament "the King had no person of greater loyalty than Sir Hudson Lowe" — two judgments this canon records without resolving into a single verdict, since a private assessment of competence and a public defense of loyalty are not strictly in contradiction. Lowe left the island 25 July 1821 and was never granted the pension customary for an officer of his rank and decorations; he died in 1844, per one account "impoverished... and was buried without honors."

## The household and its quarrels {#sthelena-household}

**Standing: established by research, with the paternity of Albine de Montholon's 1816 daughter explicitly unresolved and one author's own inference on the point flagged as speculation, not fact.** The household's roster and departure dates, beyond what is already fixed for Las Cases at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-st-helena` and `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-lascases`: Gourgaud left 14 March 1818, after requesting relief in writing on 8 February, and Lowe allowed him to sail directly to England rather than via the Cape, an unusual courtesy; Albine de Montholon left in July 1819 with her children; the surgeon Barry O'Meara was dismissed 25 July 1818 after his relationship with Lowe "degenerated"; the physician John Stokoe, diagnosed with hepatitis and giving a medical opinion Lowe did not want, was court-martialed and dismissed shortly after his own brief tenure, in the same manner as O'Meara; Francesco Antommarchi, sent by Cardinal Fesch and Madame Mère from Rome, arrived 10 September 1819 and remained to the end; Bertrand and Montholon both remained at Longwood without interruption to Napoleon's death.

The household's central rivalry ran between Montholon and Gourgaud, documented in specific, dated incidents rather than only in general terms: a dinner-table argument over seating precedence on 13 December 1815 left, per a napoleon.org account, "bad blood between them ever after"; Gourgaud's own June 1815 promotion to brigadier general and personal aide-de-camp was "a bone of contention... nearly resulting in a duel" with Montholon; and on 20 January 1817 Gourgaud protested at being made to defer to Montholon in rank and precedence at Longwood, telling Napoleon, "In the army I, artillery General, would never have obeyed M. Montholon." Albine de Montholon gave birth to a daughter, Hélène-Napoléone, on 18 June 1816; this canon records, as a documented fact, that "nobody is certain whether the father is Napoleon or Count Montholon," per the same source, and separately flags that same author's own further inference — that if Napoleon was in fact using Albine as a mistress with her husband's knowledge, "it would account for much of the remunerations they received afterwards" — as his own speculation, not an established fact this canon adopts. The historian's own overall judgment of the couple, offered as characterization rather than neutral consensus, is that "the Montholons both appear to be constantly attempting to manipulate everyone around them, including Napoleon." Gourgaud's actual reason for leaving is itself contested among three competing suppositions this canon does not resolve — that he was simply miserable, that he and Napoleon staged an apparent break so Gourgaud could act as an unofficial agent for Napoleon's cause in Europe, or that he deliberately provoked the quarrel with Montholon so Napoleon would be forced to send him away — with the same author's own reading, based on Gourgaud's private diary (unpublished until after his 1852 death), favoring the first: that he had simply had enough of the household's infighting and of Napoleon's habit of ignoring or chiding him. Old resentments outlasted Gourgaud's own 1818 departure: during his final delirious illness, on 9 April 1821, Napoleon "furiously attacked the countess Bertrand calling her a whore, claiming (falsely) that she was Antommarchi's mistress, as she had been Gourgaud's before" — a documented but explicitly false accusation from within the household's own primary record.

Fanny Bertrand's friction with the household is better attested and separate from the Montholon–Gourgaud rivalry: she was, per a napoleon.org factfile, "a very unwilling participant in the exile," refusing to live at Longwood itself and keeping the family roughly a hundred meters away at Hutt's Gate, though "very loyal to Napoleon" despite this. Clifford's 1817 diary independently corroborates her Irish descent, fluent English, and genuine domestic hardship — unwell with a swollen face, an infant's laundry drying by the fire — calling it "a sad change for a woman who had been all her life accustomed to the first circles in France," and separately records an unverified, secondhand claim attributed to her, that an earlier infant of hers had died at Elba, "poisoned by the French physician, who when drunk administered too large a dose of opium." General household morale declined steadily across the years as the household's numbers shrank and Napoleon, per the same source, "became more introverted and melancholy picking fights with mostly Gourgaud and Madame Bertrand," while the Bertrands themselves spent progressively more time away from Longwood — a spiral this canon treats as linking the household's own fraying, Napoleon's declining health and mood, and Lowe's steadily tightening restrictions as a single reinforcing dynamic rather than three separate facts.

Sources: napoleon.org, "General Baron Gaspard Gourgaud: A Survivor of St Helena," as above; napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?", https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/who-was-present-at-napoleons-death/, read via `fetch.sh`; napoleon.org, "A visit to Longwood," as above; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821" (Bertrand's Cahiers timeline), read via `fetch.sh`.

## The dictation of the memoirs {#sthelena-memoirs}

**Standing: established by research, with the literal physical mechanics of the dictation (pacing versus seated, shorthand versus longhand) not located in this research pass.** Beyond the personal, political Mémorial compiled by Las Cases (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-lascases`), Napoleon dictated a substantial, separate body of campaign memoirs to Gourgaud, Bertrand, and Montholon in turn, reconstructing his Italian, Egyptian, and other campaigns from the maps and battle sketches he kept spread out in the converted billiard room (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-longwood`); Clifford's 1817 account already confirms the project's existence and progress to visitors — "it is still supposed that he is writing his life; indeed, it is certain, and that he has got through his Italian campaigns." The scale was substantial and recognized as such at the time: Gourgaud's own collaboration was published as *Memoirs of the History of France, dictated to Gen. Gourgaud* (2 vols., Henry Colburn, London, 1823), and period paintings — one captioned "St Helena 1816 – Napoleon dictating to Count Las Cases the Account of his campaigns," another "l'Empereur à Sainte-Hélène dictant ses mémoires au Général Gourgaud" — show the project was iconographically memorialized as a deliberate act of shaping his own historical legacy from the very years it was underway, not only in retrospect. He continued dictating literally to the end of his life: Bertrand's own notebooks, kept up to the week of the death, record him still dictating his will by hand on 11 April 1821, and on 23 April he told Dr. Arnott, in his own recorded words, "In my best days, I could dictate to four secretaries and give them a great deal of work. I'm a carthorse when it comes to work" — the clearest first-person statement this canon has located of the dictation project's intended scale.

Sources: napoleon.org, "A visit to Longwood," as above; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," as above; napoleon.org bibliography and press listings, read via `fetch.sh`, for the Gourgaud-dictated *Memoirs* and the captioned images of the dictation scenes.

## The medical record {#sthelena-medical}

**Standing: established by research for the physicians, symptoms, and dosing; the cause-of-death dispute itself is not reopened here and stays exactly as fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`.** Barry O'Meara served as personal physician from the start of the exile, diagnosed chronic hepatitis by mid-1817, warned Lowe that Napoleon "could die from the poor climate and lack of exercise," performed the first medical operation on him — extracting a wisdom tooth in the autumn of 1817 — and was dismissed 25 July 1818 once his relationship with Lowe collapsed. John Stokoe, briefly attending in 1818–19, was court-martialed and dismissed "the same fate" as O'Meara after his own diagnosis of hepatitis conflicted with the governor's preferred narrative of Napoleon's health. Francesco Antommarchi arrived 10 September 1819 (his letter of employment dated 19 December 1818), aged twenty-nine or thirty, an anatomist and pathologist by training with, per one account, "no clinical experience" — a selection the same source calls "a poor choice." Napoleon's own recorded assessment of him was blunt: "a blockhead, an ignoramus, a fop, a sneak," after Antommarchi gave him a double dose of tartaric emetic that left him "roll[ing] on the floor in pain" and refusing further treatment from him; Bertrand's own notebooks record Napoleon, on 9 April 1821, harshly upbraiding Antommarchi and threatening to leave him only "a rope to hang himself with" in his will, after which Antommarchi asked to leave the island and was persuaded to stay by Bertrand two days later. Archibald Arnott, surgeon of the 20th Regiment, agreed to attend Napoleon on 31 March 1821 and effectively managed his care through the final five weeks; British medical officers Shortt, Mitchell, and — arriving only 31 March 1821 — Burton were also involved in the final examinations and, with Antommarchi, made Napoleon's death mask.

The symptom record across 1817–1821 is unusually well corroborated across the memoirists this canon otherwise treats with real caution (Montholon, Las Cases, Gourgaud, O'Meara, Antommarchi, Bertrand, Marchand, and the physicians Henry and Stokoe): stomach trouble, pain in the upper stomach, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, hiccup from diaphragm irritation, dysuria, lethargy, fever spikes, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal cramps, weakness, heavy perspiration, and circulatory problems. A single, sustained specific illness is dated by one clinical review to onset around 18 September 1820, though occasional problems recur in the record from as early as the summer of 1817. The weight gain recorded at `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-daily-regime` reversed sharply in the final year: Antommarchi is quoted directly, "he has lost a lot of weight, he is emaciated," and at autopsy found him "less than a quarter of the size he was before" his arrival on the island; Montholon separately wrote, "He is as thin as in 1800 and I look big and fat compared to him."

The final dosing is precisely documented and this canon treats it as a genuine complicating factor within the mainstream gastric-cancer account already fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`, not as evidence for either minority theory recorded there. On 3 May 1821, Lowe personally summoned Shortt and Mitchell, who had not previously been allowed to examine Napoleon directly; they jointly proposed ten grains of calomel "to take the place of the enema and to evacuate the stomach," all British doctors present agreed, and Antommarchi — despite reservations — submitted to the majority decision. The dose was given at 5:30 p.m. with sabayon and wine; by eleven that night, seeing no effect, Arnott proposed a second ten-grain dose at midnight, over Antommarchi's objection, and amid the argument, at 11:30 p.m., Napoleon passed what was read at the time as a hopeful sign — "one of the largest [stools] he had passed in a month." A separate figure for the same single administration gives 64.8 centigrams of calomel, "a dose five times greater than normal." Both figures describe the same event, and this canon's sources independently identify this specific overdose as the proximate, mechanical cause of the fatal hemorrhage that followed — "the accidental killers were the doctors Arnott, Shortt and Mitchell," in one clinical historian's own words — while still attributing the underlying disease to gastric cancer. Napoleon's valet, Louis Marchand, reportedly refused to administer the calomel himself, "knew better when he refused to give the Calomel to him but Dr. Arnott won."

The final days, from Bertrand's own notebooks: on 28 April Napoleon was "no longer himself," delirious, believing he had spoken with Joséphine and was walking in the Longwood garden, repeatedly asking for oranges — Bertrand recorded his own reaction: "Tears kept coming to my eyes as I looked at that man, so awe-inspiring, who had commanded so proudly, so absolutely, beg for a coffee spoon, asking permission, obedient like a child... 'Voilà le grand Napoléon': to be pitied, brought low!" Last rites were administered by the priest Vignali; Bertrand's notebooks date this to 1 May, between 1:30 and 2:30 p.m., while a separately sourced account dates it to 3 May — this canon states the discrepancy rather than resolving it. Fragmentary last words are recorded through 4 May ("Well then, Bertrand," at 10:45 a.m.; "Madame Bertrand, oh!" at 2:30 p.m.) and into the early hours of 5 May ("at the head of the army," between 3:00 and 4:30 a.m., matching one of the several last-words traditions already noted at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`). At six that morning Arnott struck his stomach, found it drum-tight, and announced the end was near; poultices and blisters followed as standard end-of-life intervention. Napoleon died at 5:49 p.m., 5 May 1821, with sixteen people present, twelve of them French — this canon notes explicitly that this deathbed count is a different sixteen from the sixteen witnesses (including seven British medical observers) already fixed for the following day's autopsy at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`, and the two groups should not be conflated.

## The death {#sthelena-death}

**Standing: established by research.** Present at the death, by name: Bertrand and Montholon; Antommarchi; the priest Vignali; Fanny Bertrand and her children Napoléon, Hortense, Henry, and Arthur; the valet Louis Marchand; the valet and librarian Louis-Étienne Saint-Denis ("Ali") and his wife Mary "Betsy" Hall; Jean Abraham Noverraz (nicknamed the "Helvetic bear," himself ill at the time) and his wife Joséphine; the cooks Pierron and Chandelier; the butler Coursot; Dr. Burton; Dr. Arnott; and Captain William Crokat, the orderly officer who carried the news to London and was rewarded with promotion and £500 — a reward pointedly denied to his predecessor Engelbert Lutyens, removed from his post over the Marlborough-memoirs incident recorded at `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`. The body was shaved at 11:30 that night and cleaned and re-linened six hours after death. Montholon, having eased Bertrand out of the closest confidence in the final weeks (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-household`), had helped Napoleon write his will and was its principal named beneficiary among the household; the will's own dictation, on 11 April 1821, excluded Bertrand from the room. Both Bertrand and Montholon returned to France with the household's effects; Bertrand later served as a deputy in 1830 and, in 1840, took part in the mission that brought Napoleon's remains home in the *retour des cendres* (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-retour-des-cendres`) — Gourgaud's own attempt to have Montholon, then imprisoned at Ham with the future Napoleon III, included on the same mission failed, an indirect confirmation that Bertrand's presence on it was the uncontroversial choice by comparison.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?", as above; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," as above; `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-retour-des-cendres`, established by research.
