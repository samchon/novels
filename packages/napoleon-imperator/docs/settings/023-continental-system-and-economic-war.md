# Continental System and Economic War

This file owns the blockade as an administrative, commercial, and coercive system.

## Blockade decree and enforcement gap {#blockade-decree-and-enforcement-gap}

**Status:** Primary-document setting. Napoleon's Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806 prohibited British commerce in the continental territories under French or allied control, but its legal reach depended on administrative capacity.

The decree declares the British islands blockaded, prohibits commerce and correspondence, makes British persons and property subject to seizure, and assigns prize disputes to courts. Its text is a legal command and a political argument about British maritime practice; it is not evidence that every port could inspect every cargo. Enforcement requires customs officials, port registers, patrols, courts, informers, interpreters, and cooperation from allies and occupied states. Smuggling, neutral shipping, forged papers, corrupt officials, scarcity, and diplomatic exceptions make gaps material. A central order can be obeyed publicly and evaded privately. Later scenes identify who profits, who pays, and what a particular frontier can actually enforce.

**Status:** Work canon. Economic war is lived in warehouses, customs houses, ships, credit networks, and household prices rather than in a decree alone.

A merchant facing confiscation may bribe an inspector, reroute cargo, appeal to a consul, purchase a license, or stop trading. A port town can experience unemployment and scarcity alongside official patriotism. Contraband needs a buyer, carrier, route, document, concealment method, and person willing to accept the penalty; a ship's flag does not remove the risk of a patrol or prize court. The setting tracks paperwork, currency, ship space, warehouse storage, weather, and the physical cost of hiding goods. A later scene may invent a merchant transaction within this chain but may not treat smuggling as a disembodied mood.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Allies enforce French economic policy unevenly because their states, merchants, consumers, and treasuries bear different costs.

The same treaty promise can coexist with a customs exemption, a licensed cargo, a local bribe, or a ministry unable to discover the practice. Russia's commercial interests, Spain's war and ports, German state obligations, Italian administration, and Dutch maritime position are not one case. Coercion can produce public compliance while making an ally less reliable. A commercial dispute can become a diplomatic or military crisis through accumulated receipts, protests, tariffs, seizures, and mobilization rather than one dramatic insult. The setting records which government promised what, which office enforced it, which merchant evaded it, and who could punish noncompliance.

**Status:** Work canon. A blockade becomes routine through forms, seals, inspections, warehouses, confiscation records, courts, and paid personnel.

Customs needs classifications for legal cargo, neutral cargo, seized cargo, and perishable goods. Inspectors work under pressure from merchants, superiors, soldiers, bribery, and hunger. A seizure changes a household or company before a distant ministry hears of it, while a ledger may preserve the state's version long after the goods have spoiled or been sold. Later scenes identify who may inspect, who can appeal, where goods are stored, which guard controls the door, and which document records the loss. The routine is coercive precisely because it appears ordinary: a seal, receipt, warehouse key, or court date can decide whether a family eats.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Failure is cumulative and political: revenue loss, smuggling, allied resentment, naval limits, tariffs, and divergent strategy interact over time.

The 1810 Franco-Russian rupture study identifies the blockade's application and the Polish question as two crystallizing disputes. It also ties Napoleon's annexation of the North Sea German coast in December 1810 to his effort to make the customs barrier tighter, while noting that the policy had been a principal justification of the Tilsit alliance. This is critical synthesis, not proof that one decree caused war. A ministry may report compliance while local records show evasion; a ruler may protest commerce while preparing military action; a reprisal may solve one port's problem and deepen another state's hostility. Storyline owners preserve economic, diplomatic, and military chains separately.
