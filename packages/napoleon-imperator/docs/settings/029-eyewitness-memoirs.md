# Eyewitness Memoirs

This file gives its own addressable unit to every eyewitness memoir, diary, or comparable first-person account this canon relies on beyond the sources `014-sources-and-historiography.md` already covers — so that a downstream unit can cite the memoirist directly and inherit an accurate standing, instead of a reliability judgment being restated, or silently varying, wherever the memoir happens to be used. `014-sources-and-historiography.md` remains the template these units follow and is not amended by this file; its own nine surviving source units, including the general reliability criterion set out for Caulaincourt, Ségur, Marbot, Bourrienne, and Las Cases, are unchanged. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions`, a source's standing here binds every other file's use of it.

## Baron Thiébault's memoirs {#source-thiebault}

**Standing: established by research.** Paul-Charles-François-Adrien-Henri Dieudonné Thiébault, a French general who commanded a brigade in Saint-Hilaire's division at Austerlitz, published *The Memoirs of Baron Thiébault* in ten volumes; the English translation this canon reads is by A. J. Butler (London: Smith, Elder, 1896). This canon treats Thiébault as an eyewitness of equal narrative weight to Ségur (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-segur`) for the eve of Austerlitz specifically: both give the pre-dawn exchange between Napoleon and his marshals in vivid, quotable detail, and this canon has found their accounts of that exchange to agree in substance — the intention to use the morning fog tactically — while differing in whose voice carries the direct quotation, Thiébault reporting Soult only in indirect speech. As with any single memoirist, a specific anecdote centering Thiébault's own vantage should be treated as plausible eyewitness color rather than verbatim-certain fact unless independently corroborated.

Sources: *The Memoirs of Baron Thiébault*, trans. A. J. Butler (London: Smith, Elder, 1896), vol. II, pp. 152–54, https://archive.org/details/memoirsofbaronth02thiuoft, read directly.

## Savary's memoirs {#source-savary}

**Standing: established by research, with the choreography of one episode genuinely unsettled between accounts.** Anne-Jean-Marie-René Savary, duc de Rovigo, one of Napoleon's aides and later his police minister, published *Mémoires du duc de Rovigo, pour servir à l'histoire de l'empereur Napoléon*, the edition this canon reads being that of Désiré Lacroix (Paris: Garnier Frères). Savary is a first-person participant in several of the episodes he narrates — the two missions to Olmütz before Austerlitz, and, by his own account, the Tabor Bridge ruse at Vienna — which gives his testimony real evidentiary weight but also means it is not disinterested: this canon has found his account of the Tabor Bridge episode's choreography does not fully agree with the other accounts it has read, and treats the episode's precise sequence as unsettled between sources rather than adopting Savary's version as the single record.

Sources: Savary (duc de Rovigo), *Mémoires… pour servir à l'histoire de l'empereur Napoléon*, ed. Désiré Lacroix, tome II (Paris: Garnier Frères), chs. III–IV, pp. 45–56 and 69–70, https://archive.org/details/mmoiresduducde02sava, read directly.

## Méneval's and Fain's memoirs {#source-meneval}

**Standing: established by research.** Claude-François de Méneval served as Napoleon's private secretary from 1802 to 1813 and published *Memoirs to Serve for the History of Napoleon I*; a fuller English edition, *Memoirs of Napoleon Bonaparte: the Court of the First Empire*, ed. Robert H. Sherard (New York: Appleton, 1910), is the one this canon reads in full for the institutional and working-method dimension of Napoleon's day — the levée, the dictation method, the monthly strength-report ritual, and the "economical at home, magnificent in public" maxim. Baron Fain held the parallel post of premier secrétaire du cabinet and published his own *Mémoires du Baron Fain*. This canon treats both men identically for standing: they are household insiders writing to flatter as well as to record, and this canon accordingly treats their testimony as attesting the habits they describe rather than as fixing those habits' exact scale.

Sources: Claude-François de Méneval, *Memoirs of Napoleon Bonaparte: the Court of the First Empire*, English trans., ed. Robert H. Sherard (New York: Appleton, 1910), vol. 1, archive.org id `memoirsofnapoleo01mnuoft`, read directly; Baron Fain, *Mémoires du Baron Fain, premier secrétaire du cabinet de l'Empereur*, archive.org id `mmoiresdubaronfa00fain`.

## Metternich's memoirs {#source-metternich-memoirs}

**Standing: established by research.** Klemens von Metternich's own memoirs, the English edition this canon reads being *Memoirs of Prince Metternich, 1773-1815*, vol. II (London: Richard Bentley &amp; Son, 1880), read in full directly at archive.org. As a first-person account of his own diplomacy toward Napoleon, it is not disinterested, but this canon has used it specifically to cross-verify claims sourced elsewhere: his 10 August 1809 report on "tacking, and turning, and flattering... till the day of general deliverance" independently confirms, in his own English original, a French-language quotation of the same report carried through a separate secondary account, and his letters to Schwarzenberg of late January and February 1810 document a genuine, not performed, window of uncertainty before the Austrian match's diplomatic pivot.

Sources: *Memoirs of Prince Metternich, 1773-1815*, vol. II (London: Richard Bentley &amp; Son, 1880), full text read directly at archive.org, pp. 171–72, 364–65, 372–74, and 399–415.

## Constant's memoirs {#source-constant}

**Standing: established by research, with this canon's own direct access to the memoirs unconfirmed.** Louis Constant Wairy, Napoleon's valet, published memoirs recording household and personal detail unavailable to more senior figures. This canon's own citation apparatus carries an inconsistency worth stating plainly rather than silently repeating: the Compiègne wedding-night exchange between Napoleon and Cardinal Fesch ("Suis-je marié?") is attributed in body prose to "Constant's memoirs," but the entry's own Sources line names Paul Ganière's 1978 article (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-ganiere`) as the sole source for that same passage. This canon has not independently opened Constant's memoirs to confirm the anecdote there, and treats the Compiègne exchange as reaching this canon through Ganière's synthesis rather than through this canon's own direct reading of Constant.

Sources: attribution via `settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md#marriage-journey-meeting`, itself sourced to Paul Ganière, "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 300 (1978), pp. 7–16, read in full via the fetch helper; Constant's own memoirs not independently opened by this canon.

## General Rapp's memoirs {#source-rapp}

**Standing: established by research, with the memoir's own scope limited on one specific point.** Jean Rapp, one of Napoleon's aides-de-camp, published an English-translated account of his service, *The Memoirs of General Count Rapp, Aide-de-Camp to Napoleon* (London, 1823), read directly. It gives his own first-person account of leading Mamelukes and Guard chasseurs and grenadiers into the Russian Imperial Guard cavalry at Austerlitz, his own sabre wound, Colonel Morland's death, and Prince Repnin's capture. This canon flags its scope precisely: the memoir does not itself name Grand Duke Constantine or mention the loss of a French Eagle in the same action, and is cited here only for the cavalry engagement, the wound, and Repnin's capture, not for the Eagle detail, which rests on other sources at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#constantine-pavlovich`.

Sources: *The Memoirs of General Count Rapp, Aide-de-Camp to Napoleon*, English trans. (London, 1823), https://archive.org/details/memoirsofgeneral00rapp, read directly.

## The Letters of Napoleon to Marie-Louise {#source-letters-marie-louise}

**Standing: established by research.** A published translated collection of Napoleon's private correspondence to his second wife, distinct from the *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier* and the Fondation Napoléon's *Correspondance générale* (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance`). This canon reads it directly for the sustained personal warmth of the wartime letters — "I often think of you... Tout à toi. Nap," 30 June 1812; "it is a sweet habit I have acquired," 19 June 1812 — as primary evidence of the marriage's private register across the 1812–1814 campaigns, distinct from its public, dynastic function.

Sources: *The Letters of Napoleon to Marie-Louise*, trans. Philip Guedalla (Hutchinson &amp; Co., 1935), full text at archive.org, https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.81167/2015.81167.The-Letters-Of-Napoleon-To-Marie-louise_djvu.txt, read directly.

## Chateaubriand's *Mémoires d'outre-tombe* {#source-chateaubriand}

**Standing: established by research.** François-René de Chateaubriand's posthumously published memoirs, cited here for one specific, exactly located passage: his description of Talleyrand and Fouché's joint entrance at Saint-Denis during the Second Restoration of July 1815 — "vice leaning on the arm of crime." This canon uses the passage as evidence of the two ministers' durable public reputation as a pairing, not as an 1805-period document, and it should not be cited as contemporaneous to any scene set before 1815.

Sources: *Mémoires d'outre-tombe*, tome 4, describing the July 1815 Second Restoration scene at Saint-Denis, Wikisource, https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Chateaubriand_-_M%C3%A9moires_d%27outre-tombe_t4.djvu/69, read directly.

## The divorce-era court memoirs: Roederer, Hanoteau, Talleyrand, Hortense, and Cambacérès {#source-divorce-court-memoirs}

**Standing: established by research, each work known to this canon at the single passage quoted below.** Five distinct published memoirs of the imperial court, each cited for one specific dated passage bearing on the 1809–1810 divorce and remarriage: Comte Roederer's *Mémoires sur la Révolution, le Consulat et l'Empire* (1942), for Joséphine's April 1804 remark to Joseph Bonaparte that Napoleon would divorce her once heredity was established; Jean Hanoteau's *Les Beauharnais et l'Empereur* (1936) — a distinct work from Hanoteau's edition of Caulaincourt's memoirs (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-caulaincourt`) — for Joséphine's September 1807 letter to Eugène; the *Mémoires du Prince de Talleyrand*, ed. Emmanuel de Waresquiel (2007), for the Erfurt conversation with Talleyrand; the *Mémoires de la Reine Hortense*, ed. Jean Hanoteau (1927), for the 30 November 1809 collapse and Napoleon's remark to Hortense; and Cambacérès's own *Mémoires inédits… de Cambacérès*, ed. Laurence Chatel de Brancion (Perrin, 1999), for Napoleon's own account of the 1804 coronation ceremony and Cambacérès's contemporaneous legal analysis of the annulment's two grounds. None of the five has been independently opened by this canon beyond the passage a single hosting article on napoleon.org quotes from each; a downstream unit relying on any of the five for a claim beyond the quoted passage should not assume this canon has verified it.

Sources: napoleon.org, "1804-1810: les différentes étapes conduisant à la dissolution du mariage de Napoléon Ier et de l'impératrice Joséphine," read in full via the fetch helper, quoting Comte Roederer, *Mémoires sur la Révolution, le Consulat et l'Empire* (1942), p. 201; Jean Hanoteau, *Les Beauharnais et l'Empereur* (1936), pp. 47–49; *Mémoires du Prince de Talleyrand*, ed. Emmanuel de Waresquiel (2007), pp. 330–31; *Mémoires de la Reine Hortense*, ed. Jean Hanoteau (1927), vol. II, pp. 44–45; Cambacérès's own memoirs, *Mémoires inédits… de Cambacérès*, ed. Laurence Chatel de Brancion (Perrin, 1999), vol. 2, pp. 314–17.

## The 1809 arrest testimonies: Pacca and Radet {#source-arrest-testimonies}

**Standing: established by research, neither text independently opened by this canon.** Two first-person accounts of Pius VII's arrest at the Quirinal Palace on 6 July 1809: Cardinal Bartolomeo Pacca's own memoirs, and General Étienne Radet's two written accounts of the operation he personally commanded, one written 13 July 1809 to the Minister of War and the other 12 September 1814, after the Restoration, to Pius VII himself. This canon has not independently opened either text: both reach this canon only through Othenin d'Haussonville's *Revue des Deux Mondes* reconstruction (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-dhaussonville`), which quotes both directly for the confrontation dialogue and the Pope's own later remarks to Pacca on the excommunication bull, alongside an unnamed Italian manuscript relation by Pius VII's valet de chambre. Pacca's memoirs are also the source, again only through d'Haussonville's quotation, for the two advance-drafted versions of the excommunication bull described at `settings/028-primary-documents-and-official-texts.md#source-quum-memoranda`, and for the Pope's relief at retracting the Fontainebleau concession in 1813.

Sources: Othenin d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat (1800-1814)," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, 2e période, tome 56 (1865), chs. 11, 16, and 21–24, read in full at fr.wikisource.org, quoting Cardinal Pacca's memoirs and General Radet's two written accounts throughout.

## The King of Rome's birth witnesses: Rambuteau, Lavallette, Stendhal, Bellot de Kergorre, and Poumiès de la Siboutie {#source-kingofrome-witnesses}

**Standing: established by research, none of the five independently opened by this canon.** Five distinct period eyewitness accounts of the 20 March 1811 birth of the King of Rome and the cannon-salute count by which Parisians learned the child's sex — twenty-five shots for a princess, one hundred one for a prince — each surviving only as a fragment quoted within a single modern scholarly compilation: Comte de Rambuteau's *Mémoires du Comte de Rambuteau* (Paris: C. Lévy, 1905); Comte de Lavallette's *Mémoires et souvenirs du Comte Lavallette* (Paris, 1905), for both the variant wording of Napoleon's "sauvez la mère" instruction and the salute count; Stendhal's own *Journal*, quoted for "Le canon l'éveille à dix heures... nous comptons le vingt-deuxième avec transport"; Alexandre Bellot de Kergorre's *Journal d'un commissaire des guerres sous le Premier Empire* (1997 ed.); and Dr. Poumiès de la Siboutie's *Souvenirs d'un médecin de Paris* (1910 ed.). None of the five reaches this canon independently of Christophe Beyeler's own compilation, and the variant wordings and shot-counts these five witnesses give each other should be read as the genuine texture of five separate, imperfectly consistent memories rather than resolved into one canonical account.

Sources: Christophe Beyeler, "La naissance du roi de Rome: apogée de l'Empire? Témoignages des contemporains," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 486 (2011), pp. 16–23, reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper, quoting Rambuteau (pp. 55–56), Lavallette (pp. 269–70), Stendhal's *Journal*, Bellot de Kergorre (pp. 42–43), and Poumiès de la Siboutie (p. 95).

## Baron de Bausset's and Mme de Rémusat's accounts {#source-bausset-remusat}

**Standing: established by research, neither independently opened by this canon.** Baron Louis-François-Joseph de Bausset, prefect of the imperial palace, and Claire de Rémusat, lady-in-waiting to Joséphine, each left first-person accounts of the imperial court's daily and seasonal texture; this canon draws on both only as quoted within a single napoleon.org institutional article, for the field logistics of an imperial hunting day and the matched hunting-habit anecdote from Compiègne, March 1810.

Sources: "La Cour Impériale (deuxième partie): Le Grand Veneur," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, quoting Bausset and Mme de Rémusat.

## Chancellor Pasquier's memoirs {#source-pasquier}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through two secondary quotations.** Étienne-Denis Pasquier, prefect of police and later chancellor, published memoirs (*Mémoires*, Plon, 1893) this canon has not independently opened; it reaches him instead through two distinct modern historians' direct quotations — Natalie Petiteau's, for his own record of the boldness of the original plan to exclude commoners' descendants from the new nobility ("c'était une conception bien hardie"), and Jean Tulard's, for his real-time judgment of the 1810–11 credit crisis ("Le commerce de Paris paraît frappé de terreur. L'idée d'une liquidation générale est dans presque toutes les têtes").

Sources: Natalie Petiteau, "Noblesse d'Empire et élites au XIXe siècle: une fusion réussie," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 411 (Feb. 1997), quoting Pasquier's *Mémoires* (Plon, 1893), p. 345; Jean Tulard, "La naissance du Roi de Rome, 20 mars 1811," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 486 (2011), quoting Pasquier directly, both reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper.

## The *Creevey Papers* {#source-creevey}

**Standing: established by research.** The posthumously published diary-letters of Thomas Creevey, a civilian Member of Parliament who spoke with Wellington on the evening of Waterloo itself and recorded the duke's own private assessment of the battle — "a damned nice thing — the nearest run thing you ever saw in your life."

Sources: *Creevey Papers* (1903), quoted at Wikiquote, "Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington," https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st_Duke_of_Wellington.

## Langéron's memoir {#source-langeron}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through secondary quotation.** Louis Alexandre Andrault de Langéron, a French émigré general serving in Russian command at Austerlitz, left a memoir held at the French foreign-ministry archive that this canon has not opened in the original. His eyewitness account of the 1 a.m. Austerlitz war council — Kutuzov already half-asleep when Weyrother arrived to read his elaborate battle plan "in a loud voice and with an air that announced a conviction of his self-importance and our incapacity" — reaches this canon only as quoted in secondary campaign histories.

Sources: quoted in secondary campaign histories per `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#kutuzov` and `#weyrother`; the original memoir, held at the French foreign-ministry archive, not independently opened by this canon.

## Grouchy's self-defense publications {#source-grouchy}

**Standing: established by research, neither work's content independently traced by this canon.** Marshal Emmanuel de Grouchy spent the following decades publishing his own defense of his conduct commanding the detached right wing during the Waterloo campaign: *Observations sur la relation de la campagne de 1815 par le général de Gourgaud* (1818) and the multi-part *Fragments Historiques Relatifs à la Campagne de 1815 et à la Bataille de Waterloo* (1829–1830). This canon has been able to retrieve only the existence and titles of both works, not direct quotation from either, and records his self-defense as a documented fact of his later life rather than as a settled historiographical verdict on whether he was in fact scapegoated.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Emmanuel de Grouchy, marquis de Grouchy," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_de_Grouchy,_marquis_de_Grouchy, read directly for the titles of both works; neither work's own text independently opened by this canon.

## Lieutenant Clifford's 1817 diary {#source-clifford}

**Standing: established by research.** The diary of a British officer, Lieutenant Herbert Clifford, who visited Longwood in 1817 and recorded what he saw and was told there in real time rather than in retrospect — Napoleon's daily routine ("He rises early, takes a cup of coffee..."), the household's physical conditions, Fanny Bertrand's visible hardship ("a sad change for a woman who had been all her life accustomed to the first circles in France"), and the ongoing dictation project ("it is still supposed that he is writing his life; indeed, it is certain..."). This canon reads it directly via a napoleon.org reproduction and treats it as a genuine period eyewitness source, while flagging that one specific claim it records — a secondhand, unverified accusation that an earlier infant of Fanny Bertrand's died at Elba "poisoned by the French physician" — is itself explicitly reported by Clifford as unverified hearsay, not his own direct observation, and this canon does not adopt it as fact.

Sources: napoleon.org, "A visit to Longwood," quoting Lieutenant Herbert Clifford's 1817 diary, read in full via the fetch helper.

## Count Balmain's diary and dispatches {#source-balmain}

**Standing: established by research.** Count Alexander Balmain, the Russian commissioner at Saint Helena, kept a diary and sent dispatches recording his own observations of Napoleon in exile, including the 20 January 1820 entry describing him at work in his private flower garden. This canon reads Balmain only as quoted within a tourism-history secondary source and flags that source's own sourcing chain as not independently verified, while treating the broader fact of the garden project and Balmain's role as an eyewitness to it as established with stronger corroboration than the specific engineering details (wall heights, pipe fittings) that source also carries.

Sources: quoted at sainthelenaisland.info, "Napoleon," https://www.sainthelenaisland.info/napoleon.htm, this canon's confidence in that site's own sourcing chain flagged as lower than a primary or specialist-institutional source.

## Major Gorrequer's diary {#source-gorrequer}

**Standing: established by research, not independently opened by this canon.** The diary of Major Thomas Gorrequer, Governor Hudson Lowe's own secretary at Saint Helena, published as *St Helena during Napoleon's Exile: Gorrequer's Diary*, ed. James Kemble (1969). This canon has not opened that edition and knows Gorrequer's testimony — Lowe's own private remark to him that dealing with Napoleon required being "either a blind admirer of his faculties or a yielding instrument to work with, a mere slave in thought to him" — only through a modern history blog's quotation of it.

Sources: quoted in Shannon Selin, "Hudson Lowe Gets a Bad Rap," https://www.shannonselin.com/2013/12/hudson-lowe-gets-a-bad-rap/, read directly; the original diary, ed. James Kemble (1969), not independently opened by this canon.

## Bertrand's Cahiers {#source-bertrand-cahiers}

**Standing: established by research, read only through a single hosting synthesis rather than a direct edition.** General Henri-Gatien Bertrand kept notebooks throughout the Saint Helena exile, up to the week of Napoleon's death, later published as the *Cahiers de Sainte-Hélène*; this canon has not opened that published edition directly and knows its content — the 11 April 1821 will dictation, Napoleon's 23 April remark to Dr. Arnott that "In my best days, I could dictate to four secretaries and give them a great deal of work," the 9 April accusation against Fanny Bertrand, the delirium of 28 April, and the final-days chronology through the death — only through a single napoleon.org synthesis article. A downstream unit citing Bertrand's Cahiers for any further specific detail beyond what that article quotes should not assume this canon has independently verified it against the published edition.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," read via the fetch helper, drawing on Bertrand's Cahiers throughout; the published edition itself not independently opened by this canon.

## Gourgaud's two works: the 1825 rebuttal and the 1823 dictated memoirs {#source-gourgaud-memoirs}

**Standing: established by research, neither work independently opened by this canon.** Gaspard Gourgaud left two entirely distinct published works this canon must not conflate. The first, discussed for its role in the Ségur duel at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-segur`, is his 1825 critical rebuttal of Ségur's 1812 history, *Napoléon et la Grande Armée en Russie, ou Examen critique de l'ouvrage de M. le comte Ph. de Ségur*; this canon uses it only for one further fact beyond the duel — a night temperature drop to roughly minus 35°C during the retreat that cost Loison's division 12,000 of 15,000 men in three days without a battle — known to this canon only as Wikipedia reports Gourgaud's figure, not from this canon's own reading of the 1825 text. The second, a Saint Helena product entirely separate from the first, is Gourgaud's collaboration on Napoleon's own dictated campaign recollections, published as *Memoirs of the History of France, dictated to Gen. Gourgaud* (2 vols., Henry Colburn, London, 1823); this canon records its existence, scale, and publication facts, but has not located or quoted any passage from its actual text.

Sources: for the 1825 work, Wikipedia, "French invasion of Russia," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia, attributing the minus-35°C figure to Gaspard Gourgaud, *Napoléon et la Grande Armée en Russie* (1825), p. 418, https://archive.org/details/napolonetlagran01gourgoog, located but not opened directly by this canon; for the 1823 work, its existence and publication facts per `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-memoirs`, established by research.

## Montholon's own recorded statements {#source-montholon}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through secondary quotation rather than a specific published edition.** General Charles-Tristan de Montholon was Napoleon's closest attendant in the final Saint Helena years and the will's principal beneficiary; his historically published memoirs are not independently cited or opened by this canon, which instead knows his own words — "He is as thin as in 1800 and I look big and fat compared to him" — only as quoted within unnamed napoleon.org institutional syntheses. This canon's standing on the arsenic-poisoning theory naming him a suspect is fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute` and is not reopened here.

Sources: quoted at napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?" and "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," read via the fetch helper; no specific published Montholon memoir edition independently opened by this canon.

## Louis Marchand's testimony {#source-marchand}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through secondary quotation.** Louis-Joseph Marchand, Napoleon's valet at Saint Helena, is among the memoirists this canon treats with real caution; this canon knows his testimony — his own reported refusal to administer the calomel dose Dr. Arnott ultimately gave — only through a single napoleon.org synthesis, not through an independently opened edition of his own memoirs.

Sources: quoted at napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," read via the fetch helper.

## The physicians O'Meara and Stokoe {#source-omeara-stokoe}

**Standing: established by research, both dismissed under structurally similar circumstances.** Barry O'Meara and John Stokoe were each, in turn, Napoleon's attending physician at Saint Helena, and each was dismissed after his medical judgment put him at odds with Governor Hudson Lowe: O'Meara after warning Lowe that Napoleon "could die from the poor climate and lack of exercise" and performing the first medical procedure on him in 1817, dismissed 25 July 1818; Stokoe after diagnosing hepatitis and giving an opinion Lowe did not want, court-martialed and dismissed shortly after his own brief tenure. This canon knows both men's specific testimony only through secondary napoleon.org syntheses rather than through either physician's own published account independently opened.

Sources: quoted at napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?" and "General Sir Hudson Lowe, KCB: Napoleon's Jailer" (Peter Friedman), read via the fetch helper.

## Antommarchi's testimony {#source-antommarchi}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through secondary quotation.** Francesco Antommarchi, Napoleon's last personal physician at Saint Helena, performed the official autopsy of 6 May 1821 before sixteen witnesses, including seven British medical observers, and is quoted directly — "he has lost a lot of weight, he is emaciated," and at autopsy finding him "less than a quarter of the size he was before" his arrival on the island — but only within a napoleon.org synthesis, not from this canon's own reading of any published Antommarchi memoir.

Sources: quoted at napoleon.org, "Napoleon's last days, March – May 1821," read via the fetch helper.

## The Krasnoi retreat eyewitnesses: Boulart, Bourgogne, Davidov, Clausewitz, and Chambray {#source-krasnoi-witnesses}

**Standing: established by research, none of the five independently opened by this canon.** Five distinct participant-witnesses to the November 1812 fighting at Krasnoi during the retreat from Moscow, each cited only as Wikipedia's own synthesis of their testimony: General Jean-Baptiste Bon Boulart and Sergeant Adrien Bourgogne on the French side; the partisan leader Denis Davidov on the Russian side; and the historians Carl von Clausewitz and Georges de Chambray, both themselves participants writing early campaign analysis rather than later scholarship. Chambray is also quoted directly, at second hand via Alexander Mikaberidze (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-mikaberidze`), for his verdict on the mood after Borodino: "few battles won had produced such an extraordinary effect on the winners; they seemed to be stupefied."

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Krasnoi," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Krasnoi, citing Boulart, Bourgogne, Davidov, Clausewitz, and Chambray, none opened directly by this canon; Chambray also quoted via Alexander Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow: Napoleon's Trial by Fire, 1812* (Pen &amp; Sword, 2014), read directly.

## Jakob Walter's diary {#source-jakob-walter}

**Standing: established by research, known to this canon only through secondary quotation.** *The Diary of a Napoleonic Foot Soldier* (1938 English publication) is the published diary of an ordinary German soldier in the Grande Armée's Russian campaign, a common-soldier's account distinct in kind from the officers' and generals' memoirs this canon otherwise draws on. This canon quotes his eyewitness account of the Smolensk assault only as Wikipedia's own quotation of the diary reports it, not from this canon's own reading of the diary.

Sources: quoted in Wikipedia, "Battle of Smolensk (1812)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Smolensk_(1812), citing Jakob Walter, *The Diary of a Napoleonic Foot Soldier* (1938 English publication), not independently opened by this canon.
