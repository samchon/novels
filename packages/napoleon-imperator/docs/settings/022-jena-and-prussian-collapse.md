# Jena and Prussian Collapse

This file owns the 1806 campaign's strategic and institutional setting; individual battles require dedicated owners.

## Fourth Coalition and Prussian decision {#fourth-coalition-and-prussian-decision}
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#scope-preservation This H2 owns Fourth Coalition diplomacy, Prussian court factions, mobilization, Saxon position, public aims, and private calculations. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#scope-preservation #cc1e606 Compared the Jena itinerary and coalition chronology; diplomacy, mobilization, and battlefield entry remain separate. -->

<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#proportionate-development This H2 separates public declaration, private bargaining, mobilization, officer, minister, court, Saxon, and civic information. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#proportionate-development #0a1be6f Checked each actor and information state against the campaign scope; Prussia is not a monolith. -->

<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked source dates and removed unsupported claims about unanimous public or military intent. -->

**Status:** Source-supported political setting. Prussian entry into the Fourth Coalition emerged from diplomacy, court politics, mobilization, Saxon interests, and fear of French power rather than one battlefield impulse.

The Jena itinerary places the crisis after Pressburg and the French reordering of Germany; a public coalition aim could coexist with private bargaining and an army moving before political purpose was settled. Prussian ministers, court factions, officers, Saxon authorities, and townspeople did not receive identical information or accept identical risks. Mobilization is not war declaration, and a declaration does not prove that every unit or town understood the objective. The setting gives each actor a report route, institutional interest, and time at which the decision becomes actionable. Later French victory language must not retroactively turn the Prussian choice into irrational unanimity.

**Status:** Work canon. French concentration uses corps routes and staff information against a slower coalition, but roads, rivers, detachments, and latency limit what “concentrated” means.

The Grande Armée's apparent omnipresence comes from separated corps converging on assigned roads and reports. A corps commander may know only his objective; a messenger may carry an accurate report that is obsolete after the enemy changes direction; a bridge or baggage tail may keep a planned formation from closing. The setting distinguishes order issued, road head, first contact with another corps, and actual formed mass. A map shows intention, not arrival. This is why a later battle owner must identify which staff document, courier, road, and observer justify a corps placement rather than borrowing the Emperor's retrospective view.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Jena and Auerstedt on 14 October 1806 are simultaneous but distinct fields, commanders, forces, terrain, and information systems.

The Fondation itinerary presents Napoleon and Davout as connected to the double victory, but a setting cannot treat that later synthesis as contemporaneous shared knowledge. A report of success at Jena could coexist with crisis at Auerstedt; headquarters, couriers, maps, and sound did not synchronize instantly. Napoleon's view, Davout's command, a Prussian staff officer's report, and a village witness have different horizons. Later narrative must identify which field the witness occupies, what terrain blocks sight or sound, which message has arrived, and when the result becomes known. The two battles remain linked by date and campaign consequence, not merged into one perspective.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Corps geography is causal: roads, villages, heights, ravines, gun positions, smoke, and line of sight govern action and knowledge.

Maps distinguish an intended route from occupied ground. Infantry, artillery, cavalry, ambulances, and baggage have different speeds and turning radii; a village can anchor a flank, conceal a reserve, delay a train, or become a hospital only when its roads and buildings permit it. A height may show one road while masking another. Smoke can hide a formation without making it invisible to every observer. Later scenes keep a witness's horizon bounded by terrain and the interval between reports, and they state whether a unit is road-bound, deployed, limbered, wounded, or detached.

**Status:** Work canon. Military collapse is an information and institutional process as well as a battlefield result.

After 14 October, headquarters had to learn which roads remained open, which fortresses could hold, and which units still obeyed. The chronology records Frederick William's request for an armistice on 16 October, the day after the double defeat; that political act is a dated response, not proof that every fortress, town, or soldier had already accepted one state of defeat. A courier may carry a true report that is obsolete. Panic, exhaustion, missing horses, broken wagons, surrendered posts, and rumors create a cascade in which authority disappears unevenly. The setting therefore gives units, towns, officials, and fortresses their own date and information rather than saying “Prussia fell” once.

**Status:** Work boundary. Occupation changes taxes, garrisons, courts, requisition, travel, and fortress status without erasing local society.

French authority required orders, interpreters, registries, escorts, stores, and officials who could turn military demands into local action. A capitulation had terms and dates; it was not a universal permission for arbitrary seizure. The 9 July 1807 Franco-Prussian Treaty later specified territorial transfers, peace, and protections for persons and property in articles whose legal promise had to be implemented by administrators and occupying forces. Prussian civilians and officials retained interests and ways to evade or negotiate. Settings track who pays, who supplies, who is punished, who records the transaction, and whether a garrison can enforce a paper beyond its walls.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Defeat, occupation, reform, resentment, accommodation, and later Prussian recovery are related but not interchangeable explanations.

Military humiliation can produce administrative reform, aristocratic resistance, popular resentment, and pragmatic accommodation at the same time. A later reform history may connect Stein, Scharnhorst, civil status, education, and military participation, but that interpretation must not become a single 1806 birth scene for German nationalism. The treaty text supplies legal conditions; contemporary policy and later reform scholarship supply different evidence. Later owners may connect institutions and memory across volumes only after identifying whether a claim comes from policy, participant memory, or retrospective history. The setting keeps reform as a contested response to defeat rather than an inevitable moral conversion.
