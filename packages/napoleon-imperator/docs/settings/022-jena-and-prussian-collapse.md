**Status:** Source-supported. Prussian entry into the war reflects diplomacy, court politics, mobilization, and fear of French power, not a single battlefield impulse.

The coalition's public aims and private calculations differ. A state can mobilize while negotiating, and an army can move before its political purpose is settled. The novel must give Prussian and Saxon actors independent information and avoid treating them as a monolith. Court factions, officers, ministers, and townspeople may want different risks from the same mobilization.

**Status:** Work canon. Napoleon's army uses corps routes and concentration against a slower coalition, but roads, rivers, detachments, and staff latency remain limiting conditions.

The army's apparent omnipresence comes from separated corps converging on information. A corps commander may know only his assigned road and objective. Reports become obsolete as the enemy changes direction. Scenes must distinguish a planned concentration from troops actually closed up, and must show the messengers, maps, bridges, and delays that turn a staff intention into a field formation.

**Status:** Source-required domain. The simultaneous battles at Jena and Auerstedt must remain distinct in terrain, commanders, forces, and initial information.

A report of victory at one field can coexist with crisis at the other. Headquarters, couriers, and maps do not synchronize instantly. The settings layer forbids transferring Napoleon's Jena perspective to Davout's Auerstedt command or treating the later result as present knowledge. The two fields also produce different tactical problems; a later storyline must identify which witness stood where before assigning what was visible or audible.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Corps geography is a causal constraint: roads, villages, heights, ravines, gun positions, and line-of-sight govern what a commander can do.

Maps must distinguish the route a column intends to use from the ground it actually occupies. Infantry, artillery, cavalry, ambulances, and baggage do not share the same speed or turning radius. A village can anchor a flank, conceal a reserve, delay a train, or become a hospital, but only if the setting supplies the relevant roads and buildings. Later scenes should keep a witness's horizon bounded by smoke, terrain, and the interval between reports.

**Status:** Work canon. Military collapse is an information and institutional process as well as a battlefield result.

Once formations retreat, headquarters must learn which roads remain open, which fortresses can hold, and which units still obey. A courier may carry a true report that is already obsolete. Panic, exhaustion, missing horses, broken wagons, and surrendered posts create a cascade in which a state loses control unevenly. The novel must not represent “Prussia” as defeated at one instant when units, towns, and officials experience different dates and pressures.

**Status:** Work boundary. Occupation changes taxes, garrisons, courts, requisition, travel, and the status of fortresses; it does not erase local society.

French authority requires orders, interpreters, registries, escorts, stores, and officials who can turn military demands into local action. Prussian civilians and administrators retain their own interests, memories, and ways to evade or negotiate. A capitulation has terms and dates; it should not be written as a universal permission for arbitrary seizure. Settings must track who pays, who supplies, who is punished, and who records the transaction.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Defeat, French occupation, reform, nationalism, and later Prussian recovery are related but not interchangeable explanations.

Military humiliation can produce administrative reform, aristocratic resistance, popular resentment, or accommodation at the same time. The novel must preserve competing motives and dates instead of assigning a single retrospective birth to German nationalism. Later owners may connect institutions and memory across volumes, but they must identify whether a claim comes from contemporary policy, later memoir, or retrospective history.
