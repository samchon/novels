**Status:** Source-required domain. Revolutionary abolition, colonial enforcement, and restoration of slavery must be dated and sourced separately; no simplified moral arc is permitted.

The sequence is not one moral arc. Sonthonax and Polverel proclaimed abolition at Saint-Domingue on 21 September 1793; the Convention decreed abolition for French colonies on 4 February 1794 (16 Pluviôse an II), but the Fondation's critical study notes that Martinique was under British occupation and that owners resisted in the Indian Ocean. The 20 May 1802 law then used the legal word “maintained” for colonies returned under Amiens and colonies east of the Cape, while later measures and military action extended coercion elsewhere. A decree can promise freedom while plantation, military, and local authorities resist or reinterpret it. Every scene must name whose law applies, who can enforce it, and which people bear the risk.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Plantation profit, coercive labour, military force, and European consumption must be connected rather than abstracted into “colonial policy.”

Sugar, coffee, land, shipping, and credit are material systems built through violence. The critical Haiti dossier calls Saint-Domingue the “perle des Antilles” precisely because intensive plantation production depended on forced labor; the expedition study connects the Caribbean project to wider commercial and geopolitical plans. A legal order restoring or protecting plantation property affects bodies, families, food, movement, and the military units that enforce it. The manuscript must name beneficiaries, coerced people, intermediaries, and risks, and must avoid treating an imperial balance sheet as a neutral measure of success or a labor statistic as a substitute for testimony and survival.
