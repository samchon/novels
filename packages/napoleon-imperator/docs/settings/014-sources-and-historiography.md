# Sources and Historiography

This file records the primary-source base and its known reliability problems, so that every other file's citations carry an accurate, consistent standing rather than treating a propaganda bulletin, a grievance-colored memoir, or a sympathetic modern biography as equally authoritative. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions`, a source's standing here binds every other file's use of it.

## The Correspondance de Napoléon Ier and the Fondation Napoléon correspondence project {#source-correspondance}

**Standing: established by research.** The Correspondance de Napoléon Ier, published 1858–1869 under the Second Empire on Napoleon III's order, comprises a little over 22,000 letters — by the editing project's own reckoning only about a third of what was available. The comparison figure needs stating precisely, because the looser version understates the problem: 65,000 was the number estimated in 1858 for the *public archives alone*, so the true body of known correspondence was larger than that, not "65,000 and a little more." Napoleon III commissioned the edition explicitly to legitimize the Second Empire by linking it to the First; editorial control passed through two commissions — the first (1854–63) chaired by Marshal Vaillant, the second (1863–69) by Prince Napoléon — and the second applied greater, not lesser, selectivity, discarding letters and censoring passages far more systematically than its predecessor, and excluding as a class the letters of Napoleon's youth and everything it judged private. Anticlerical material suffered disproportionately, and the concrete instance this canon records is sharper than the general claim it previously made: of 148 letters *lent to the commission by the baronne Nougarède de Fayet*, daughter of Bigot de Préameneu, Napoleon's Minister of Worship, only 62 were retained. This selectivity was contested at the time: Comte d'Haussonville published suppressed religious correspondence in the *Revue des Deux Mondes* specifically to expose the gaps (1865–69). The modern superseding project, the Fondation Napoléon's Correspondance générale (launched 2002, completed with a fifteenth volume in 2018, fully online since January 2024 at napoleonica.org), identifies over 40,000 letters, of which roughly 58% did not appear in the Second Empire edition at all and roughly 29–30% were entirely unpublished before this project. This canon treats the Correspondance générale as the preferred, more complete source wherever it and the Second Empire edition conflict, and treats the Second Empire edition as established-but-known-incomplete rather than fully authoritative on its own.

Sources: Jacques-Olivier Boudon, "Un monument de papier à la gloire du Premier Empire : l'édition de la correspondance de Napoléon Ier sous le second empire," *Publications de l'École nationale des chartes* (2004), https://doi.org/10.4000/books.enc.677 (also at https://books.openedition.org/enc/677) — open access, opened in full, and the source of every figure above: "un peu plus de 22 000 lettres" published, 65,000 estimated in 1858 for "les seules archives publiques," "seul un tiers des lettres a été retenu," the Vaillant and Prince Napoléon commissions, the second of which "beaucoup plus systématiquement que la précédente commission… écarte des lettres ou en censure certains passages" and set aside "toutes les lettres de jeunesse, celles qui appartiennent selon elle au domaine privé," and "Sur 148 lettres qu'elle avait prêtées, 62 seulement ont été retenues par la commission." This critique is Boudon's, published by the École nationale des chartes; this canon previously described it as the Fondation Napoléon's own dossier, which it is not. For the modern edition, Éditions Fayard, *Correspondance générale*, tome 15 (2 May 2018), https://www.fayard.fr/livre/correspondance-generale-tome-15-9782213706177/ — opened, confirming the fifteenth and final volume and a total of some 40,500 letters published under the Fondation Napoléon's direction. The finer breakdown (40,497 letters; 23,717, or 58.5%, absent from the Second Empire edition; 11,938, or 29.5%, previously unpublished) comes from the Fondation Napoléon's announcement of 3 January 2024 at fondationnapoleon.org; that page returns HTTP 403 to automated retrieval and was read only as an indexed snippet, not opened, and napoleonica.org serves no fetchable text.

## Bulletins de la Grande Armée {#source-bulletins}

**Standing: established by research, with one concrete instance of exaggeration independently verified and the total bulletin count disputed between sources.** Four numbered series tied to Napoleon's major campaigns (1805, 1806–07, 1809, 1812), published through Le Moniteur Universel and distributed as printed sheets read aloud to troops and civilian audiences, with a deliberate triple audience: soldiers, the French domestic public, and foreign courts. This canon's prior total of 186 bulletins is not fully settled: French Wikipedia gives 40+87+30+29 = 186, while The Napoleon Series and napoleon.org's own review of the standard published edition instead converge on 37+87+30+29 = 183 — the 1805 series count (37 or 40) is the specific point of disagreement, and this canon states the range rather than asserting 186 as settled. Propaganda techniques — selective praise of units, favorable framing of Napoleon's personal role, systematic minimization of French losses and inflation of enemy losses and chaos — were recognized by contemporaries themselves, giving rise to the genuine period idiom "mentir comme un bulletin" ("to lie like a Bulletin"), attributed to soldiers of the Grande Armée; its exact first attestation is not pinned down, but the idiom's existence and period currency are corroborated across independent sources. The strongest concretely attested instance of exaggeration is the 30th Bulletin's claim of roughly 20,000 Allied troops drowned at the Satschan pond after Austerlitz, decisively contradicted by physical reconstruction (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-satschan-pond`). This canon withdraws a minority-view claim it previously attributed to the nineteenth-century historian Adolphe Thiers — that the Bulletins were in fact more truthful than other European publications of the era — having found no source in this research pass that attributes any such argument to Thiers. In its place, this canon records a genuine, sourced minority view: J. David Markham, editing and translating a collection of the Bulletins (*Imperial Glory*, Greenhill Books, 2003), argued "much of what was published was reasonably accurate" — a claim napoleon.org's own reviewer explicitly rejects, judging that "Markham goes too far when he tries to defend their veracity." This canon records Markham's claim as a documented, named minority counter-claim with pushback attached, not as the historiographical consensus.

Sources: Wikipédia (fr), "Bulletin de la Grande Armée," https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_de_la_Grande_Arm%C3%A9e — for the 186-count reading (40/87/30/29), the triple audience, and the "mentir comme un Bulletin" idiom; Robert A. Mosher, "Napoleon as Spin-Doctor and Mythmaker: 'To Lie Like a Bulletin…'," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/biographies/napoleon/napoleon-as-spin-doctor-and-mythmaker-to-lie-like-a-bulletin/ — for the competing 183-count reading (37/87/30/29) and corroboration of the soldiers'-idiom origin (citing Herold); napoleon.org, review of J. David Markham (ed., trans.), *Imperial Glory: The Bulletins of Napoleon's Grande Armée*, https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/ed-trans-imperial-glory-the-bulletins-of-napoleons-grande-armee/ — the actual locator for the Markham minority claim and its rebuttal, replacing the unlocated Thiers claim this canon previously carried.

## Le Moniteur Universel {#source-moniteur}

**Standing: established by research, except the date on which it became the official gazette, which is disputed between sources.** Founded 24 November 1789 in Paris by Charles-Joseph Panckoucke as the *Gazette nationale ou Le Moniteur universel*, the principal newspaper of the Revolution — with Maret, later Napoleon's minister, a co-founder, his *Bulletin de l'Assemblée* having been merged into it. It became the official gazette of the French government at the end of December 1799, granted exclusive privilege to publish acts of government and official communications, but the exact day is not settled and this canon does not use the 2 December 1799 date it previously carried: that date appears only in English Wikipedia and corresponds to no known act, while the BnF's own catalogue notice and the *Encyclopædia Universalis* both give 1er nivôse an VIII (27 December 1799) and French Wikipedia gives the *arrêté* of 7 nivôse an VIII (28 December 1799). This canon prefers the French institutional cluster of late December 1799. Under Napoleon the paper functioned as the central propaganda organ of the Empire: he directly inspired its editorial line when he did not write in it in his own hand, and Maret drafted and edited political articles. The further claim that Napoleon personally fixed publication dates and corrected copy is not asserted here, having no source this canon has opened. It is the primary conduit through which the Bulletins reached the French public. It lost its official role at the end of 1868, replaced by the new *Journal officiel* on 1 January 1869, and continued in reduced form until 1901 — or 1902 in its hundred and thirteenth year, as *Universalis* has it.

Sources: Bibliothèque nationale de France, *Catalogue général*, notice for "Gazette nationale ou le Moniteur universel," https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb34452336z (ISSN 1169-2510) — opened; Panckoucke's founding, the run 24 November 1789 – 31 December 1810 under this title continuing as *Le Moniteur universel* from 1811, and the paper becoming the official organ of government on 27 December 1799; "Le Moniteur universel," *Encyclopædia Universalis*, https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/le-moniteur-universel/ — free portion opened, remainder paywalled; gives "le 1er nivôse an VIII (27 décembre 1799)," names Maret alongside Panckoucke at the founding, states that Napoleon "inspirait directement sa rédaction lorsqu'il n'y participait pas de sa main," and dates the paper's disappearance to 1902 in its 113th year; Wikipédia (fr), "Le Moniteur universel," https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Moniteur_universel — opened; the *arrêté* of 7 nivôse an VIII (28 December 1799), the exclusive right to print parliamentary debates and government acts, the loss of official status on 1 January 1869, and cessation on 30 June 1901; Wikipedia, "Le Moniteur Universel," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Moniteur_Universel — opened, and the sole source for the 2 December 1799 date this canon no longer uses. ## Caulaincourt's memoirs {#source-caulaincourt}

**Standing: established by research.** Armand de Caulaincourt, Master of the Horse and Duke of Vicenza, was one of Napoleon's closest confidants and the sole companion in Napoleon's sledge on the flight home from Russia in 1812, to whom Napoleon spoke candidly. Consensus among historians treats his memoirs as the most reliable of the major memoirists, credited by Vincent Cronin with "a high degree of value and authenticity" specifically because they derive from contemporaneous day-to-day notes rather than retrospective reconstruction decades later — the key reliability criterion this canon applies across all memoirists below. Published in three volumes in 1933 (ed. Jean Hanoteau), they are still filtered through personal loyalty and hindsight about the regime's collapse, so conversations he reports Napoleon having should be treated as plausible and widely trusted rather than verbatim-certain, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#language-conventions`.

Sources: Max Sewell, "The Truth About Memoires," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/eyewitness/c_truth.html — opened; the locator for Cronin's judgement of "a high degree of value and authenticity" and for Chandler's description of the memoirs as a vital source. The characterization of the 1933 Hanoteau edition rests on general secondary description rather than on an edition this canon has handled.

## Ségur's history and the Gourgaud duel {#source-segur}

**Standing: established by research.** Philippe-Paul de Ségur's *Histoire de Napoléon et de la Grande Armée pendant l'année 1812* (1824) is literary and dramatized in style; historian John Elting called him "a perpetual romantic, always embroidering his stories." Gaspard Gourgaud, who had also served on Napoleon's staff in 1812, published a page-by-page critical rebuttal, *Examen critique de l'ouvrage de M. le comte P. de Ségur* (Paris, 1824), accusing Ségur of factual inaccuracies and distorted storytelling; the dispute escalated to an actual duel, with Ségur — not Gourgaud — issuing the challenge, and Gourgaud wounding him. Gourgaud himself is not a purely neutral corrective, described by contemporaries as possessing "extreme sensitivity and vanity" and a polemical streak. This canon treats Ségur's account as valuable for narrative color and atmosphere but requiring cross-checking on specifics, and Gourgaud's critique as essentially accurate on facts but personally invested.

Sources: Max Sewell, "The Truth About Memoires," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/eyewitness/c_truth.html — confirming Elting's "a perpetual romantic, always embroidering his stories and seeing many things much larger than life"; Wikipedia, "Gaspard Gourgaud," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard_Gourgaud — confirming the 1824 *Examen critique*, that Ségur (not Gourgaud) issued the duel challenge and Gourgaud wounded him, and the "extreme sensitivity and vanity" characterization; napoleon.org, "GOURGAUD, Gaspard (1783–1852), general, Baron de l'Empire," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/gourgaud-gaspard-1783-1852-general-baron-de-lempire-2/ — independently corroborating the *Examen critique*'s full title (*Napoléon et la Grande Armée en Russie ou examen critique de l'ouvrage de M. le comte Philippe de Ségur*) and the duel's outcome.

## Marbot's memoirs {#source-marbot}

**Standing: established by research, with two attributions corrected against the source this canon cites.** Jean-Baptiste de Marbot's memoirs are vivid, entertaining, and widely read, but notorious among historians for self-aggrandizement and tall tales. Two verdicts this canon previously recorded were misattributed and are corrected here. The phrase "lively but unreliable" belongs to Markham, not to Jean Tulard, who does not appear in the cited discussion of Marbot at all, and Markham applies it to Bourrienne as well. And John Elting's reading is not the forgiving one this canon claimed: he dismisses Marbot's tales as "howling cock-and-bull inventions," the opposite valence to the emollient gloss previously given. Philip Haythornthwaite's specific doubt of Marbot's claimed rescue exploit at Eylau stands. This canon treats Marbot as excellent for period color, tone, and plausible camp and battlefield atmosphere, but treats any specific heroic anecdote centering Marbot himself as disputed or embellished unless independently corroborated elsewhere, as already applied at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-lannes-murat`.

Sources: Max Sewell, "The Truth About Memoires," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/eyewitness/c_truth.html — opened directly for this pass, and the basis of both corrections above: it attributes "lively but unreliable" to Markham and records Elting's dismissal of Marbot as "howling cock-and-bull inventions."

## Bourrienne's memoirs {#source-bourrienne}

**Standing: established by research.** Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne, Napoleon's former schoolmate at Brienne and private secretary until dismissed in October 1802 for a fraudulent commercial dealing with the Coulon brothers involving cavalry-equipment supply — per Napoleon's own letter of 28 October 1802 — wrote his ten-volume *Mémoires sur Napoléon* (1829–31) decades later, colored by the personal grievance of that falling-out. This canon notes that English-language Wikipedia gives only the vaguer "autumn of 1802... financial speculations" for the dismissal, without the Coulon detail or the exact month; the specific October 1802/Coulon-affair facts trace to the French-language article, not the English one, and a citation to "Wikipedia" generically should specify which. Historians widely dismiss the memoirs as unreliable: Cronin calls them outright unreliable, Chandler cites their dubious accuracy; they provoked a substantial contemporary rebuttal, a 720-page counter-work attributed to Comte Boulay de la Meurthe and published in Brussels to escape Bourbon censorship — evidence of how contested they were even at publication — and structurally they invent or reconstruct extensive dialogue, including conversations Bourrienne was not present for. This canon treats them as unreliable for specific quoted dialogue or motive-attribution, especially post-1802 material colored by grievance, but valuable — sometimes uniquely so — for early-life and Brienne/Consulate-period texture no other source records, to be used with corroboration where possible.

Sources: Max Sewell, "The Truth About Memoires," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/eyewitness/c_truth.html — the locator for Cronin's "unreliable," Chandler's "dubious accuracy," and the 720-page contemporary rebuttal attributed to Comte Boulay de la Meurthe, published in Brussels to escape Bourbon censorship; Wikipédia (fr), "Louis Antoine Fauvelet de Bourrienne," https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Antoine_Fauvelet_de_Bourrienne — the actual locator for the October 1802 date and the Coulon-brothers affair, sourced there to Napoleon's own letter of 28 October 1802; this canon notes that the English-language Wikipedia article at the same title does not contain the Coulon detail.

## Las Cases and the Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène {#source-lascases}

**Standing: established by research.** Emmanuel de Las Cases compiled the *Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène* from near-daily conversations with Napoleon during exile from 1815, published 1823 after Napoleon's death. This canon treats it as essentially Napoleon's self-authored legacy narrative, filtered through a devoted secretary, not an independent reliable memoir: sources describe it becoming the foundational text of the "Napoleonic legend." The standard scholarly judgement is John Holland Rose's, in the 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica*, and this canon gives it in Rose's actual wording rather than the paraphrase that circulates: the *Mémorial* "should be read with great caution, as the compiler did not scruple to insert his own thoughts and to colour the expressions of his master," and "in some cases he misstated facts and even fabricated documents." The familiar "did not hesitate… color" form, which this canon previously quoted, is a later paraphrase of Rose, not an independent source. Cronin found the work "by no means free of propaganda." The manuscript history matters and this canon corrects its own earlier account of it: Las Cases was arrested on 25 November 1816 for smuggling correspondence to Europe, his manuscript seized, and he was expelled about a month later — but the original was returned to him after Napoleon's death in 1821. What the British kept was a copy made before restitution, and it is that copy, descending in the Bathurst papers and identified at the British Library in 2004, which underlies the fuller version published in 2017. That is why the Fondation's own announcement describes the publication of *a copy of* the original manuscript. The classic 1823 text remains once removed from the original notes, but by Las Cases's own reworking rather than by permanent loss of the original. This canon treats the Mémorial as primary evidence for "what Napoleon wanted posterity to believe about himself" — the Napoleonic legend as a historical phenomenon — never as reliable factual testimony about earlier events in his life; any specific self-serving claim in it must be labeled disputed or self-mythologizing unless independently corroborated elsewhere.

## Modern scholarship and its divergences {#source-modern-scholarship}

**Standing: established by research.** David Chandler (*The Campaigns of Napoleon*, 1966) is the classic military-operational historian, a Sandhurst instructor whose reputation rests on this single monumental campaign narrative; he treats Napoleon as a supremely gifted improviser and practitioner and is generally sympathetic to Napoleon's generalship specifically, while staying narrower in scope than moral or political judgment of the man — this canon treats his operational-tactical detail as close to established fact wherever uncontradicted by the other historians named here. Andrew Roberts (*Napoleon: A Life* / *Napoleon the Great*, 2014) is explicitly and self-consciously sympathetic and rehabilitative, foregrounding energy, charisma, and administrative and legal achievement, and was the first major biography to draw on the newly completed Fondation Napoléon correspondence — strong on primary-source breadth, but its interpretive stance skews favorable and should be flagged as such wherever it diverges from Esdaile. Charles Esdaile (*Napoleon's Wars* and other works) is the leading critical and revisionist counterweight — reviewing Esdaile's *Napoleon's Wars: An International History, 1803–1815* (Allen Lane, 2007) for the *Literary Review*, Andrew Roberts wrote that "another subtitle might have been: 'Napoleon – The Case for the Prosecution'," a tighter and more accurate rendering than the loose paraphrase this canon previously carried — foregrounding the pan-European human cost over operational brilliance and explicitly skeptical of the "Napoleon-as-genius" framing. Michael Broers (three-volume biography, *Soldier of Destiny*, *The Spirit of the Age*, *The Decline and Fall of an Empire*) is a major, exhaustively researched, generally comprehensive and balanced narrative rather than a polemical one in either direction; whether its final volume extends narrative coverage through the Saint Helena years is itself disputed between reviewers — a magazine review describes the trilogy as covering "the withering epilogue of St Helena," while a separate detailed review states the narrative "basically ends... at the second abdication" with only "a brief concluding epilogue of just five pages" for the Saint Helena years, and this canon does not resolve the two readings. Dominic Lieven (*Russia Against Napoleon*, 2009) is the key Russian-archival corrective to Franco-centric historiography, written explicitly to counter the dominance of Western narratives of 1812–1814 using previously unused Russian archives, and winner of the Wolfson Prize for History and the Prix Napoléon. Alexander Mikaberidze (*The Napoleonic Wars: A Global History*, 2020; *The Burning of Moscow*) draws on Russian, Georgian, and other non-Western-European archival and linguistic sources, functioning as a global and Eastern-Front corrective analogous to Lieven's. Where Roberts and Esdaile give materially different characterizations of the same event, this canon labels the fact disputed and cites both; where Lieven and Mikaberidze diverge from older Franco-centric accounts on Russian-side numbers, decisions, or intent, this canon prefers them as the more archivally current correction.

## Archives and access {#source-archives}

**Standing: established by research.** Gallica (gallica.bnf.fr), the Bibliothèque nationale de France's digital library, holds digitized copies of the Second Empire Correspondance and numerous other period correspondence collections, plus Caulaincourt's memoirs in full scan. archive.org hosts full-text or scanned editions of Caulaincourt's memoirs, Bourrienne's memoirs, Las Cases's Mémorial, and works by Esdaile and Lieven. The Fondation Napoléon (napoleon.org / fondationnapoleon.org) is the leading French scholarly institution, running napoleonica.org (the Correspondance générale) and publishing critical historiographical dossiers, though the "monument de papier" critique of the Second Empire edition cited at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance` is not one of them: that is Boudon's, published by the École nationale des chartes, and this canon previously credited it to the Fondation in error — this canon treats it as authoritative and fairly balanced, not a Napoleon-boosting fan site. The Napoleon Series (napoleon-series.org) is a large, volunteer-scholar-edited resource sponsored by the International Napoleonic Society, editorially vetted, good for synthesis articles aggregating named historians' verdicts and detailed order-of-battle and casualty breakdowns; this canon treats it as a solid secondary and tertiary aggregator, not itself a primary source, but reliable for pointing to which named historian said what.

Sources: Bibliothèque nationale de France, "Gallica"; Fondation Napoléon, "Our Partners"; napoleon-series.org.
