# Foreign Crowns and Commanders

This file gives figure-level units for the principal non-French heads of state and commanders the work relies on, and relation-level units among them, per `principles/settings.md#figures-and-relations`. Most entries below belong to the Third Coalition of 1805; campaign-day deployment for them is at `settings/012-austerlitz.md` and diplomatic formation of that coalition is at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md`. Later entries — Blücher, Wellington, and Hudson Lowe — belong to the collapse of 1812–1821 and are cross-referenced to `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md` through `settings/020-saint-helena.md` in turn.

## Tsar Alexander I of Russia {#alexander-i}

**Standing: established by research, with the precise staging of his tearful departure from the battlefield flagged as secondary-sourced.** Born 23 December 1777, St. Petersburg (died 1 December 1825, Taganrog); Emperor since March 1801; aged 27 at Austerlitz. Raised by his grandmother Catherine the Great specifically to bypass his erratic father Paul I, tutored by the Swiss Enlightenment philosopher Frédéric-César de La Harpe in liberty and rational government, genuinely absorbing these ideals even while raised to inherit an absolute autocracy. He took the throne after the March 1801 palace conspiracy that murdered his father; scholarly consensus holds he almost certainly knew a plot to force abdication was underway and gave at least tacit assent to that, but did not consent to and was reportedly horrified by its escalation into murder — he carried guilt about his father's death for the rest of his life, and this canon treats that guilt as a permanent undercurrent in any scene involving him, never stated outright by him. Married at 16 to Princess Louise of Baden (Empress Elizabeth Alekseyevna); both their children died in infancy, and by 1805 the marriage is nominal — Elizabeth conducting a long affair with Alexander's own close advisor Prince Adam Czartoryski, while Alexander has been with Maria Naryshkina since 1799. Contemporaries and historians describe him consistently as a study in contradictions: charming, handsome, outwardly warm, fundamentally evasive and performative — Vyazemsky's epithet, "the Sphinx, who remained an enigma to the grave," and Chateaubriand's "a strong soul and a feeble character" both stuck. He had poor eyesight from birth and forbade courtiers from wearing eyeglasses in his presence; he was deaf in his left ear from youth and, because of it, chronically suspected aides were mocking him — a genuine paranoid streak beneath the cultivated public charm. Went into the 1805 campaign with zero personal experience of combat command yet cast himself, per contemporary report, as "the new St. George of Europe crushing the dragon," and personally overrode Kutuzov's caution in favor of the aggressive plan urged by his own young favorites, chiefly Prince Pyotr Dolgorukov. On the day, Alexander personally ordered Kutuzov's corps off the Pratzen Heights — the decisive error Napoleon's deception was built to produce — and was led from the field in tears as the center collapsed. He blamed Kutuzov rather than himself afterward; the specific further claim that he had Kutuzov falsify the casualty figures could not be traced to a citable source in this canon's research and is not treated as established — a downstream unit may use the blame-shifting but not the falsification claim. Napoleon later called him a "true Byzantine" and the "Northern Talma," meaning a gifted political performer whose sincerity could not be trusted.

Sources: Britannica, "Alexander I, Emperor of Russia," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-I-emperor-of-Russia; Gregory Afinogenov, "Field Maneuvers," *New York Review of Books*, 19 October 2023, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2023/10/19/field-maneuvers-mikhail-kutuzov/, reviewing Alexander Mikaberidze, *Kutuzov: A Life in War and Peace* (Oxford University Press, 2022), for the Alexander–Kutuzov command relationship and the post-battle blame; historyofwar.org, "Battle of Austerlitz, 2 December 1805," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_austerlitz.html, for the Pratzen exchange in which Alexander overrode Kutuzov. The "true Byzantine" and "Northern Talma" epithets are widely reproduced but this canon has not traced either to a primary document; the standing reference for Alexander's life is Marie-Pierre Rey, *Alexander I: The Tsar Who Defeated Napoleon* (Northern Illinois University Press, 2012), which this canon names rather than cites, not having obtained it.

## Emperor Francis II (Holy Roman Empire) / Francis I (Austria) {#francis-ii-i}

**Standing: established by research, with the sealing-wax anecdote and Napoleon's quoted "blockhead" verdict flagged as popular-history sourcing rather than documented fact.** Born 12 February 1768, Florence (died 2 March 1835, Vienna); aged 37 at Austerlitz. From a cadet Habsburg-Lorraine line, sent to Vienna in 1784 by his childless uncle Joseph II, who judged him harshly as backward in bodily dexterity and imagination while crediting his application and sense of duty. Became Holy Roman Emperor in March 1792 at 24 after his father Leopold II's sudden death. On 11 August 1804, in direct reaction to Napoleon's own imperial proclamation, Francis proclaimed himself hereditary Emperor of Austria as Francis I — a title created to claim parity of rank and to hedge against the possible collapse of the older, largely ceremonial Holy Roman title. From August 1804 to August 1806 he holds both titles simultaneously, the first and only "double emperor" in European history; at Austerlitz he is both at once, and this canon uses "Francis" without insisting on one number where the distinction is not load-bearing to a given scene, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#naming-conventions`. Sources split usefully on his personality: popular nineteenth-century accounts paint a folksy, curmudgeonly "beloved Kaiser Franz," while modern critical assessment (habsburger.net) calls him cold, calculating, and lacking in emotion; his own foreign minister Metternich credited him with "immovable strength of character" and dispassionate judgment. He dressed plainly, disliked pomp, and was a genuinely skilled hobbyist — making sealing wax and toffee, gardening with custom tools — while running an absolutist, censorship-heavy state shaped by fear of revolutionary contagion. Napoleon's own verdict, frequently quoted, calls him "a child governed by his ministers, a weak and false prince, and a good and religious man, but a blockhead occupied only with botany and gardening." At the 1 December 1805 war council he was the cautious voice, seconded by Kutuzov, overruled by the eager Tsar; no eyewitness account of his specific activity during the battle itself has been located, and this canon leaves his exact battlefield position open rather than inventing one. He acted decisively afterward, offering Napoleon an armistice on 3 December and meeting him personally on 4 December near Nasedlovice, producing the armistice that led to the Treaty of Pressburg (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-aftermath`). His daughter Marie-Louise, aged 13 at Austerlitz (turning 14 ten days after the battle), fled to Hungary with the imperial family as French forces occupied Vienna and reportedly called Napoleon the "Antichrist"; she marries him five years later (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage`), an irony this canon records as fact but does not schedule as a scene.

Sources: [napoleon.org — "Francis II/I"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/francis-ii-i/); [Wikipedia — Francis II, Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor); habsburger.net (for the personality assessment); the sealing-wax anecdote and the exact 4 December remark remain sourced only to a popular-history blog that itself flags the anecdote as circulating gossip, and are treated accordingly as disputed/popular-history sourcing rather than documented fact.

## General Mikhail Illarionovich Kutuzov {#kutuzov}

**Standing: established by research, with the precise motive behind his sleeping through Weyrother's briefing flagged as unresolvable from the sources.** Born 16 September 1745, St. Petersburg (died April/May 1813, Bunzlau, Silesia, during the pursuit of Napoleon); aged 60 at Austerlitz. Service nobility; graduated the noble artillery and engineering school at fourteen; combat against Poland and the Ottomans from his twenties; a protégé of Suvorov. Spent 1776 leave studying at the University of Strasbourg, genuinely cultivated and fluent in French, unusually well-read for a soldier of his generation — a register this canon makes available beneath his coarser public persona. At the Battle of Alushta (Crimea) in 1774, aged 26, a musket ball struck near his left temple and exited near his right eye, causing catastrophic local damage he improbably survived, left with the eye permanently disfigured and lasting pain and dizziness. He took a second, separate head wound on 18 August 1788 at the siege of Ochakov — not, as popular summaries have it, a repeat of the same injury: the surgeon Massot, who treated him, recorded a ball passing from his left cheek through the head and out at the occiput, destroying part of his jaw on the way, and wrote that fate must intend Kutuzov for something great, since by every rule of medicine two such wounds should have killed him. He survived that one as well. Popularly, if imprecisely, called "one-eyed" by contemporaries; Napoleon dubbed him "the old fox of the North." Pointedly sidelined after Alexander I's 1801 accession, recalled to field command only for the 1805 campaign. Personality sources converge: outwardly indolent and self-indulgent — he rose late, ate to excess, slept roughly three hours after dining and needed two more to "come to his senses" — inwardly shrewd; Suvorov's verdict, "Clever, clever, cunning, cunning… No one can deceive him," and the compressed historian's epithet "Lazy, gluttonous, cunning, brave, pious, courtly, and coarse" both apply. At the 1 December war council, recorded by the French émigré general Langéron, who was present, Kutuzov argued for retreat, was overruled, and was already half-asleep when Weyrother arrived to read his elaborate plan and fully asleep by the time the briefing ended — whether from genuine exhaustion or as a form of silent protest cannot be resolved from the sources, and this canon leaves the ambiguity open rather than deciding it. At Austerlitz he took a minor wound, a graze to the cheek, and his aide-de-camp and son-in-law Count Ferdinand von Tiesenhausen was killed near him; he suppressed his grief in the field. Never warm with Alexander even before the battle, and blamed rather than credited afterward, he was relegated to provincial governorships for roughly seven years until public pressure forced his 1812 recall — the relation's later repair is recorded at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#relation-alexander-kutuzov`. Appointed to overall command against the French invasion in August 1812 under exactly the political pressure Alexander had resisted for seven years, he continued Barclay de Tolly's underlying strategy of avoiding decisive battle while satisfying the court's demand for a stand at Borodino (7 September 1812), which he fought and then, that same evening, reported to St. Petersburg as an outright victory before the fighting had even fully ended — a report Alexander himself had edited to remove any reference to the following retreat before its public release (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-aftermath`). He overrode a war council split roughly evenly at Fili on 13 September and ordered Moscow abandoned rather than risk the army defending it, reasoning that "having preserved our army, the hopes of our Fatherland would persevere, and the war, our only means to salvation, would be continued on better terms" — the same underlying calculation, attrition over a single decisive engagement, that had governed his conduct since the recall. He continued to command through the retreat and the pursuit into Poland, and died in April or May 1813 at Bunzlau in Silesia, during the pursuit of Napoleon's beaten army out of Russian territory (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`).

Sources: on both head wounds, their dates and their distinct anatomy, and Massot's contemporary report, "Two bullets to the head and an early winter: fate permits Kutuzov to defeat Napoleon at Moscow," *Neurosurgical Focus* 39(1):E3 (2015), https://thejns.org/focus/view/journals/neurosurg-focus/39/1/article-pE3.xml (PubMed 26126402) — this corrects the "wounded near the same spot again in 1787" formulation this canon previously carried, which is wrong in both year and anatomy; Gregory Afinogenov, "Field Maneuvers," *New York Review of Books*, 19 October 2023, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2023/10/19/field-maneuvers-mikhail-kutuzov/, reviewing Alexander Mikaberidze, *Kutuzov: A Life in War and Peace* (Oxford University Press, 2022); the Langéron war-council account is Louis Alexandre Andrault de Langéron's own memoir, held at the French foreign-ministry archive, which this canon knows only as quoted in secondary campaign histories and does not claim to have read in the original; Wikipedia, "Ferdinand von Tiesenhausen," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_von_Tiesenhausen. For the 1812 material: Alexander Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow: Napoleon's Trial by Fire, 1812* (Pen & Sword, 2014), archive.org id `nap-the-burning-of-moscow-napoleons-trial-by-fire-1812`, read directly; `settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-aftermath` and `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-retreat-decision`, each established by research.

## General Franz von Weyrother {#weyrother}

**Standing: established by research.** Born Vienna, 1755 (died Vienna, February 1806, aged approximately 50–51). Son of the director of the Spanish Riding School; trained at the Ingenieurakademie, entered the army in 1775 under the patronage of Field Marshal Lacy, earned real early distinction in the Rhine campaigns of 1794–96. His subsequent career is a case study in confident, meticulous staff planning producing repeated catastrophe: he drafted the flawed Austrian dispositions for Rivoli (1797) and for Hohenlinden (1800), and finally for Austerlitz. Contemporaries were harsh — Langéron himself judged him a man with "all the faults of an upstart" — yet he was a skilled self-promoter who made Major General by 1805. After the death of the previous Austrian chief of staff, Kutuzov himself requested Weyrother as replacement, and the two disagreed almost immediately, Kutuzov favoring defense and Weyrother pushing the aggressive attack Alexander ultimately backed over both Kutuzov's and Francis's objections. At the 1 a.m. war council he read his five-column plan "in a loud voice and with an air that announced a conviction of his self-importance and our incapacity," per Langéron. The plan performed exactly as Napoleon had gambled; some Russians afterward accused Weyrother of being in French pay, an accusation with no supporting evidence, reflecting the depth of the humiliation rather than actual treachery. He withdrew from active service and died within three months.

Sources: napoleon.org, "WEYROTHER, Franz von," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/weyrother-franz-von/, citing Ian Castle / Robert Goetz, *1805: Austerlitz* (Greenhill, 2005), for the outline of his career; this source states his death followed "of shame" a few months after the battle, which no medical or other independent record corroborates, and this canon does not adopt that framing as documented cause of death. Wikipedia, "Franz von Weyrother," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_von_Weyrother, for the fuller career detail: family background (son of Anton von Weyrother, Chief Rider of the Spanish Riding School), training at the Ingenieurakademie, entry into the army under Lacy's patronage on 1 December 1775, distinction in the Rhine campaign of 1794–96 (the Mainz actions of November–December 1794, earning the Military Order of Maria Theresia on 11 May 1796), the flawed Rivoli (1797) and Hohenlinden (1800) plans, Kutuzov's request for him as chief of staff after Johann von Schmitt's death at Dürnstein (11 November 1805), and the Langéron "faults of an upstart" quotation.

## Prince Pyotr Bagration {#bagration}

**Standing: established by research.** Commanded the Allied right and advance guard at Austerlitz, engaging Lannes and Murat in the northern sector around the Santon hill; his wing held reasonably well through the day but could not affect the collapse of the Allied center. Also commanded the rearguard that bore the brunt of the November 1805 Hollabrunn/Schöngrabern action after Murat's unauthorized armistice was broken (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-murat`), a fighting withdrawal that bought the time Kutuzov needed to preserve the main Russian army.

## Friedrich Wilhelm von Buxhowden {#buxhowden}

**Standing: established by research, with his exact birth day disputed by one day between two sources — a Julian/Gregorian calendar artifact, not a substantive conflict.** Born 13 or 14 September 1750, Muhu/Mohn, Governorate of Riga (died 23 August 1811 Old Style / 4 September 1811 New Style — the same day under different calendars, not a discrepancy — at his estate of Lohde, Estonia). Baltic German nobility tracing to Bexhövede in Lower Saxony; entered Russian service in 1764, saw action in the Russo-Turkish War of 1768–74. His 1777 marriage to Countess Natalia Alexeyeva — reputed, though disputed among historians, to be an illegitimate daughter of Catherine the Great — did more for his career than his generalship: sources converge that his advancement owed more to court connections than to battlefield skill. Colonel by 1783, major general by 1789, he fought Sweden in 1788–90 and Poland under Suvorov in 1792–94; Catherine made him governor of Poland, Paul I made him military governor of St. Petersburg and a count (1797) before an abrupt fall from favor and discharge that same year. Alexander I recalled him in 1802 as governor-general of the Baltic provinces (Courland, Livonia, Estonia), restoring his general's rank in 1803. In the 1805 campaign he commanded the Russian 2nd Corps, forming the Allied left wing at Austerlitz — under Weyrother's plan, the main southern attacking force (columns 1–3 under Dokhturov, Langeron, and Przybyszewski, `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-allied`) tasked with sweeping the French right through Telnitz and Sokolnitz. As Soult and Davout turned south against him in the early afternoon after the French center's success, his wing was cut off and driven onto the Satschan and Menitz ponds (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-satschan-pond`); multiple sources describe him as "scarcely in possession of his wits" during the resulting retreat, one of the sharpest personal-conduct verdicts recorded against any Allied commander that day. He went on to command the Russian 2nd Army in the 1806–07 Polish campaign, clashing bitterly with his rival Bennigsen and being passed over for supreme command after Pultusk, commanded in Finland in 1808–09 as both military commander and civil administrator of the newly annexed territory, and retired shortly after, dying two and a half years later.

## Grand Duke Constantine Pavlovich {#constantine-pavlovich}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 8 May 1779 (27 April Old Style), Tsarskoye Selo (died 27 June 1831, Vitebsk, of cholera, while commanding the suppression of the November Uprising); Alexander I's next-younger brother, aged 26 at Austerlitz. Second son of Paul I and Sophie Dorothea of Württemberg; like Alexander, tutored from 1783 by Frédéric-César de La Harpe, but where the elder brother absorbed the Enlightenment liberalism, contemporaries describe Constantine as physically and temperamentally the child who most resembled their unstable father — impulsive, headstrong, prone to violent temper, and by multiple accounts brutal to his first wife, Julie of Saxe-Coburg. He first saw combat under Suvorov in Italy and Switzerland in 1799, losing at Bassignana but earning a reputation for personal physical bravery at Novi. At Austerlitz he commanded the Russian Imperial Guard reserve. Around 1 p.m., in the last serious Allied bid to retake the Pratzen, he led the Guard cavalry — reinforced by Guard horse artillery in an unusually effective combined-arms strike — against Vandamme's division atop Staré Vinohrady: the attack broke a battalion of the French 4th Line Regiment in the open and the Horse Guards rode down its eagle-bearer, taking the Eagle — the only French standard lost in the entire battle — while the adjacent 24th Light Regiment was put to flight. Napoleon answered by committing five squadrons of his own Imperial Guard cavalry under Bessières; the two Guards fought hand to hand atop the heights, Napoleon's aide-de-camp Rapp led the counter-charge that broke the Russian Guard in turn, and Prince Nikolai Repnin-Volkonsky was taken prisoner (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#general-rapp`). The Eagle was never recovered. Constantine shared in the blame for the day's defeat afterward, but the capture stood as the Russian army's only tangible trophy of the battle.

## Prince Pyotr Petrovich Dolgorukov {#dolgorukov}

**Standing: established by research for birth/death dates, family, and career; cross-referencing `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-diplomatic-feint` (itself established by research from primary correspondence and Savary's memoir) for the Austerlitz-campaign material; a specific "Montmartre cannon fire" remark sometimes attributed to Napoleon during their meeting is flagged as unverified.** Born 19 December 1777; died 8 December 1806, St. Petersburg, of typhoid fever contracted after a hard winter ride from the southern army, aged 28. Second son of infantry general Prince Pyotr Petrovich Dolgorukov (1744–1815) and Princess Anastasia Simonovna Lapteva; entered the Izmailovsky Life Guards as a child per the era's noble custom, rose steadily under Paul I — colonel by 1797, major general and commandant of Smolensk by 1798, adjutant general to the Emperor at 21 — and was drawn close as a personal favorite once Alexander I took the throne in 1801, serving him on diplomatic missions to Berlin and Stockholm in 1802–05. Young, confident, and — per Savary, who dealt with him directly — very much of the Allied war party, certain the Russian army could beat Napoleon outright. When Napoleon, through two missions by his aide-de-camp Savary, sought a personal meeting with Alexander in late November 1805, Alexander declined to go himself and sent Dolgorukov in his place; Napoleon met him at the outposts on 29 November, riding out from a straw bivouac and walking the high road alone with him, deliberately performing weakness and hesitation. Writing to the Elector of Württemberg on 5 December, Napoleon states this was calculated: Dolgorukov "must have taken my extreme moderation for a mark of great terror, which is what I wanted from the military point of view, and which gave rise to the battle of Austerlitz" (`settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-diplomatic-feint`). Dolgorukov reported the French position weak and beatable, reinforcing the Allied war party's push for immediate attack over Kutuzov's counsel of continued withdrawal — the direct precondition for the Allied decision to fight on 2 December. He served afterward under Bagration at Austerlitz itself and, per the one available account of his conduct there, fought with personal courage; Russian officers and nobility subsequently gave him much of the blame for the defeat because of his role in the outpost meeting. He continued in active diplomatic and military service through 1806, was recalled from an inspection of the southern army after Prussia's defeat at Jena–Auerstädt, and died of sudden illness within days of a forced return ride to the capital.

## Frederick William III of Prussia and the Hardenberg–Haugwitz split {#prussia-frederick-william}

**Standing: established by research.** Frederick William III kept Prussia neutral through most of 1805 despite pressure from Britain, Russia, and Austria alike, though by late November this formal neutrality was closer to a legal fiction than a real policy choice: Danish legation dispatches from Berlin record the King and Queen as privately committed to entering the war, with mobilization already under way, and only the speed of Austerlitz — followed by Austria's collapse — prevented actual Prussian entry as a fourth coalition partner. Two rival voices at his court: Foreign Minister Christian von Haugwitz counseled caution and continued mediation, while Karl August von Hardenberg was willing to consider war but only in alliance with France, since Napoleon's price for ceding Hanover to Prussia was Franco-Prussian alignment; absent that, Hardenberg preferred armed neutrality backed by the implicit threat of mobilization. The decisive break came only after Bernadotte's I Corps marched through neutral Prussian Ansbach en route to the Danube (3 October 1805, `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-bernadotte`), outraging Frederick William III and producing the Treaty of Potsdam with Alexander I (3 November 1805) — but this arrived too late to affect Ulm and only days before Austerlitz. Haugwitz was dispatched with Prussia's ultimatum to Napoleon but delayed presenting it until after the battle, then congratulated Napoleon on his victory instead and negotiated the Treaty of Schönbrunn (15 December 1805) at Vienna, with Duroc (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#duroc`) as Napoleon's counterpart — Prussia gaining Hanover but ceding Ansbach to Bavaria, Neuchâtel to Berthier, and Cleves (combined with Berg into a new grand duchy for Murat) to France, a major diplomatic reversal caused directly by the speed of the French victory.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Prussia — the Third Coalition and the Battle of Austerlitz," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/prussia-the-third-coalition-and-the-battle-of-austerlitz/ (Eric Lerdrup-Bourgois, drawing on Danish legation dispatches from Berlin and on T. Stamm-Kuhlmann's and B. Simms's Hardenberg scholarship), for the neutrality-as-legal-fiction reading, the Potsdam ultimatum and its one-month deadline, and Haugwitz's mission; Wikipedia, "Karl August von Hardenberg," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_August_von_Hardenberg, and "Christian von Haugwitz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_von_Haugwitz, for the two ministers' rival positions and Haugwitz's delay; Wikipedia, "Treaty of Schönbrunn (1805)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Sch%C3%B6nbrunn_(1805), for the treaty's specific territorial terms, the Vienna signing, and Duroc as negotiator.

## William Pitt the Younger and Britain {#pitt-britain}

**Standing: established by research, with the "roll up that map" remark flagged as attributed tradition rather than a verified direct quotation.** British Prime Minister and the diplomatic architect of the Third Coalition from London, financing Russian, Austrian, and Swedish participation through subsidy. On receiving news of Austerlitz in January 1806 he is widely reported to have said, gesturing at a map of Europe, "Roll up that map; it will not be wanted these ten years" — a remark sourced to Earl Stanhope's biographical *Life of the Right Honourable William Pitt* and classified by Wikiquote as "Attributed" rather than documented. Pitt died 23 January 1806, roughly seven weeks after Austerlitz; the timing and the coalition's collapse were a severe blow to his final weeks in office, but no direct medical or causal link between the news of defeat and his death is established, and the popular framing that he "died of a broken heart over Austerlitz" is legend this canon does not adopt as fact.

Sources: Wikiquote, "William Pitt the Younger"; Earl Stanhope, *Life of the Right Honourable William Pitt*, vol. 4 (1862), digitized at [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/lifeofpitt0004earl) — this canon provides the volume as a locator for direct verification but has not itself confirmed the exact passage against a readable page, and the remark is treated as attributed tradition, not a verified direct quotation, accordingly.

## Relation: Alexander I and Kutuzov {#relation-alexander-kutuzov}

**Standing: established by research.** Never warm even before Austerlitz: Alexander appointed Kutuzov to the 1805 command while simultaneously insisting on making major decisions personally, a built-in structural humiliation for the older, more experienced general. **The Tsar's side:** genuine hunger for personal martial glory to offset the private guilt of his father's death, receptivity to younger, more flattering advisors over Kutuzov's caution, and — after the defeat — a need to displace blame that leads him to hold the experienced general responsible for not having stopped the Tsar's own decision to fight. **Kutuzov's side:** professional judgment overruled by a sovereign he cannot openly defy at court and survive, channeled into passive resistance (arguing for retreat, then visibly disengaging during Weyrother's briefing) rather than open confrontation, and, after the defeat, quiet endurance of a demotion dressed as reassignment. The relationship's eventual repair comes only in 1812, when public and noble pressure forces Alexander, against his own inclination, to recall the man he had sidelined for seven years — outside this canon's primary window but available as a documented later beat.

Sources: Gregory Afinogenov, "Field Maneuvers," *New York Review of Books*, 19 October 2023, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2023/10/19/field-maneuvers-mikhail-kutuzov/, reviewing Alexander Mikaberidze, *Kutuzov: A Life in War and Peace* (Oxford University Press, 2022), for the structural command humiliation, the Tsar's receptivity to his young favourites over Kutuzov's caution, and the post-defeat displacement of blame; `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#alexander-i` and `#kutuzov` above, each established by research.

## Field Marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher {#blucher}

**Standing: established by research, with the "elephant" episode's exact origin — a genuine 1814 breakdown, an idiomatic joke to Wellington in 1815, or both fused in later retelling — not settled between sources.** Born 16 December 1742, Rostock (died 12 September 1819, Krieblowitz, aged seventy-six). Nicknamed "Marschall Vorwärts" ("Marshal Forward") for a tireless, relentlessly aggressive tactical instinct his soldiers openly appreciated; aged seventy-two at Waterloo, and Wikipedia's own article flags that "one source mistakenly calls him 74," a documented instance of the kind of small sourcing error this canon notes rather than silently repeats. He wants direct, immediate contact with the enemy and refuses caution he reads as squeamishness, in himself or in his allies; he can lead a cavalry charge personally at seventy-two — at Ligny his horse was shot and fell on him, and he lay trapped and repeatedly ridden over by cavalry for hours before his aide-de-camp, Count Nostitz, concealed his identity with a greatcoat and got him clear (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-ligny`) — but he cannot, on his own, restrain that same instinct when it runs ahead of what his army can actually do, which is why Gneisenau's more cautious judgment mattered as a check on him rather than merely as a subordinate's opinion. His hatred of Napoleon and of the French was personal and, at least once, more radical than his allies': after the 1815 abdication, Wellington reportedly wrote that "Blücher wishes to kill him [Napoleon]; but I have told him that I shall remonstrate," having already resolved that if the sovereigns wanted Napoleon executed, someone other than Wellington himself would have to do it — a position distinct from, though consistent with, Blücher's separately attested 1814 desire to punish Paris more severely than his fellow commanders allowed. A documented mental breakdown followed the strain of the 1814 campaign, after the Battle of Laon in March, during which he lost his sight for a time and suffered the delusion, per one biographer, "that a Frenchman had impregnated him with an elephant" — this canon records the episode as genuine but flags that a separate tradition holds the "elephant" phrasing itself originated later, in 1815, when Blücher told Wellington in Paris that the French were giving him a headache using a German idiom ("pregnant with an elephant by" someone, meaning a splitting headache) that the English took literally; this canon does not resolve whether the two traditions describe one event or two, and a downstream unit should not present the literal-delusion reading as the only one available.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gebhard_Leberecht_von_Bl%C3%BCcher, which attributes the Laon breakdown account there to Simon Sebag Montefiore (2016) — this canon has not opened that work directly and repeats the account as Wikipedia's synthesis; "Battle of Ligny," as above; a history blog, "Situation Excellent," and material linked via the Waterloo Association's "Age of Revolution" outreach site, for the idiomatic-origin counter-reading of the elephant episode, neither independently verified against Philip Henry, 5th Earl Stanhope's *Notes of Conversations with the Duke of Wellington*, which this canon has not opened directly; a widely circulated letter from Wellington to Charles Stuart, quoted via secondary discussion this canon has not traced to an archival original, for the "wishes to kill him" quotation.

## Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington {#wellington}

**Standing: established by research, with the recipient and date of the "infamous army" remark not independently confirmed in this research pass.** Born 1 May 1769, Dublin (died 14 September 1852, Walmer, Kent). Rose through India (Seringapatam, 1799; Assaye, 1803 — a battle he reportedly later called greater than Waterloo itself) and the Peninsular War, where Talavera (1809) earned him his field marshal's baton (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-peninsular-war`); a lifetime of Indian and Peninsular command left him, per the military historian Richard Holmes, with "a strong sense of discipline through drill and order" and a mastery of "the vital necessity of a secure supply line" that shaped his consistent preference for a defended, carefully chosen position over an improvised one — the same reverse-slope discipline that governed his choice of ground at Waterloo (`settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-terrain`). He wants a battle fought on ground he has already inspected and refuses to be drawn into one he has not; he can hold an army together under sustained pressure and refuses to credit good luck for a win he considers to have been earned, calling Waterloo, to the civilian MP Thomas Creevey on the evening of the battle itself, "a damned nice thing — the nearest run thing you ever saw in your life" — "nice" here meaning uncertain and delicately balanced, in its period sense, not the casual modern one. He privately rated the multinational army under his own command with equal bluntness: "an infamous army, very weak and ill-equipped, and a very inexperienced Staff," a remark this canon has not independently confirmed against a specific dated letter in this research pass, though its wording is solidly attested. After 1815 he commanded the Allied occupation forces in France, served as ambassador to Paris, and was Prime Minister from 1828 to 1830, overseeing Catholic emancipation before retiring from active politics in 1846.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st_Duke_of_Wellington; "Battle of Waterloo," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo; Wikiquote, "Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington," for the "nearest run thing" quotation, sourced there to the posthumously published *Creevey Papers* (1903).

## Sir Hudson Lowe {#hudson-lowe}

**Standing: established by research, with historians genuinely split on how much of his reputation he deserves — including within the Fondation Napoléon's own published authors — per `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`.** Born 28 July 1769, County Galway, Ireland (died 10 January 1844, Chelsea, London, of paralysis). His prior career is a genuinely distinguished thirty-five-year record this canon does not let the "petty jailer" caricature obscure: an ensign at twelve, he formed and commanded the Corsican Rangers from 1793, present at the Siege of Toulon the same year Napoleon first rose to prominence there; commanded the Rangers in Egypt in 1800–01, fighting French forces including troops under Napoleon's own command; captured Capri in 1806 and surrendered it in 1808 against three-to-one odds; served in 1813 as liaison officer with Blücher's Russian-German Legion through thirteen battles including Leipzig; personally carried the news of Napoleon's first abdication to London in 1814, was knighted and decorated by both the Tsar and Blücher, and served as Quartermaster-General under Wellington in the Netherlands before his appointment as Napoleon's custodian on 1 August 1815. He wants his instructions from London followed to the letter and refuses to be maneuvered, by Napoleon or by his own subordinates, into an appearance of leniency he has not been authorized to grant; he can run a rigid, procedurally exact administration and cannot read when that same rigidity is producing exactly the martyrdom narrative it is meant to prevent. He believes, with real documentary support, that the severity of the regulations he enforces originated in London rather than in his own initiative — Lord Bathurst's own words hold that "we could not have found a fitter person of his rank in the army willing to accept a situation of so much confinement, responsibility and exclusion from society" — but he does not register, and this canon does not have him register, that his own reporting and his handling of the physicians O'Meara and Stokoe went beyond what those instructions required, a criticism a historian within the Fondation Napoléon's own circle makes explicitly. He is owed, by his own conviction, a pension and a public defense of his record that never fully came; he died impoverished and, per one account, was "buried without honors."

Sources: napoleon.org, "General Sir Hudson Lowe, KCB: Napoleon's Jailer" (Peter Friedman), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/general-sir-hudson-lowe-kcb-napoleons-jailer/, read via `fetch.sh`; Shannon Selin, "Hudson Lowe Gets a Bad Rap," https://www.shannonselin.com/2013/12/hudson-lowe-gets-a-bad-rap/; `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`, established by research.

## Relation: Napoleon and Lowe {#relation-napoleon-lowe}

**Standing: established by research.** Neither side of this relationship wants what the other can give. Napoleon wants to be addressed and treated as a sovereign whose fall was a change of fortune, not a change of category, and Lowe's refusal to write "Emperor" or even "General Bonaparte" — insisting instead on "Napoleon Bonaparte," the "u" of "Buonaparte" deliberately dropped — reads to Napoleon as a small, constant, deliberate insult precisely because it costs Lowe nothing to grant and he grants it anyway. Lowe wants exact compliance with instructions he did not write and cannot alter, and reads every one of Napoleon's calculated performances of grievance — Napoleon later privately admitted to Bertrand that he had baited Lowe about the loss of Capri specifically to make himself sympathetic — as confirmation that leniency would be exploited rather than appreciated, which becomes a self-fulfilling account on both sides: each man's settled expectation of the other produces exactly the behavior that expectation predicted. Both sides are right about something true of the other and wrong about how much it explains; this canon establishes no scene in which either man persuades the other of anything, across six years and six recorded meetings.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#hudson-lowe` above and `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`, each established by research; the characterization itself is this canon's own reading of that record.
