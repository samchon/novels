# Later Campaigns, Exile, and Death

This file gives fuller canon for the campaigns and final years that `settings/002-chronology.md` dates but does not expand, so that a life-and-age biography has scene-level support across its full span even where the current storyline commitment (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`) treats these years as summarized rather than dramatized. Since this file was first written, the collapse period of 1812–1821 has been given its own dedicated files at the same scene-level standard `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md` set for 1805 — `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md` through `settings/020-saint-helena.md` — and the entries below on the Russian campaign, the Hundred Days and Waterloo, and Saint Helena are accordingly trimmed to short connective pointers into that fuller canon rather than repeating it. The Peninsular War, the Fifth Coalition, Leipzig, the first abdication and Elba, the death's historiographical dispute, and the retour des cendres remain at their original depth here, since no later file has taken them over.

## Peninsular War {#later-peninsular-war}

**Standing: established by research.** At Bayonne (5–7 May 1808), Napoleon coerced Spanish King Charles IV and his son Ferdinand VII (who had just forced Charles's abdication in the Tumult of Aranjuez, 17–19 March 1808) into successively renouncing the Spanish throne, transferring it to his brother Joseph Bonaparte, proclaimed King of Spain 7 July 1808 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-peninsular-begins`; `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-joseph`). Historian Miguel Artola calls the resulting power vacuum "the first Napoleonic error." The Dos de Mayo uprising (2 May 1808), a spontaneous Madrid rebellion against French occupation brutally suppressed, ignited a national uprising once the Bayonne abdications became known. Napoleon himself coined the phrase describing the resulting drawn-out conflict as a "Spanish ulcer." Historian Charles Esdaile puts French deaths in the war at approaching a quarter of a million, with total French casualties (killed, wounded, captured) cited around 500,000 across the war; French occupation forces tied down fluctuated between roughly 100,000 and 370,000 men (peak August 1810) — troops permanently unavailable for the German, Austrian, and Russian theaters. Esdaile's revisionist scholarship also complicates the romantic "guerrilla" narrative, noting many irregular bands were as much banditry as patriotism, and that some guerrilla formations grew large enough to fight pitched battles. Wellington's conventional campaigns (Talavera 1809, Salamanca 1812, Vitoria 1813) combined with the guerrilla drain to make Iberia unwinnable for France.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Abdications of Bayonne"; napoleon.org, "Bleeding ulcer: the commencement and long-term consequences of guerilla warfare in Iberia"; Charles Esdaile, *The Peninsular War* (2001).

## War of the Fifth Coalition {#later-fifth-coalition}

**Standing: established by research, with Wagram's strengths disputed between sources and this canon's earlier figures withdrawn as unlocatable.** At the Battle of Aspern-Essling (21–22 May 1809), Napoleon's attempted forced crossing of the Danube near Vienna was repelled by Austrian forces under Archduke Charles; French losses ran to roughly 20,000–26,000, and Marshal Jean Lannes was mortally wounded (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-lannes`). Whether it counts as an outright defeat is itself contested — it is commonly called Napoleon's first personal battlefield reverse in a decade, but Napoléon &amp; Empire records that historians dispute classing it a defeat at all, and it appears among the victories inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe.

The Battle of Wagram (5–6 July 1809) was fought on the Marchfeld plain northeast of Vienna, and this canon withdraws the figures it previously carried. "Roughly 154,000 French/allied against roughly 158,000 Austrians" is supported by no source this canon has opened, and its implication of rough parity with an Austrian edge is the reverse of the case: historyofwar.org gives 147,852 French with 404 guns on the morning of 5 July rising to about 171,900, against 135,766 Austrians with 388 guns, and Gill's figures via Wikipedia give 171,939 against 136,000. Napoléon &amp; Empire is the low outlier at 131,000 against 139,000. The battle was therefore won by a substantial French numerical superiority and by weight of massed artillery, which is what makes it a costly and imperfect but decisive victory rather than a narrow one. Casualties were close to symmetrical, roughly 35,000 French against roughly 39,000 Austrian.

The Treaty of Schönbrunn — signed at Vienna on 14 October 1809 by Champagny and Prince Johann of Liechtenstein, and ratified by Napoleon at Schönbrunn the next day, which is why it also appears as the Treaty of Vienna — ended the Fifth Coalition. Austria ceded roughly 83,000 km² and 3.5 million subjects. This canon states the cessions as the treaty states them rather than by their eventual outcome: Salzburg, Berchtesgaden, and part of Upper Austria went to *France*, and reached Bavaria only afterwards; the Illyrian provinces (Goritia, Monfalcone, Trieste, Carniola, Fiume, the Hungarian Littorale, and Austrian Istria) went to France; West Galicia with a district round Kraków on the right bank of the Vistula went to the *King of Saxony, to be united to Warsaw*; and a tract of eastern Galicia containing 400,000 souls went to Russia. Carinthia, which this canon previously listed among the Illyrian cessions, is not named in the articles it has read and is not asserted. The printed articles carry no indemnity and no limit on the size of the Austrian army, both of which are commonly attached to this treaty. Note also that "Treaty of Schönbrunn" names two different instruments in this canon: this one, and the Prussian treaty of 15 December 1805 at `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-effect-prussia-britain`. No downstream unit may use the name without its year.

Sources: historyofwar.org, "Battle of Aspern-Essling, 21–22 May 1809," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_aspern_essling.html (French losses probably around 20,000; Lannes's wounding); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of Aspern-Essling," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/aspern-essling.php (roughly 6,000 killed and 20,000 wounded or prisoners, about 45 per cent of those engaged, and the note that historians dispute classing the action a defeat); historyofwar.org, "Battle of Wagram, 5–6 July 1809," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_wagram.html (147,852 French with 404 guns on 5 July rising to ~171,900; 135,766 Austrians with 388 guns); Wikipedia, "Battle of Wagram," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wagram, citing John H. Gill, *1809: Thunder on the Danube* (2010), pp. 391 and 401, for 171,939 French and allied against 136,000 Austrians — Gill's own text is not opened by this canon; Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of Wagram," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/wagram.php, for the dissenting low pair of 131,000 against 139,000; Wikisource, *Treaty of Schönbrunn* (titled there *Treaty of Vienna, 1809*), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Sch%C3%B6nbrunn — the treaty text, read directly and cited by article: Art. III §1 (Salzburg, Berchtesgaden, and part of Upper Austria to France), §2 (the Illyrian cessions to France), §4 (West Galicia and the Kraków district on the right bank of the Vistula to the King of Saxony, to be united to Warsaw), §5 (a tract in eastern Galicia containing 400,000 souls to Russia).

## Russian campaign of 1812 {#later-russia-1812}

**Standing: established by research, with force size explicitly disputed among named historians for a stated reason.** The historiographical corrective this entry originally recorded — that Lieven's and Mikaberidze's Russian-archival scholarship credits the campaign's outcome to Barclay de Tolly's deliberate scorched-earth strategy and Alexander I's political resolve rather than to "General Winter" — still governs how this canon treats the campaign, and this entry's standing is unchanged. Force size remains disputed exactly as this canon originally stated: roughly 450,000 for the narrowly counted first-wave crossing force, 600,000–685,000 across the full multinational theater, with roughly two-thirds of the invading force non-French.

## War of the Sixth Coalition and Leipzig {#later-leipzig}

**Standing: established by research, with total strengths disputed between sources and given as ranges; one previously carried figure is withdrawn as unlocatable.** The Battle of Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations" (16–19 October 1813, `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-1813-1814`), was the largest battle of the Napoleonic Wars. Both armies grew across the four days, so a single pair of numbers misrepresents it: French and allied strength ran from roughly 177,000 with about 700 guns on 16–17 October to roughly 190,000–195,000 with about 900 guns by 18–19 October, while Coalition strength ran from roughly 257,000 with about 1,145 guns to roughly 320,000–360,000 with about 1,500 guns. The Coalition figure of 394,000 this canon previously recorded has no locator in any source it has read and is withdrawn; so is the French figure of 191,500. Over 500,000 combatants in total and more than 100,000 casualties both hold, French losses running 60,000–79,000 including 30,000–36,000 captured and several thousand Saxon defections, against an official allied return of 53,804. The defeat destroyed French power in Germany and Poland and precipitated the 1814 invasion of France; Coalition forces entered Paris in spring 1814. It was the first decisive defeat of an army under Napoleon's own command in a general set-piece battle — a claim this canon states in that narrower form deliberately, since Aspern-Essling four years earlier is itself commonly called his first personal battlefield reverse (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-fifth-coalition`), and the two statements would contradict each other if either were left unqualified. Napoleon abdicated 11 April 1814 (Treaty of Fontainebleau) and was exiled to Elba; the Bourbon monarchy was restored under Louis XVIII. The German campaign that led to Leipzig — Lützen, Bautzen, the Armistice of Pläswitz, and the costly Dresden/Kulm/Katzbach cluster — and the retreat's own Elster bridge disaster are now recorded at scene level at `settings/017-the-collapse-and-first-abdication.md`, which does not repeat the strength and casualty figures already fixed here.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Leipzig," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig, whose infobox gives French strength of 177,000 with 700 guns on 16–17 October rising to 195,000 with 900 guns on 18–19 October, and Coalition strength of 257,000 with 1,145 guns rising to 359,000 with 1,500 guns, the Coalition figures cited to Bogdanovich (1863), which this canon has not opened; Britannica, "Battle of Leipzig," https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Leipzig (~185,000 French against ~320,000 Coalition; more than 300,000 allied on 18 October; allied losses 55,000); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of Leipzig," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/leipzig.php (190,000 against 330,000, falling to roughly 175,000 against 300,000 by 18 October); historyofwar.org, "Battle of Leipzig," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_leipzig.html (allies around 300,000; French losses around 70,000 including some 30,000 prisoners; allied losses around 54,000), which dates the battle 16–18 rather than 16–19 October. The World History Encyclopedia figure of "470,000+" Coalition troops is not used: it adds Bennigsen's and Bernadotte's forces to a total that already contains them.

## First abdication and Elba {#later-first-abdication-elba}

**Standing: established by research, with the size of the Elba force corrected and the payment of the pension not yet decided.** Under the Treaty of Fontainebleau, signed 11 April 1814 by Caulaincourt, Macdonald, and Ney against Metternich, Nesselrode, and Hardenberg, and ratified by Napoleon on the 13th, Elba was to "form, during his life, a separate principality, which shall be possessed by him in all sovereignty and property" (Art. III); Parma, Piacenza, and Guastalla went to Marie-Louise (Art. V); 2,500,000 francs a year were settled on his family, distinct from his own revenue (Art. VI); and he kept an armed corvette as his property (Art. XVI). Article XVII allowed him "400 men, volunteers, officers, subofficers, and soldiers" as his guard — and this canon corrects its own earlier reading of that number: 400 is the treaty's ceiling, not a count of the men on the island. Cambronne reached Elba on 26 May 1814 with 675 grenadiers and 54 Polish light horse, carried in English ships, and by February 1815 the Elban force stood at roughly 1,100–1,150 including a Corsican battalion. Napoleon left Elba with more men than the treaty had ever permitted him to keep. Whether the annual revenue was actually paid this canon does not settle: the grant is in the treaty, but no source it has read establishes the Bourbon government's non-payment, so the claim that it was "never fully paid" is withdrawn rather than repeated. The campaign of France that preceded the abdication — La Rothière, the Six Days' Campaign, Arcis-sur-Aube, the fall of Paris, the marshals' revolt, the conditional and unconditional abdications, the suicide attempt, and the farewell to the Old Guard — is now recorded at scene level at `settings/017-the-collapse-and-first-abdication.md#collapse-fontainebleau-abdication` and the adjoining entries.

He was watched by Austrian, French, and British observers — notably Colonel Sir Neil Campbell, the British commissioner, gazetted colonel in June 1814 and knighted that October — but was far from isolated: Castlereagh's instructions were express that Campbell was in no way to act as gaoler, but to put Napoleon in possession of the island of which he was sovereign prince. He received correspondence and newspapers from across Europe and actively improved the island's infrastructure (roads, hospitals) while drilling his troops. Security was lax enough that Napoleon escaped on the evening of 26 February 1815, timed for one of Campbell's frequent absences in Italy, with no British naval vessel patrolling nearby; he sailed in the brig *L'Inconstant* with six other vessels and roughly 1,150 men — some 600–650 of the Guard, about 100 Polish lancers, a Corsican battalion of about 300, plus gendarmes and civilians — landing at Golfe-Juan between Cannes and Antibes on the afternoon of 1 March 1815 and reaching Paris by 20 March as Louis XVIII fled, opening the Hundred Days (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-elba-hundred-days`).

Wikisource, *Dictionary of National Biography*, "Campbell, Neil (1776–1827)," https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1885-1900/Campbell,_Neil_(1776-1827), for Campbell's commission, his colonelcy of 4 June 1814 and knighthood of 2 October 1814, Castlereagh's express orders that he act in no way as gaoler, and his absence in Italy from 17 to 28 February 1815; Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Napoleon Bonaparte on the island of Elba," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/napoleon-bonaparte-on-the-island-of-elba.php, for Cambronne's arrival of 26 May 1814 with 675 grenadiers and 54 Polish light horse, Campbell's departure aboard the *Partridge*, and the escape of 26 February 1815 aboard *L'Inconstant* with roughly 1,150 men, landing at Golfe-Juan on 1 March. ## The Hundred Days and Waterloo {#later-hundred-days-waterloo}

**Standing: established by research.** The Hundred Days' political and military reconstitution, the invasion of Belgium, and the two actions of 16 June 1815 — Ligny and Quatre-Bras — are now recorded at scene level at `settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md`; Waterloo's own terrain, both orders of battle, chronology, and casualties have their own file at `settings/019-waterloo.md`. This canon's original summary judgment stands unchanged: Grouchy's detachment failed to prevent Blücher's Prussians reaching Waterloo, fighting a separate, inconclusive action at Wavre instead, a failure historians generally regard as a critical, though not sole, cause of Napoleon's defeat; Wellington's "the nearest run thing you ever saw" is well attested (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#wellington`). ## Saint Helena {#later-st-helena}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon was transported to the remote South Atlantic island of Saint Helena, disembarking 17 October 1815 after the *Northumberland* anchored off Jamestown two days earlier, moving to Longwood House on 10 December 1815, where he spent his final roughly five and a half years (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`). The house itself, his daily regime and garden, his relationship with Governor Hudson Lowe, the household's composition and its quarrels, and the dictation of his campaign memoirs are now recorded at scene level at `settings/020-saint-helena.md`, including the figure-level detail for Lowe, Bertrand, and Montholon now carried at `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#bertrand`, `#montholon`, and `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#hudson-lowe`. This entry's original summary — the household long called "the four evangelists" for the memoirs they later wrote, shaping his historical legend, comprising Bertrand, Montholon, Las Cases, and Gourgaud, plus the Irish surgeon Barry O'Meara — stands unchanged; Las Cases's arrest and expulsion, and the reliability of the individual memoirists, remain as originally fixed at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-lascases` and `#source-segur`.

## Death and its historiographical dispute {#later-death-dispute}

**Standing: established by research, with the cause of death disputed between the mainstream position and two named minority scholarly positions, per `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`.** The official autopsy of 6 May 1821, performed by Dr. Francesco Antommarchi before sixteen witnesses including seven British medical observers, found the stomach almost entirely diseased with a tumor over 10 cm extending from cardia to pylorus, consistent with gastric cancer — the disease that also killed his father, Carlo Buonaparte (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-parents`). In 1961, Swedish toxicologist Sten Forshufvud, working from the memoirs of Napoleon's valet Louis Marchand, proposed chronic arsenic poisoning, published the following year as Smith, Forshufvud & Wassén's hair-strand analysis showing arsenic levels up to roughly 38 times normal; the theory gained wide public attention, reinforced by a 1982 finding that wallpaper from Longwood contained arsenic-based pigment sufficient to cause illness but probably not death, published in the same *Nature* issue as an immediate corrective arguing against chronic poisoning as the actual cause of death. The arsenic theory has since been substantially contested: the tested hair strands were never definitively authenticated or dated to the relevant period, and a 2008 comparative neutron-activation study (University of Milano-Bicocca/Pavia) found Napoleon's hair arsenic levels — measured across several life stages — statistically indistinguishable from a comparison set including his son (the King of Rome), Joséphine, and ten living people, consistent with pervasive period-wide environmental arsenic exposure (in medicines, cosmetics, and pigments) rather than deliberate poisoning. A separate, later minority position (Mari, Bertol, Fineschi & Karch, 2004) argues for death by drug-interaction-induced cardiac arrhythmia — specifically that period medications given to Napoleon, including compounds containing antimony potassium tartrate, may have caused fatal QT-interval prolongation — a theory distinct from and not to be conflated with the arsenic-poisoning theory. Current mainstream scholarly consensus favors gastric cancer as cause of death, with both the arsenic-poisoning and the cardiac-arrhythmia theories treated as named minority positions rather than as established fact; a downstream unit may not present either alternative as established fact.

Sources: Lugli et al., "The gastric disease of Napoleon Bonaparte: brief report for the bicentenary of Napoleon's death on St. Helena in 1821," *Virchows Archiv* 479(5):1055–1060 (2021), PMC8572813, [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572813/); Smith, Forshufvud & Wassén, "Distribution of Arsenic in Napoleon's Hair," *Nature* 194:725–726 (1962); Jones & Ledingham, "Arsenic in Napoleon's wallpaper," *Nature* 299:626–627 (1982), [doi:10.1038/299626a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/299626a0), with the same-issue corrective at *Nature* 299:627–628 (1982), [doi:10.1038/299627a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/299627a0); the 2008 Milano-Bicocca/Pavia neutron-activation study, *Il Nuovo Saggiatore* (2008); Mari, Bertol, Fineschi & Karch, "Channelling the Emperor: what really killed Napoleon?", *Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine* 97(8):397–399 (2004), PMC1079564, [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1079564](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1079564/).

## Retour des cendres {#later-retour-des-cendres}

**Standing: established by research, except the specific 9 May 1821 burial date, which is not yet decided — no source this canon has read pins the burial to a specific day rather than only the year.** Napoleon died 5 May 1821 and was buried in the days following in the "Valley of the Tomb" (Geranium Valley), per his wishes but without his desired epitaph. This canon corrects the reason the tomb went unmarked: it was not a dispute over the epitaph's wording alone but a deadlock over whose wording would prevail — General Montholon wanted the slab to read simply "NAPOLEON," while Governor Hudson Lowe insisted "Bonaparte" be added, and the impasse left the slab blank; separately, subsequent petitions to the British and French governments for permission to repatriate the body were refused, keeping the grave as it was for two decades. His remains were exhumed and returned to France in the *retour des cendres*, initiated by Prime Minister Adolphe Thiers and King Louis-Philippe I, arriving at the Hôtel des Invalides in Paris on 15 December 1840, where they remain entombed — an event this canon notes as falling well outside the work's primary narrated window (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`) but available as established fact for any epilogue or retrospective framing a later layer chooses to use.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Retour des cendres," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retour_des_cendres (Thiers and Louis-Philippe as initiators; Valley of the Tomb/Geranium Valley; arrival and burial at the Invalides 15 December 1840; gives Napoleon's death only as 5 May 1821, with no specific burial day); napoleon.org, "The 'Retour des Cendres': Napoleon's body is returned to Paris," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/close-up-on-the-retour-des-cendres-napoleons-body-is-returned-to-paris/ (corroborating Thiers/Louis-Philippe and 15 December 1840; stating the grave was left unmarked because British and French governments refused repatriation petitions); Wikipedia, "Valley of the Tomb," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_of_the_Tomb (the actual locator for the epitaph deadlock: Montholon wanted "NAPOLEON" alone, Lowe insisted on adding "Bonaparte," and the impasse left the slab blank — the basis for the correction above).
