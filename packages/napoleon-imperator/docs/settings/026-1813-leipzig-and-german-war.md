# 1813 Leipzig and German War

This file owns the multinational war after the Russian retreat and hands the weakened French system toward the separate 1814 invasion setting.

## Rebuilding after Russia {#rebuilding-after-russia}
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#scope-preservation This H2 owns post-Russia manpower, remounts, training, readiness, and the distinction between paper strength and field capacity. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#scope-preservation #cc1e606 Compared the correspondence introduction and 1813 chronology; recruitment, experience, cavalry, and campaign readiness remain separate variables. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#substantive-completion This H2 settles how France could generate a large army while lacking the veterans, horses, and preparation that made numbers reliable. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#substantive-completion #ef2e621 Tested the numerical claim against the source's dated 60,000/137,000 context and removed unsupported universal totals. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#proportionate-development This H2 separates levy law, barracks, officers, drill, remounts, artillery teams, and combat endurance. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#proportionate-development #0a1be6f Checked that each material bottleneck has a distinct operational consequence and is not substituted by a generic quality judgment. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance This host cites dated French recruitment context and official campaign chronology without inventing battalion strength or training schedules. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked the source paragraphs and qualified every readiness inference. -->

**Status:** Source-supported with bounded inference. The 1813 levies restored mass more quickly than experience, horses, and staff competence could be restored.

The Correspondance générale introduction records only about 60,000 men beyond the Elbe while the 1812 class was exhausted and the 1813 class was only beginning to enter barracks (pp. 22–23 cited in its note). Fondation's chronology records 180,000 new conscripts called on 3 April, including early 1814 men, and its critical account credits the Marie-Louise recruits and National Guard with tenacity at Lützen, Bautzen, Dresden, Kulm, and Leipzig. Those facts do not prove equal training: a depot return, a marched battalion, and a formation that can reload under fire are different states. Lost veterans, remounts, team horses, and staff habits constrain reconnaissance, artillery movement, and cohesion. The setting therefore permits rapid strength recovery but not automatic recovery of the Grande Armée's 1812 competence.

Sources: Napoleon.org, Correspondance générale, Tome 13 introduction, pp. 22–23, https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/correspondance-generale-de-napoleon-bonaparte-tome-13-le-commencement-de-la-fin-janvier-juin-1813-introduction-au-volume/; Napoleon.org, “1813 and the lead up to the Battle of Leipzig,” 3 April and 2 May entries, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/38920/.

**Status:** Source-critical. German mobilization is a bundle of state decisions, military obligations, and uneven local responses, not one national switch.

The chronology records a Prussian order for a volunteer corps on 3 February, reaching roughly 8,000 within weeks, while its April entries place Russian and Prussian forces around Leipzig. It also records that Saxony's Frederick Augustus returned to the French alliance after Lützen and ordered Torgau open, while commander von Thielmann delayed and then fled to the Allies. By contrast, the Hamburg entry says Tettenborn's invasion did not produce a general popular rising and that inhabitants' unwillingness helped force abandonment on 30 May. These cases establish administrative and local divergence: a court may sign, a fortress commander may delay, a regiment may march, and a town may refuse the expected revolt. Language, uniform, tax, billeting, and requisition are mechanisms through which loyalty becomes observable, never proof of a single German will.

**Status:** Source-supported and source-critical. Leipzig's scale is a constraint on knowledge as much as on manpower.

The critical account describes Napoleon's forces as centrally commanded and positioned on 16 October, while allied armies were spread around the city with different strategic goals, languages, and commanders. Its timeline separately records Probstheida in the south, Schönefeld and the Halle Gate in the north, the French withdrawal through Leipzig, and the Elster crossing on 19 October. A later narrative may connect these sectors only through a documented road, courier, visible signal, or staff report. The same bridge can be a route for artillery, a reinforcement channel, and a retreat choke point, but those uses compete in time and space. A regiment in a village knows its own smoke, officers, and neighboring frontage—not the final coalition map. This is the scale rule for every subsequent Leipzig unit.

**Status:** Source-critical. A coalition can share a strategic purpose while orders still travel through distinct headquarters, languages, maps, and roads.

The critical synthesis explicitly contrasts Napoleon's shorter central command distances with allied armies on a perimeter, commanded by generals of different languages and cultures. That is a structural condition, not proof that any particular message was mistranslated. Settings therefore record a dispatch by sender, recipient, medium, route, reference clock, and receipt where a source supplies them. A staff officer may translate a place-name or summarize a map; a courier may find a road occupied; a report may describe an enemy seen hours earlier. The execution consequence is a bounded delay or ambiguity, never a made-up conversation. Coalition decisions remain real at the political level while their local implementation is asynchronous.

**Status:** Source-supported with contested quantitative detail. Rivers are operational infrastructure whose capacity, approach, and control change over time.

The critical account says Napoleon failed to prepare more than one bridge over the Pleiße for an army requiring artillery and huge numbers of men. Its 19 October chronology then describes one bridge over the Elster, allied fire reaching it around midday, and a corporal detonating charges after the officer who sought precise instructions had left. The same source reports traditional figures of 30,000 soldiers, 30 generals, 260 cannon, and 870 ammunition wagons trapped, while warning that casualty totals vary and offering a different reconstruction. The setting uses the bridge mechanics—queue, guard, gun teams, wounded, wagons, demolition, and alternative ford or road—without treating every number as settled. A planned route and a physically open route are never synonymous.

**Status:** Primary-text anchored and source-critical. Bavaria's realignment is a legal and military act whose knowledge and implementation travel unevenly.

The Ried treaty text of 8 October 1813 states in Article 2 that Bavaria disengages from the Confederation of the Rhine and joins the Allied powers; Article 5 places the Bavarian army in the Austrian and Allied grand army. A critical reference records Bavaria's 14 October declaration of war, a later step than the signature. The chronology separately shows Saxony returning to France after Lützen and Thielmann delaying before fleeing to the Allies. These dates forbid an instant regional switch: a plenipotentiary signs, a court publishes, commanders receive instructions, stores and roads change hands, and neighboring civilians learn through different channels. The file treats the Confederation's dissolution after Leipzig as political consequence, not proof that every contingent or town changed allegiance simultaneously.

**Status:** Source-critical. The westward retreat is an administrative crisis whose numbers depend on category and point of measurement.

The chronology distinguishes men captured when the Elster bridge was destroyed, cannon and ammunition wagons left behind, traditional French loss totals, a higher critical estimate, allied losses, and the men under arms and straggling at Erfurt. Those are not interchangeable denominators. A wounded man may become a hospital patient, a straggler, or a captive; a missing man may later rejoin; a gun lost at a bridge is matériel loss rather than a dead crew. Rear guards spend ammunition and time to protect roads while engineers, surgeons, wagoners, and civilians compete for the same crossings and shelter. The novel may stage these pressures, but every report must preserve its observer, date, category, and route. “The army was destroyed” is therefore a political summary, not a sufficient operational fact.

**Status:** Work boundary grounded in critical synthesis. Leipzig opens the road to France but does not erase command, institutions, or every allied relationship at once.

The chronology concludes that many French troops escaped, that a substantial force and matériel were trapped at the Elster, and that most later encounters would occur on French soil. The critical “Napoleon in 1813” account connects the retreat to the repossession of Leipzig, the collapse of French influence in Germany, and the eventual fall of Napoleon, while also stressing that the emperor could still have won before the errors accumulated. The next setting must therefore inherit surviving cadres, exhausted recruits, damaged cavalry and artillery transport, hostile or uncertain German roads, and a coalition encouraged by victory. It must not inherit omniscient hindsight: Napoleon, local officials, soldiers, and civilians learn the transition through separate dispatches, movement, requisition, and visible flags. The 1814 invasion begins after this handoff, not inside it.
