**Status:** Source-supported. Friedland's military outcome creates leverage but does not dictate every treaty clause.

The defeated army, the emperor's diplomatic representatives, and the French command possess different information. A battlefield report arrives before the treaty; a victory can be exaggerated to strengthen bargaining. The novel must preserve that sequence and keep military reputation separate from the legal authority to sign.

An emperor's spoken promise cannot be treated as a signed article, and a signed article does not specify every local consequence. **Status:** Work canon. Tilsit reorganizes territory and creates dependent political arrangements whose populations experience new administrations.

Maps and treaties assign sovereignty, but officials, taxes, armies, and local elites implement it. A client state is not a puppet with no agency; it has institutions and interests constrained by French power. Settings must identify the new ruler, the administrative language, the garrison or military obligation, and the people who collect revenue or resist the transfer.

Diplomatic warmth can coexist with economic friction, Polish questions, Ottoman interests, and mutual military suspicion. The relationship must deteriorate through accumulated decisions, not one invented betrayal. Each government maintains its own army, court, treasury, and information system even while an official communiqué describes friendship.
