# The King of Rome

This file gives the birth of Napoleon's only legitimate child the granular, scene-level detail `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage` dates but does not carry: the difficult delivery and the choice it forced, the salute that announced it to Paris, the title's actual legal basis, and the regency the dynasty then rested on. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Birth and the "sauvez la mère" instruction {#kingofrome-birth}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact wording of Napoleon's instruction to the accoucheur genuinely unsettled among four independently sourced variants.** Labor began the evening of 19 March 1811, around 8-9 p.m.; contractions continued through the night, slowed around 6 a.m., then resumed "avec le plus de vivacité" from 8 a.m. Birth occurred at 9:20 a.m. on 20 March 1811 at the Tuileries. The attending accoucheur was Baron Antoine Dubois (1756-1837), who was, per historian Jean Tulard's account (drawing on Napoleon's own later retelling to Las Cases at St. Helena), "écrasé par sa responsabilité," crushed by his responsibility, when the child presented badly; Napoleon reassured him he "n'avait qu'à se figurer qu'il accouchait une bourgeoise de la rue Saint-Denis" (had only to imagine he was delivering a shopkeeper's wife of the Rue Saint-Denis), and when Dubois hesitated between saving mother or child, Napoleon ruled: "Sauvez la mère... Avec elle j'aurai un autre enfant. Conduisez-vous ici comme si vous attendiez le fils d'un savetier" (save the mother; with her I will have another child; conduct yourself here as if you were attending the son of a cobbler). Marie-Louise reportedly cried out at the sight of the forceps: "Parce que je suis impératrice, me sacrifiera-t-on?" (because I am an empress, will they sacrifice me?). This canon records the instruction's substance as well established but its exact wording as genuinely disputed: the Comte de Rambuteau's own memoirs give Napoleon's phrase as "sauvez la mère, c'est votre devoir, et agissez délibérément comme avec la femme d'un épicier de la rue Saint-Denis" (save the mother, it is your duty, and act deliberately as with the wife of a grocer of the Rue Saint-Denis); the Comte de Lavallette's memoirs give it instead as "Faites comme pour une simple bourgeoise: sauvez la mère avant tout" (act as for a simple bourgeoise: save the mother above all); and a later biographical account of Dubois himself, by Dr. Paul Ganière, gives a sharper, angrier outburst directed at Dubois when he stalled: "La nature n'a pas deux lois... Faites comme si vous vous trouviez en présence d'une petite bourgeoise du faubourg Saint-Denis... Dubois, je vous ordonne d'accoucher l'Impératrice" (nature has no double standard; act as though you were in the presence of a humble bourgeoise of the Faubourg Saint-Denis; Dubois, I order you to deliver the Empress). No source located in this canon's research gives the popularly compressed English form "treat her as you would a bourgeoise, not as an empress" verbatim; that antithetical "not as an empress" clause does not appear in any of the four documented variants and should be treated as a later paraphrase, not a quotation, in any downstream use. Dubois was afterward rewarded with a 100,000-franc gratification, 15,000 francs in fees, the Legion of Honor, and a barony carrying a 4,000-9,000-franc dotation.

## The 101-gun salute and the counting crowds {#kingofrome-salute}

**Standing: established by research, except the number of guns fired for a girl, which is disputed between two named sources (20 and 25) rather than the popularly assumed 21.** The Moniteur's own 21 March 1811 announcement records that Napoleon "a donné l'ordre de faire tirer les salves de cent un coups de canon" (gave the order to fire the salvo of one hundred and one cannon shots) and describes Parisians learning the news in real time: "Dès que les salves se firent entendre, on vit de toutes parts les habitants de Paris se mettre à leurs fenêtres, descendre à leurs portes, remplir les rues et compter les coups de canon, avec une vive sollicitude" (as soon as the salvos were heard, the inhabitants of Paris were seen on every side coming to their windows, descending to their doors, filling the streets, and counting the cannon shots with vivid anxiety). Independent eyewitness accounts corroborate the scene: Stendhal's own journal records "Le canon l'éveille à dix heures... nous comptons le vingt-deuxième avec transport" (the cannon wakes him at ten; we count the twenty-second with rapture); Alexandre Bellot de Kergorre's *Journal d'un commissaire des guerres* records, "La fenêtre ouverte, je comptais attentivement les coups... aussitôt que le nombre des coups eut proclamé la naissance du roi de Rome, on n'entendit partout que ces mots: « C'est un garçon! »" (window open, I counted the shots attentively; as soon as the count proclaimed the birth of the King of Rome, nothing was heard everywhere but the words: it's a boy!); and Dr. Poumiès de la Siboutie's *Souvenirs d'un médecin de Paris* records, "les ouvriers quittaient leur ouvrage, les marchands fermaient leurs boutiques... C'était une joie désordonnée, c'était de l'ivresse" (workers left their work, shopkeepers closed their shops; it was an unruly joy, it was intoxication). The number of shots that would have been fired for a girl is genuinely disputed rather than the commonly assumed 21: Jean Tulard's own article states "Vingt coups auraient suffi pour une fille" (twenty shots would have sufficed for a girl), while Comte de Lavallette's memoirs, quoted by Beyeler, state the government had announced "cent un coups de canon" for a prince and "seulement vingt-cinq" for a princess, with the counting anecdote there pivoting on the twenty-sixth shot rather than the twenty-second: "Au vingt-sixième coup de canon la joie fut portée jusqu'au délire" (at the twenty-sixth cannon shot the joy rose to delirium). This canon records both figures as disputed rather than asserting either, and does not use 21 as a settled number.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Document: Le Moniteur du 21 mars 1811...," read in full via the fetch helper, for the primary Moniteur text quoted above; Christophe Beyeler, "La naissance du roi de Rome: apogée de l'Empire?," as above, quoting Stendhal's *Journal*, Bellot de Kergorre's *Journal d'un commissaire des guerres sous le Premier Empire* (1997 ed., pp. 42-43), Dr. Poumiès de la Siboutie's *Souvenirs d'un médecin de Paris* (1910 ed., p. 95), and Comte de Lavallette's memoirs for the 25/26-shot figure; Jean Tulard, "La naissance du Roi de Rome," as above, for the competing 20-shot figure.

## The title: legal basis and practical governance of Rome {#kingofrome-title}

**Standing: established by research.** The title rested on a standing dynastic-designation clause fixed more than a year before the birth, not on a decree issued at the moment of birth: the *sénatus-consulte* of 17 February 1810, Article VII, provided that "le prince impérial porte le titre et reçoit les honneurs du Roi de Rome" (the imperial prince bears the title and receives the honors of King of Rome) — a wording corroborated identically across two independent sources this canon consulted. This clause followed the annexation of the Papal States (`settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md#church-annexation`): per historian Louis Madelin's own scholarly account, "Du 10 juin 1809 au 19 janvier 1814, Rome fut, officiellement, une ville française, le chef-lieu d'un des 130 départemens qui composaient l'énorme empire de Napoléon. Un sénatus-consulte la proclama seconde ville de l'Empire et lui promit comme roi l'héritier à venir de la couronne impériale" (from 10 June 1809 to 19 January 1814, Rome was officially a French city, the chief town of one of the 130 departments composing Napoleon's enormous empire; a senatus-consulte proclaimed it the Empire's second city and promised it, as king, the future heir of the imperial crown) — the Senate had heard the exposé des motifs for Rome's annexation to France on 14 February 1810, three days before the 17 February decree that followed from it. The title carried no actual governing role whatsoever: Rome's real administration in this period ran through a provisional five-member Consulte extraordinaire des États Romains (1809-1810), with General Sextius de Miollis commanding the occupying corps and functioning as de facto administrator from the Piazza di Spagna. Joseph Fouché was named governor-general in June 1810 but never went, per Madelin preferring "to remain second in Paris rather than become first in Rome," and quietly resigned the post; in February 1811 Napoleon gave Miollis instead the deliberately modest and provisional title "lieutenant du gouverneur général," Madelin's own reading being that Napoleon, "toujours mû par la jalousie" (always moved by jealousy), never actually appointed a full governor-general at all. The former Papal States were administratively split into two French départements — Tibre (capital Rome, prefect Camille de Tournon, nicknamed "Camillus Capitolinus") and Trasimène (capital Spoleto, roughly Umbria, prefect Rœderer). The King of Rome's title was, in short, purely dynastic and symbolic — an assertion of Napoleonic primacy over both the dispossessed Pope and the older Habsburg tradition of "King of the Romans" — never a governing office the infant himself or his household exercised.

Sources: Jean Tulard, "La naissance du Roi de Rome," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 486 (2011), read in full via the fetch helper, quoting the 17 February 1810 *sénatus-consulte*'s Article VII; this wording is independently corroborated by a French Wikisource page ("Le Roi de Rome et les femmes/2"), reached only as an AI-generated summary rather than raw text and flagged accordingly, but its Article VII quotation matches Tulard's independently-sourced one exactly; Louis Madelin, "La Domination française à Rome de 1809 à 1814," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, 5e période, tome 28 (1905), pp. 614-650, full text read directly at fr.wikisource.org — the source for the 10 June 1809-19 January 1814 dating, the Consulte extraordinaire, Miollis's role, Fouché's declined governorship, Miollis's February 1811 "lieutenant" title, and the Tibre/Trasimène départements and their prefects.

## Regency arrangements, 5 February 1813 {#kingofrome-regency}

**Standing: established by research, correcting a commonly circulated "1811" dating this canon's research found no support for.** No dedicated 1811 instrument establishing Marie-Louise as regent exists in the sources this canon could locate; going into 1811 the operative constitutional baseline remained the original *sénatus-consulte du 28 floréal an XII* (18 May 1804), Title IV, Article 18, §2, which explicitly excluded women from the regency ("Les femmes sont exclues de la Régence"). The actual instrument is dated over a year after the birth: on 1 February 1813 Napoleon held a privy council to which he communicated the "Sénatus-consulte organique sur la Régence," including articles on the coronation of the Empress and the King of Rome; the text was transmitted to the Senate on 2 February, where Regnaud attempted to justify the provisions abolishing, so far as the regency was concerned, the old constitutional exclusion — "Ici on disait: « Les femmes sont exclues de la Régence »; à présent on dit: « A défaut de toute disposition de l'Empereur mort, l'Impératrice mère réunit de droit à la garde de son fils, la Régence de l'Empire »" (here it used to be said, women are excluded from the regency; now it is said, failing any disposition by the deceased Emperor, the Empress mother unites by right, along with guardianship of her son, the Regency of the Empire). The reform deliberately structured the accompanying Regency Council to exclude the princes of the imperial family and vest real authority instead in the Empire's grands dignitaires and vice-grands dignitaires — a check against a Bonaparte-family power grab. Presented 2 February, the *sénatus-consulte* was adopted 5 February 1813. It was put into actual practice twice: in spring 1813, when Napoleon left to fight in Germany after the Russian disaster, per the same napoleon.org biography of Marie-Louise, "il laisse à Marie-Louise la régence, en limitant toutefois ses pouvoirs politiques" (he leaves the regency to Marie-Louise, though limiting her political powers) — and again during the 1814 Campaign of France. A downstream unit should not date the regency's legal basis to 1811, the birth year, and should not present a named regent as fixed for the roughly two years between the birth and the February 1813 act; this canon's research could not settle who, if anyone, would have exercised the regency under the still-standing 1804 exclusion had Napoleon died in that interval, and treats the question as not yet decided rather than inventing an answer.
