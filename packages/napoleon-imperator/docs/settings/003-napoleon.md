# Napoleon Bonaparte

This file is the figure-level unit for Napoleon himself, per `principles/settings.md#figures-and-relations`. It records what the work needs of him as a person rather than as a sequence of events; his dated acts are in `settings/002-chronology.md` and his relations with named others are recorded in their own units in the family, marshal, court, and foreign-crown files.

## Origins and formation {#napoleon-origins}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon was formed by three overlapping displacements: a minor, cash-poor Corsican nobility whose standing depended entirely on his father's success in petitioning the French crown (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-birth`); a French military education (Brienne, then the École Militaire) that marked him as provincial, Italian-accented, and financially straitened among wealthier French cadets; and an early loss of his father in 1785, aged 15, that made him the practical head of a large family's finances years before he had rank or income to support it. He compressed his final year of officer training under that financial pressure (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-education`). He was Corsican by birth and early loyalty — he sought leave to join Paoli when the National Assembly allowed his 1789 return, but the relationship was strained from the start, Paoli having "no sympathy for the young man, whose father had deserted his cause and whom he considered to be a foreigner"; when Napoleon sided with the Corsican Jacobins against Paoli's separatist policies and civil war broke out in April 1793, Paoli had the Buonaparte family condemned to "perpetual execration and infamy," and the whole family fled to mainland France — and French by career, language of command, and eventual identity — a fact this canon treats as a permanent, load-bearing tension in him rather than a resolved biographical footnote.

Sources: Britannica, "Napoleon I," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Napoleon-I, for the Paoli relationship, its strain from the 1789 return, the April 1793 civil war and condemnation, and the family's flight to France, quoted directly above; `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-birth`, `#chron-education`, each established by research, for the birth-family and education material this entry otherwise synthesizes.

## Ambition and self-conception {#napoleon-ambition}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** Napoleon wants total, personally verified control of outcomes he can be blamed for, and refuses to be dependent on an account of events he has not gathered or checked himself — hence his lifelong preference for direct correspondence and personal reconnaissance over trusting subordinates' summaries. He wants dynastic permanence for what he has built (crown, marriage, and heir all serve this single want, not romantic or religious motives in their own right) and refuses to accept that any given defeat or setback is more than a temporary interruption of a trajectory he privately regards as owed to him by talent and effort rather than birth. He conceives of himself as the corrective to inherited privilege — a man who earned every rank he holds against men who were merely born to theirs — and this self-conception is both genuinely true of his early career (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-toulon`) and increasingly false of his later one, as he comes to demand exactly the inherited deference he once scorned in the old nobility, without registering the contradiction.

Sources: Saythala Lay Phonexayphova, "Expectations and Resentment: The Roots of Napoleon Bonaparte's Ambition and Success," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/napoleon/c_ambition.html, which traces the meritocratic self-conception and its formation to Philip Dwyer, *Napoleon: The Path to Power* (Yale University Press, 2007); Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life* (per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship`); the self-made-man trajectory itself rests on `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-toulon` and `#chron-vendemiaire`, each established by research. This entry's specific characterizing phrasing remains an authorial synthesis of those attested facts.

## Capabilities {#napoleon-capabilities}

**Standing: established by research for the dictation and legal-engagement claims; decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus, for the battlefield working-memory claim specifically.** Napoleon has a demonstrated, exceptional capacity to hold a battlefield's or a campaign's full order of battle, terrain, and timing in working memory and to issue correct, fast, written orders from it without needing to re-derive the situation each time — the corps system he matured (`settings/008-grande-armee.md#corps-system`) is itself evidence of this capability, not merely its instrument, and his own account of the *coup d'œil militaire* (`settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-ambition`) is his own description of the same faculty. He can dictate several distinct letters on unrelated subjects to several secretaries in immediate succession without losing the thread of any one of them, a habit attested of his working method across Méneval's and Fain's terms as secretary and load-bearing for how his headquarters actually functioned (`settings/008-grande-armee.md#command-staff`). He reads administrative and legal detail (tax schedules, code drafts, quartermaster returns) with the same appetite he brings to maps, and the Napoleonic Code (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`) reflects genuine, sustained personal engagement with legal drafting, not a delegated project he merely signed: he attended in person 36 of the drafting commission's 87 sessions, a degree of sustained personal involvement no other head of state of the period gave to a civil code, even though it left the majority of sessions to the jurists. He is a highly effective public performer of confidence under pressure — the deliberate feint of weakness before Austerlitz (`settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-diplomatic-feint`) required him to convincingly perform doubt he did not feel, which he could do on demand.

Sources: on the Code, The Napoleon Series, "Code Napoleon," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/code/, recording that he attended 36 of the drafting commission's 87 sessions in person; on the Austerlitz feint, Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of Austerlitz," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/austerlitz.php; on the dictation habit, the testimony of his own secretaries — Claude-François de Méneval, *Memoirs to Serve for the History of Napoleon I* (archive.org, item `memoirsofnapoleo01mn`) and Baron Fain, *Mémoires du Baron Fain, premier secrétaire du cabinet de l'Empereur* (archive.org, item `mmoiresdubaronfa00fain`) — both household insiders writing to flatter as well as to record, so treated as attesting the habit rather than fixing its exact scale; `settings/008-grande-armee.md#command-staff`, established by research. The battlefield working-memory claim is this canon's characterizing extrapolation from the corps system's design and the secretarial testimony, not an independently documented psychological trait. No citation to David Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon* is made here: this canon has not opened that book.

## Limits and blind spots {#napoleon-limits}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** Napoleon cannot reliably read a subordinate's genuine limits once that subordinate has proven useful to him once — he over-trusts a demonstrated success (Murat's cavalry, `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-murat`) into contexts that success does not transfer to, and under-forgives a demonstrated failure once it has cost him (Villeneuve's retreat to Cádiz hardens into contempt he cannot set aside even when a fairer reading of the naval situation was available). He mistakes a plan's clarity in his own mind for its clarity in execution, and is repeatedly surprised — genuinely, not performatively — when an order he considers unambiguous is executed wrongly, slowly, or not at all by men operating with less information than he has. He cannot sit with an unresolved personal matter: the Joséphine succession question (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`) is allowed to fester for years specifically because he will not force the medical and dynastic question to a conclusion while a plausible alternative story (that the fault is not proven to be hers) remains available to him. He does not register, and this canon does not have him register, the human cost of scale once a decision is made at the level of corps and armies rather than individuals — not from cruelty performed for its own sake, but from a working habit of thought that treats a corps's casualty return the way he treats a quartermaster's return of spoiled flour: a number to be managed, not a population of deaths to be felt.

Sources: this entry is an authorial synthesis for characterization; its factual anchors are Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life*, and Charles Esdaile's more critical assessment, per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship`; on the Villeneuve instance, Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of Trafalgar," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/trafalgar.php, recording that Napoleon ordered Admiral Rosily to replace Villeneuve on 17 September 1805 without informing Villeneuve himself; the Murat instance cross-references `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-murat`, established by research.

## Beliefs and things he believes wrongly {#napoleon-beliefs}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** Napoleon genuinely believes that a settlement's durability is proportional to the decisiveness of the military victory that produced it, and treats a crushing win (Austerlitz, `settings/012-austerlitz.md`) as having settled a political question rather than merely deferred it — a belief the actual behavior of Prussia, Austria, and Russia in the years following each of his victories repeatedly disproves without dislodging the belief itself. He believes his own administrative and legal reforms (the Code, `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`; the Legion of Honor, `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#legion-of-honor`) are received by conquered and allied populations substantially as they are received in France — as rational, meritocratic modernization — and consistently underestimates how much of that reception in occupied territory is instead resentment of a foreign occupier's law, however well drafted. He believes, from the Corsican formation described at `settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-origins` onward, that he reads men accurately by watching them under pressure once, and does not update this belief even where it has visibly failed him (Bernadotte, `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-bernadotte`, remains in positions of trust for years after repeated evidence that he cannot be relied on). He also holds a genuine, non-cynical belief in his own fortune or "star" — a favorable providence attached specifically to him — which the deliberately mythologized "sun of Austerlitz" (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-weather`) both reflects and, through the Bulletins' own propaganda, reinforces in a feedback loop he does not fully distinguish from the underlying military reality.

Sources: this entry is an authorial synthesis for characterization, anchored in Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life*; on the belief in his "star," Claude-François de Méneval, *Memoirs to Serve for the History of Napoleon I* (archive.org, item `memoirsofnapoleo01mn`), which records his conviction that he was called to play a part on the world's stage, read alongside Caulaincourt's memoirs (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-caulaincourt`, established by research); the Bernadotte instance cross-references `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-bernadotte`, established by research, though this canon has not located a scholarly biography of Bernadotte establishing the *repeated*-failure pattern as documented rather than inferred, and treats that specific characterization as its own extrapolation; the propaganda mechanics are documented at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`.

## Manner and speech {#napoleon-speech}

**Standing: established by research, with specific documented registers.** Napoleon's formal register — proclamations, bulletins, addresses to troops — is short-clause, rhetorically confident, and built to be read aloud or reprinted; the attested 1 December 1805 address to the army before Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`) is representative of this register and this canon treats its documented wording, not an invented paraphrase, as the standard for how he addresses troops in formal set pieces. In private working exchanges with staff he is fast, interrupting, and impatient with hedged answers — he wants a number or a location, not an assessment of confidence in the number or location, and personnel who learn to answer him in exactly that compressed form (Berthier chief among them, `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-berthier`) become disproportionately valuable to him for that skill alone, independent of their other merits. His documented private correspondence to intimates (Joséphine during the Italian campaign, later Marie-Louise) carries a register of intense, almost adolescent personal declaration that coexists, without apparent contradiction to him, with the calculating dynastic register he uses about the same relationships in the same period — this canon treats both registers as simultaneously genuine rather than one as a mask for the other.

Sources: `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`, whose full text is at The Napoleon Series, "Napoleon's Addresses: the Austerlitz Campaign 1805," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/napoleon/speeches/c_speeches5.html; `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance`; *The Letters of Napoleon to Marie-Louise* (trans. Philip Guedalla, Hutchinson &amp; Co., 1935), full text at archive.org, for the documented intimate-register letters ("I often think of you... Tout à toi. Nap," 30 June 1812; "it is a sweet habit I have acquired," 19 June 1812); Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life*.

## Habits and physical person {#napoleon-habits}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon stood approximately 1.68–1.70 m (roughly 5 ft 6–7 in) — average or slightly below average for a Frenchman of his generation, not the marked shortness of later British caricature; the "short" myth partly reflects wartime propaganda and partly a discrepancy between French and English measurement units applied to a French autopsy record. He slept in short, broken intervals rather than one sustained block, particularly on campaign, and was capable of falling asleep rapidly under conditions (gunfire, a moving carriage) that kept other men awake. He ate quickly and with little apparent interest in the food itself, a trait remarked on by several who dined with him. He suffered recurring stomach and digestive complaints across his adult life, worsening in his final years on Saint Helena, consistent with the gastric disease that is the established cause of his death (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`). He habitually pinched the ear or cheek of a soldier or officer he wished to single out with rough, wordless affection — an attested gesture this canon treats as available for any scene where he wants to convey approval without a speech. He took snuff, though inconsistently and often more as something to fidget with than to actually inhale.

Sources: Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life*; napoleon.org, on the height myth; the 1821 autopsy record per `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-st-helena-death`.

## What would move him against habit {#napoleon-against-habit}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** Three pressures reliably move Napoleon off his default patterns of decisive, unilateral action: first, direct, credible evidence — gathered personally, not reported secondhand — that a subordinate he has trusted has deceived him (this canon establishes his fury at Murat's unauthorized armistice at Hollabrunn, `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-murat`, as the clearest attested instance, and any scene invoking his capacity for genuine rather than performed rage should draw on that register); second, a threat to the legitimacy or permanence of the dynasty rather than to himself personally, which can make him act with a coldness toward people he otherwise treats with real warmth (the Joséphine divorce, `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`, is the governing instance); and third, being shown — again, personally and concretely, not merely told — that a plan he considers settled has failed in its actual execution, at which point he can pivot with a speed his staff find disorienting, abandoning a design he defended completely an hour before without acknowledging the reversal as a reversal. He does not reliably yield to moral appeal, to the argument that a course is unjust, or to simple accumulated fatigue in others; those pressures do not move him, and this canon does not permit a downstream scene to have them do so without an additional, specific pressure of the three kinds above also present.

Sources: this is an authorial decision for characterization, drawing on the documented Murat episode (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-murat`) and the documented divorce (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`), both established by research.
