# Brienne and Artillery Education

This file owns Napoleon's mainland schooling from Autun through Brienne and the transition toward artillery service. It records institutions, teachers, resources, social friction, and documented correspondence; it does not decide the reader's flashback sequence or invent classroom dialogue.

Autun is the intermediate institution between Ajaccio and Brienne. The brothers do not arrive as blank candidates for a military career: they arrive from Corsica, carrying family expectations and an uneven relationship to French. The school gives them access to the language used by mainland officials and the military education system. It also makes their provincial and insular origins visible to teachers and other boys.

The setting distinguishes language preparation from full assimilation. A student can memorize lessons, understand an order, and still speak with an accent or search for a word under ridicule. A scenario may show correction, imitation, written exercise, or a practical misunderstanding, but it cannot turn a later memoir's comic anecdote into a contemporaneous transcript. Joseph and Napoleon share the move while having different temperaments and educational prospects; neither brother's later career should be projected backward as inevitable.

Autun's short duration matters. Napoleon leaves on 21 April after roughly four months, stays about three weeks with M. de Champeaux, and proceeds to Brienne. The route is a chain of temporary rooms, adult escorts, and institutional gates. The manuscript must not collapse it into one school year or place Brienne teachers at Autun.

**Status:** Source-verified for Brienne's role as a royal military school and Napoleon's residence from May 1779 to October 1784; bounded in the exact daily timetable and in retrospective descriptions of its buildings.

Brienne is a branch of the royal military-school system, not a modern academy in which every pupil immediately commands troops. It prepares boys of recognized noble status for further military education. Napoleon enters on 15 May 1779 as a king's scholar and leaves in 1784 for the École militaire in Paris. The institution supplies uniform, discipline, academic instruction, physical training, religious instruction, and a hierarchy of teachers and inspectors.

The scholarship is double-edged. It removes some financial barriers while marking a student as dependent on royal support. A boy from a newly French island is placed among provincial and metropolitan nobles whose resources and accents differ. The school can produce opportunity and humiliation simultaneously. The settings canon therefore treats the uniform as a resource, a sign of status, and a means of surveillance rather than a costume that erases class.

The building is an operational environment. Dormitories, refectory, classroom, yard, chapel, riding space, and exercise grounds impose different rules and witnesses. A manuscript may make a classroom argument visible through posture, correction, or a shared book, but it must not give one room every function. Later museum arrangements and commemorative objects inform orientation; they do not prove where an eighteenth-century event occurred.

This is enough to establish a social pressure, not enough to quote every taunt or label every classmate a permanent enemy. A scenario should show the practical consequences: a name mispronounced, an answer interrupted, a uniform inspected, a friendship formed around a shared subject, or a solitary meal. **Status:** Primary-source verified for Napoleon's letter to his father from Brienne dated 12 or 13 September 1784, preserved in Archives nationales 400 AP 137 and published in the *Correspondance générale*; bounded in translation, emotional inference, and the family's unrecorded reply.

The letter is a rare direct voice from the adolescent, but it is not a transparent diary. Napoleon writes to Charles from the Royal Military School at Brienne and responds to news that his father's planned visit to Napoleon and Lucien will not occur because Charles's health requires his return to Corsica. The letter expresses concern for his father's health and affection for the family while acknowledging disappointment. Its exact date is given as 12 or 13 September 1784 in the Fondation presentation.

**Status:** Source-verified for the end of Napoleon's Brienne period in October 1784 and his move to the École militaire in Paris; bounded in the exact examination ranking and in retrospective claims that artillery was chosen solely by passion or solely by poverty.

Napoleon leaves Brienne in 1784 for the École militaire in Paris, where he will complete the next stage of officer education. The move is a selection event: the school, inspectors, scholarship system, family resources, and the student's performance combine to determine which professional route remains available. The settings file does not assign a single motive. Mathematics and artillery fit one another; a short course and the need for a rapid commission also matter; family finances and the status of a king's scholar remain present.

The departure carries the school forward. Napoleon leaves teachers, classmates, routines, and a documented letter exchange, but he does not leave Corsica behind. Language, family obligation, Paoli's image, and the need for institutional recognition continue into Paris and the later revolutionary period.
