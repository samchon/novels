# Court Ceremony and Household

This file owns court as an institution of access, rank, gender, and performance.

<!-- principles/common.md#purpose-fit principles/common.md#layer-boundary principles/common.md#work-language -->

## From Consular residence to imperial court {#from-consular-residence-to-imperial-court}

**Status:** Source-supported. Court develops through offices, apartments, audiences, uniforms, calendars, and a hierarchy of access.

The emperor's household is an administrative machine as well as a social scene. An invitation, an usher, a chamberlain, a guard, and a written appointment determine who can approach power. Proximity does not guarantee influence. Settings must identify the office, gate, document, and person that turns physical nearness into a possible audience.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Ceremony as political technology {#ceremony-as-political-technology}

**Status:** Work canon. Coronations, receptions, marriages, medals, and military displays make hierarchy visible and invite public interpretation.

Ceremony allocates precedence, money, costume, space, and witness. A slight in rank can have diplomatic consequences; a splendid display can conceal fiscal strain. Scenarios must stage servants and preparation alongside the public image and must distinguish the schedule announced by court from what a participant actually sees.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/; Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/.

## Household staff and information {#household-staff-and-information}

**Status:** Source-critical. Secretaries, valets, ladies, physicians, cooks, guards, and visitors know different fragments of the sovereign's life.

Staff are not invisible scenery. They carry letters, hear quarrels, manage rooms, and shape access, but their knowledge is partial and their testimony later mediated. The novel must not make a servant an omniscient narrator merely because he is nearby. Each scene must keep the worker's duty, location, access, and reason for silence visible.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon correspondence, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/; Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/.

## Apartments routes and controlled access {#apartments-routes-and-controlled-access}

**Status:** Source-required domain. Rooms, corridors, doors, guards, schedules, and keys make court access a physical system.

A visitor can be admitted to a palace without reaching the emperor; a servant can carry a note without reading it; a family member can possess intimacy without controlling policy. Settings must map the route, threshold, escort, and interruption that shape each encounter. The physical house is part of the evidence, not a decorative backdrop.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Fondation Napoléon correspondence, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.

## Gender rank and household authority {#gender-rank-and-household-authority}

**Status:** Work canon. Court access is structured by gender, title, marriage, kinship, office, wealth, and the law of the household.

Women can exercise influence through correspondence, salon, marriage, patronage, or household management while remaining constrained by legal and ceremonial rules. Men of rank can still be excluded by office or timing. Later scenes must identify the route of influence rather than assuming that public precedence or private affection automatically produces a decision.

Sources: Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Display, cost, and political legitimacy {#display-cost-and-political-legitimacy}

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Splendor is a political claim supported by budgets, labor, textiles, food, transport, and police.

A ceremony can reassure allies, discipline officials, or provoke resentment. The setting must show who pays, who prepares, who performs, and who bears the cost. A later memoir's image of effortless grandeur should not erase the clerks, seamstresses, guards, cooks, musicians, and suppliers who make the display possible.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/; Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/.

## Household testimony and private intimacy {#household-testimony-and-private-intimacy}

**Status:** Work boundary. Bedroom, medical, and family scenes require individual primary or critical sources.

Court ceremony may reveal public status while hiding private feeling. Later memoirs and gossip need attribution, and a scene must identify whether a statement is witnessed, remembered, or invented for the fiction. No household employee becomes an all-knowing narrator merely because later historians found the person's name in an archive.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon correspondence, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Boundary to family and court storylines {#boundary-to-family-and-court-storylines}

**Status:** Work boundary. Detailed marriages, children, medical life, and household conflicts belong to their own character and narrative owners.

This file hands forward the physical and institutional grammar of court: access, rank, gender, labor, spectacle, and partial information. Later documents must cite the particular room, office, ceremony, or testimony they use rather than treating “the court” as a single speaking character.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/.
