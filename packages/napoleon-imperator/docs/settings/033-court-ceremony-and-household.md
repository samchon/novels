# Court Ceremony and Household

This file owns court as an institution of access, rank, gender, and performance.

## From Consular residence to imperial court {#from-consular-residence-to-imperial-court}
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#scope-preservation This H2 owns residence, household offices, apartments, audiences, invitations, ushers, chamberlains, guards, and calendars. -->

<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked household office, audience, and palace-space distinctions. -->

**Status:** Source-supported. Court develops through offices, rooms, schedules, and ranked thresholds, not through proximity alone.

An imperial household is an administrative machine as well as a social scene. An invitation must be issued, an appointment recorded, an usher or chamberlain informed, a guard satisfied, and a route opened before a visitor reaches an audience. A person admitted to a palace may still stop in an antechamber; a family member may have intimacy without policy control; a servant may carry a letter without reading it. The setting therefore names the office, gate, document, escort, time, and interruption that turn physical nearness into possible access. Court calendars and apartments are evidence of a system, not proof that every petitioner reached the Emperor or changed his decision.

**Status:** Source-supported. Ceremony is political technology: it makes hierarchy visible while relying on material labor and selective witness.

The 1810 marriage chronology separates Marie-Louise's presentation to the court on 31 March, the civil marriage at Saint-Cloud on 1 April, the religious ceremony in the Louvre's transformed Salon carré on 2 April, the banquet and theatre, and the reception in the throne room on 3 April before state bodies, princes, officials, and palace ladies. Each event had a place, schedule, architect, officiant, guest list, costume, and backstage labor. A slight in precedence can be diplomatic information; a splendid room can conceal expense or shortage. Scenarios must stage preparation, servants, guards, and waiting alongside public display and distinguish the announced schedule from what each participant actually saw.

**Status:** Source-critical. Household workers carry letters, rooms, food, bodies, and fragments of knowledge, not a complete life of the sovereign.

A secretary sees documents; a valet sees clothing and schedules; a lady of the household may hear a conversation in one room; a physician sees a body at a particular appointment; a cook, guard, or visitor has another route and duty. Each can shape access by carrying, delaying, misplacing, or refusing something, yet each has reasons to remain silent and a later testimony may be edited. The setting records worker, duty, location, access, audience, and date. A servant's name in an archive does not make the servant an all-knowing narrator, and a later memoir does not erase the threshold through which information passed.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Court access is a spatial system of rooms, doors, keys, guards, routes, and interruptions.

The Compiègne account identifies apartments designed for princely couples and records the imperial court's return there with the King of Rome; the 1810 marriage account describes the Louvre Salon carré transformed into a chapel and the Tuileries theatre into a banquet hall. Those details make rooms operational: a visitor can enter a palace without reaching the Emperor, a note can cross a threshold without its carrier reading it, and a ceremony can temporarily change a room's jurisdiction and movement pattern. **Status:** Work canon with source-critical limits. Court authority is structured by gender, title, kinship, office, wealth, and law.

A woman may act through correspondence, salon, marriage, household management, or patronage while lacking a formal office; a man of rank may still be excluded by timing, precedence, or an absent appointment. The 1810 marriage sequence shows Marie-Louise entering the court through presentation, civil and religious ceremonies, reception, and dynastic expectation, but those public roles do not automatically reveal private authority. The setting names the route of influence—letter, office, kinship, financial control, salon, or direct audience—and keeps public precedence separate from affection and legal power. No household member becomes politically decisive merely by being close to the imperial couple.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Splendor is a political claim supported by money, labor, materials, transport, and police.

**Status:** Work boundary. Bedroom, medical, and family scenes require individual primary or critical sources, not generic court atmosphere.

Court ceremony can reveal public status while hiding private feeling. A physician's note, a servant's later memoir, a family letter, and a gossip report have different access, purpose, and reliability. Later narrative must identify whether a statement was witnessed, remembered, transmitted, or invented for the fiction. A household employee's archival name is not permission to know every room, and intimacy is not evidence of policy control.
