# The Church and the Papacy

This file records the rupture between Napoleon and Pope Pius VII — the annexation of the Papal States, the excommunication, the Pope's arrest and years of captivity, and the governance crisis it created for a Catholic empire — that any scene touching faith, clergy, or the empire's relationship with Rome between 1808 and 1814 must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`. It extends `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#concordat-1801` forward from the 1801 settlement rather than repeating it. Pius VII's figure-level unit is at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#pius-vii`. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Annexation of the Papal States {#church-annexation}

**Standing: established by research.** French troops under General Miollis occupied Rome on 2 February 1808; a decree of 2 April 1808 detached the Marches (Urbino, Ancona, Macerata, Camerino) from the Papal States and annexed them to the Kingdom of Italy, which Pius VII protested as an "iniquitous decree." The formal annexation followed on 17 May 1809, signed by Napoleon from Schönbrunn during the Wagram campaign: Article 1 declared "les états du pape sont réunis à l'empire français" (the states of the Pope are reunited to the French Empire), and Article 5 promised the Pope's own household lands and revenues would be maintained up to an annual income of 2 million francs. Napoleon's stated justification, given in his own instructions on how the Senate report should present the matter, rested on a revisionist reading of the Donation of Charlemagne: that when Charlemagne made the popes temporal sovereigns, he intended them to hold Rome as vassals of the empire, not as independent rulers, so the annexation was a restoration of an interrupted feudal relationship rather than a conquest — a parallel this canon notes Napoleon drew consciously, signing the decree from Schönbrunn, seat of the old Holy Roman Empire, as a self-styled second Charlemagne. A second decree of the same date created an extraordinary consulta to administer the transition, headed by Miollis as president and Salicetti as vice-president, with Joachim Murat, King of Naples, placed in overall charge of the operation.

XIX, p. 13 ("Note pour M. le comte de Champagny... 17 mai 1809") and Napoleon's Charlemagne-vassalage framing; this canon has not independently verified the decree text against a second edition of the Correspondance or a Bulletin des lois reproduction and flags it as read only through d'Haussonville's quotation.

## The excommunication: *Quum memoranda*, 10 June 1809 {#church-excommunication}

**Standing: established by research, with the bull's intended personal target genuinely disputed.** Pius VII responded to the annexation with the bull *Quum memoranda*, dated 10 June 1809 — prepared in two alternate versions in advance of the seizure of Rome (one citing violation of the apostolic palace and the Pope's own sacrilegious deportation as grounds, the other citing only his "simple déchéance comme souverain temporel," his mere loss of temporal sovereignty) and signed both, so that whichever contingency occurred a bull was ready. As promulgated, it excommunicated "tous ceux qui avaient donné l'ordre, tous les fauteurs, conseillers ou adhérents, tous ceux enfin qui avaient facilité l'exécution" of the seizure — all who had given the order, all abettors, counselors, or adherents, all who had facilitated its execution — but did not name Napoleon anywhere in its text; Napoleon's own minister of cults, Bigot de Préameneu, reporting to the emperor on the bull's canonical scope, confirmed as much directly: "votre majesté n'est nulle part nommée" (your majesty is nowhere named). Historians dispute whether Pius VII intended the deliberately unspecific "auteurs et fauteurs" formula to strike Napoleon personally in strict canon law or left it ambiguous by design; what is not disputed is that Napoleon received it as "une mortelle injure," a mortal insult, regardless of the technical drafting. Since ordinary channels were blocked, the bull was posted in broad daylight during vespers on the walls of St. Peter's, Santa Maria Maggiore, and St. John Lateran, without any of the men who posted it being caught by the French consulta's police. Napoleon's own reaction, in a letter to Murat six days later: "Vous avez vu par mes décrets que j'ai fait beaucoup de bien au pape; mais c'est à condition qu'il se tiendra tranquille" (you have seen from my decrees that I have done the Pope much good; but that is on condition that he keeps quiet) — and he separately ordered Bigot de Préameneu to suppress any publicity for the bull within France and moved preemptively against itinerant missionary priests as likely couriers for it. Bigot de Préameneu's own canonical assessment to the emperor judged the bull, in practice, "une vaine et inutile protestation," a vain and useless protest — a verdict events did not bear out, since it permanently poisoned relations. Pius VII himself, after his own arrest less than a month later, told Cardinal Pacca with evident satisfaction: "nous avons bien fait de publier la bulle du 10 juin, car aujourd'hui comment ferions-nous?" (we did well to publish the bull of 10 June, for how could we do it today?).

Sources: Peter Hicks, "Napoléon et le pape Pie VII: du Concordat signé en juillet 1801 à l'excommunication de l'empereur en juin 1809," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, citing Bernardine Melchior-Bonnet, *Napoléon et le Pape* (Paris: Amiot Dumont, 1958), p. 122, for the excommunication clause's actual wording and its omission of Napoleon's name; d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 11-12, read in full (the two advance-drafted versions per Cardinal Pacca's memoirs; the bull's incipit "Pius. P. P. VII, ad perpetuam memoriam... Quum memoranda"; the posting on the three basilicas; Bigot de Préameneu's "nulle part nommée" reassurance and his "vaine et inutile protestation" verdict; Napoleon's 17 June 1809 letter to Murat and his 12 September 1809 order against itinerant missionaries; Pius VII's later remark to Pacca).

## The Pope's arrest, 5-6 July 1809 {#church-arrest}

**Standing: established by research, with the precise chain of orders — whether a written order to seize the Pope himself ever existed — flagged as unresolved.** Napoleon, from Schönbrunn, directed General Étienne Radet, then in Tuscany, to proceed to Rome; Miollis gave Radet only a written order to arrest Cardinal Pacca, while the order to seize the Pope himself, if he resisted evacuation, was given only verbally, and no unambiguous written order to that specific effect survives in either scholarly account this canon consulted. The operation began before dawn on 5 July with troop movements disguised as routine patrols; the signal was given at 2:35 a.m. on 6 July once the night sentry on the Quirinal tower withdrew at first light, and Radet's men breached the palace with ladders (two broke) and forced a side door of the Daterie, the roughly forty-man Swiss Guard offering no resistance under standing orders. Radet found the Pope with Cardinals Pacca and Despuig, wearing the pontifical ring Pius VI had worn during his own French captivity a decade earlier — a detail this canon treats as symbolically loaded rather than incidental. After a silence of over five minutes, Radet, visibly shaken, spoke first: "Très saint-père, je viens au nom de mon gouvernement réitérer à votre sainteté la proposition de renoncer officiellement à son pouvoir temporel" (Most Holy Father, I come in the name of my government to reiterate to your holiness the proposal that you officially renounce your temporal power). Pius VII refused: "Nous ne pouvons ni céder ni abandonner ce qui n'est pas à nous. Le temporel appartient à l'église" (we can neither cede nor abandon what is not ours; the temporal belongs to the Church). He did not physically resist and was removed with Pacca, taken via the Certosa at Florence toward captivity. On learning of the arrest on 18 July, Napoleon wrote to Fouché calling it a "grande folie," a great folly — a reaction this canon records without resolving whether it reflects genuine surprise at Radet's overreach or a calculated later distancing from an outcome Napoleon's own ambiguous instructions had made likely; Napoleon maintained at St. Helena, to Las Cases, that he had never ordered the arrest, a claim this canon's sources treat with open skepticism given the surviving correspondence.

Sources: d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 11, read in full, drawing on General Radet's own two written accounts (13 July 1809, to the Minister of War; 12 September 1814, to Pius VII himself, under the Restoration) and Pacca's own memoirs and the Italian manuscript relation of Pius VII's valet de chambre for the confrontation dialogue; Peter Hicks, "Napoléon et le pape Pie VII," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, for the 19 June 1809 letters to Murat and Miollis and their silence on seizing the Pope specifically, and for Napoleon's "grande folie" reaction and his later St. Helena denial.

## Captivity at Savona, 1809-1812 {#church-savona}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon's own instructions, given while the Pope was still en route, fixed the destination: "mon intention est que le pape n'entre pas en France... le meilleur endroit pour le placer serait Savone. Il y a là une grande maison où il serait assez convenablement" (my intention is that the Pope not enter France... the best place to put him would be Savona; there is a large house there where he would be reasonably comfortable) — an administratively convenient choice (a large residence, on recently annexed Ligurian territory, easily isolated) rather than a symbolically chosen one, and Napoleon wavered before settling on it, briefly ordering the Pope diverted to Grenoble before regretting the order: "cela aurait l'air de se jouer de ce vieillard" (it would look like toying with this old man). Even under close official supervision the journey through southern France became, in this canon's sources' words, "une véritable ovation pour le saint-père," a genuine ovation for the Holy Father, especially at Avignon — evidence of strong popular Catholic sentiment surviving official suppression. At Savona, Pius VII was kept under close surveillance by General Berthier (comte César, no relation to the marshal) and the local prefect; the Austrian envoy Lebzeltern, reporting to Metternich after a spring 1810 conversation with the Pope, recorded his own words on his isolation: "Quoique détenu ici sans correspondance libre, sans nouvelles, excepté les nouvelles très vagues que nous puisons dans quelques feuilles détachées du Moniteur... C'est un vrai schisme établi par le gouvernement" (though detained here without free correspondence, without news, except the very vague news we draw from a few loose sheets of the Moniteur... this is a true schism established by the government). He nonetheless maintained a clandestine correspondence with French bishops, chiefly through the bishop of Savona acting informally as his agent, tolerated by Berthier and the prefect until it grew too active — from 1810 the prefect was ordered to seize and register all documents before they reached the Pope, and some correspondence was smuggled out via a valet to Lyon and Paris (the abbé d'Astros affair), leading to clergy interrogations and arrests in Paris.

Sources: d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 11 and 15, read in full, quoting Napoleon's instructions on the choice of Savona and the reversed Grenoble order; the Avignon reception, per Fouché's own precautions failing to suppress it; and Lebzeltern's dispatches to Metternich, quoted at length from Pacca's and the Austrian diplomatic record, for the conditions of isolation, the abbé d'Astros smuggling affair, and Savary's interrogation of d'Astros ("Donnez votre démission, ou vous êtes mon prisonnier" / "Je serai alors votre prisonnier" — give your resignation, or you are my prisoner / then I shall be your prisoner).

## Transfer to Fontainebleau and its Concordat, 1812-1814 {#church-fontainebleau}

**Standing: established by research, except the exact date of the transfer itself from Savona to Fontainebleau, which this canon's research could not pin down and does not assert.** Pius VII was moved to Fontainebleau by 1812; under direct pressure and personal humiliation — Napoleon reportedly taunted him, of the still-imprisoned Cardinal di Pietro, "Quand le cardinal di Pietro sera arrivé, vous irez vous confesser à lui" (when Cardinal di Pietro arrives, you will go and confess to him) — he was induced to sign a new concordat on the evening of 25 January 1813, its drafting having taken five days. Its terms: the Pope would exercise canonical institution of newly nominated bishops within six months of nomination, after which the right would devolve automatically to the metropolitan or senior prelate of the province without further papal consent; in exchange the Pope regained direct nomination to the six suburbicarian sees of Rome and ten further bishoprics to be designated later. Napoleon began executing the concordat's terms the same morning the Pope signed, issuing instructions to Miollis in Rome before the ink was dry. The retraction came quickly: driven above all by Cardinal Consalvi, who pressed a majority of the cardinals toward "une rétractation prompte et entière" over the objections of two cardinals who feared it would only harden Napoleon's position, Pius VII wrote to Napoleon on 24 March 1813 retracting his consent — "la crainte des jugemens de Dieu, dont notre grand âge et le dépérissement de notre santé nous rapprochent tous les jours davantage, doit nous rendre supérieur à toute considération" (the fear of God's judgment, which our great age and failing health bring us nearer every day, must make us superior to every other consideration) — and Pacca records him receiving the advice to retract "avec joie" despite its pain, feeling "soulagé... du poids énorme qui l'avait oppressé jour et nuit," relieved of the enormous weight that had oppressed him day and night. A further, sharper letter to the cardinals declared void every institution granted under the retracted concordat and protested the imperial decrees of 13 February and 25 March 1813 that had implemented it. As French power collapsed in January 1814, Napoleon offered the Pope, in a letter delivered 20 January, full restoration of his temporal sovereignty as it stood before the revolutionary wars — while his actual secret instructions to the escorting officer Lagorse directed a deliberately circuitous route back toward Savona rather than Rome, keeping the Pope as a potential bargaining counter in case of a French recovery. Pius VII made his triumphal return entry into Rome on 24 May 1814, the crowd at the Vatican breaking into what d'Haussonville's account calls "mille acclamations frénétiques."

Sources: d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 21-24, read in full, quoting the Fontainebleau concordat's terms (via *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier*, t. XXIV, p. 450), Napoleon's taunt about di Pietro, the 24 March 1813 retraction letter, Pacca's account of the Pope's relief, the January 1814 offer of restoration set against Napoleon's contradicting secret instructions to Lagorse, and the 24 May 1814 entry into Rome — all quoted from d'Haussonville's own reproduction of the Correspondance and Pacca's memoirs, not independently verified against a second edition.

## The National Council of Paris, June-July 1811 {#church-national-council}

**Standing: established by research.** With Pius VII a prisoner refusing canonical institution to bishops Napoleon nominated, sees across the Empire and annexed German territory stood vacant or under-administered; Napoleon's own frustration is recorded directly: "Des évêchés sont devenus vacans; j'ai usé dans la forme ordinaire de mon droit de nomination... Il refuse obstinément de donner l'institution canonique. En Allemagne, la religion est perdue par sa faute" (sees have become vacant; I have exercised my right of nomination in the ordinary form... he obstinately refuses to grant canonical institution; in Germany, religion is being lost through his fault). Rather than settle the matter by simple Senate decree, Napoleon convened a national council of the French and Italian church, judging the question one of ecclesiastical rather than civil competence. It opened 17 June 1811 at Notre-Dame, deliberately timed with the legislative session, comprising 95 prelates — six cardinals, eight archbishops, and eighty-one bishops — representing less than half the combined sees of the two churches. Friction was immediate: at the opening roll call, the Archbishop of Bordeaux answered "Oui, je le veux," but added "sauf toutefois l'obéissance due au souverain pontife" (yes, I consent — but always saving the obedience due the sovereign pontiff), a qualification that so angered Napoleon that Cardinal Fesch, the presiding uncle, was summoned to Saint-Cloud and treated "avec la dernière dureté," with the utmost harshness, and the Moniteur was ordered to print nothing of the opening at all. The substantive proposal put to the council essentially anticipated the later Fontainebleau mechanism: if the Pope arbitrarily refused institution, the right would devolve, provisionally and until an ecumenical council decided otherwise, to the metropolitans. Napoleon refused to permit any deputation to negotiate with the Pope at Savona until the council itself had voted a formal decree along these lines; a minority led by the Bishop of Ghent pushed instead to lead with a demand for the Pope's personal liberty, voted down as impolitic, with only a note of unanimous assent "en principe" to the idea entered in the minutes. When several bishops, including the Bishop of Ghent, insisted the council itself — not a closed commission working with the ministers of cults — must draft and vote its own official acts, Napoleon had three of the council's leading members imprisoned in the donjon of Vincennes and dissolved the council outright, proceeding instead to negotiate with individual bishops one by one — d'Haussonville's own summary judgment on the episode is that Napoleon resorted to "voie de captation individuelle," a method of individual co-optation, having failed to extract a clean collective solution.

Sources: d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 16-20, read in full, quoting Napoleon's own frustration over vacant sees, the council's opening composition and date, the Bordeaux archbishop's qualified assent and Fesch's summons to Saint-Cloud, the six-month devolution proposal, the Bishop of Ghent's rival push for the Pope's liberty, and the council's dissolution and the imprisonment of three of its members at Vincennes.

## The excommunicate empire: general texture {#church-excommunicate-empire}

**Standing: established by research.** Ruling a Catholic empire under a standing (if not personally named) excommunication created concrete, recurring friction rather than a purely symbolic embarrassment. The clearest single episode is the "cardinaux noirs" affair of 2 April 1810: thirteen cardinals, including Ercole Consalvi, refused to attend Napoleon's religious wedding to Marie-Louise (`settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md#marriage-april-ceremonies`), in protest at the Church's treatment. Napoleon's fury was immediate: he dictated a note accusing them of "le même esprit de rébellion qu'ils ont manifesté depuis dix ans, et qui a obligé sa majesté à s'emparer de Rome, et qui les a induits à porter le pape à fulminer contre lui une excommunication qui est la risée des contemporains" (the same spirit of rebellion they have shown for ten years, which obliged his majesty to seize Rome, and which led them to induce the Pope to fulminate against him an excommunication that is the laughingstock of his contemporaries) — a formulation notable for openly naming the excommunication as directed at himself, whatever the bull's own text avoided. The thirteen were stripped of cardinalatial red on the spot, reduced to ordinary black clerical dress (hence "black cardinals," as distinct from the loyalist "red cardinals"), had their property sequestered, and lived thereafter off charitable subsidies from pious sympathizers; this canon reached this specific episode's count and consequences only through two independently-written English-language tertiary summaries (both citing the Catholic Encyclopedia's Napoleon entry, itself unreachable in this research pass) rather than through a primary document or a Fondation Napoléon article, and flags the figure of thirteen and the exile-to-Reims detail for Consalvi specifically as needing independent confirmation before being treated as fully settled. More broadly attested: Napoleon moved preemptively to intercept the Pope's letters and the excommunication bull's circulation, distrusting itinerant clergy as harder to police than settled parish priests; foreign Catholic courts, Austria's chief among them, had to weigh the canonical propriety of Napoleon's Marie-Louise marriage — arranged with a Pope simultaneously his prisoner — far more carefully than Orthodox Russia had needed to for the earlier, failed Russian marriage negotiation, a live diplomatic sensitivity this canon records as part of what made the Austrian match faster to close in some respects and more canonically fraught in others; and popular demonstrations of Catholic loyalty to the captive Pope recurred spontaneously along his transport routes despite official suppression, with police reports to Napoleon noting comparable "fermentation" in Catholic Belgium.

Sources: d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat," ch. 12 and 16, read in full, for the clergy-surveillance measures, the Austrian court's greater canonical sensitivity relative to Russia's, and the Belgian "fermentation" reports; Wikipedia, "Pope Pius VII," and "Ercole Consalvi," both opened directly and cross-checked against each other, for the "cardinaux noirs" episode's scale (thirteen cardinals, Consalvi named, exile to Reims), both citing the Catholic Encyclopedia's Napoleon I entry as their ultimate source, which this canon could not itself open (newadvent.org returned no readable content) — this canon flags the episode's core (a mass cardinal boycott of the 2 April 1810 wedding, punished by loss of red vestments, property sequestration, and exile) as corroborated by two independent tertiary summaries, but the specific count and Consalvi's Reims exile as not independently verified against a primary or specialist-institutional source.
