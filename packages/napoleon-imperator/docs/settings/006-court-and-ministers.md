# Court and Ministers

This file gives figure-level units for Napoleon's principal civilian ministers and household officers, and relation-level units for the bonds among them, per `principles/settings.md#figures-and-relations`. Institutional roles (Conseil d'État, the ministries themselves) are at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md`.

## Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand {#talleyrand}

**Standing: established by research, with the "worse than a crime, a blunder" epigram flagged as disputed attribution.** Born 1754. A childhood fall dislocated his foot, leaving a permanent limp that closed off the expected military career and redirected him, via his father's choice, into the Church — Bishop of Autun by 1788. Legendarily venal, continuous with *ancien régime* norms of being paid for state favors (the 1797–98 XYZ Affair against American envoys is the most notorious instance). Foreign Minister by 1805 and central to Napoleon's diplomacy, but privately doubtful of Napoleon's overreach well before his overt 1807–08 turn toward the Tsar: on 17 October 1805 he presented Napoleon a plan for a lenient peace with Austria to preserve European equilibrium, rejected outright, and again urged leniency after Ulm, again rebuffed and accused by Napoleon of being "too soft." The Austrian minister Metternich reported in 1805 that Talleyrand privately intended "to oppose with all his influence... the destructive projects undertaken by Napoleon." The famous line "It was worse than a crime, it was a blunder," commonly attributed to him regarding the 1804 execution of the Duc d'Enghien, is disputed among sources — variously credited instead to the deputy Antoine Boulay de la Meurthe or to Fouché; this canon treats it as available only as an anonymous or disputed period remark, never as a confirmed Talleyrand quotation.

Sources: [Hoover Institution — "The Indispensable Talleyrand"](https://www.hoover.org/research/indispensable-talleyrand) (Metternich's 1805 report and the October 1805 peace-plan rejection, drawing on Duff Cooper's biography); [Quote Investigator — "It Was Worse Than a Crime; It Was a Blunder"](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2026/05/06/crime-blunder/) (earliest print attribution, 1807, credits Antoine Boulay de la Meurthe; Talleyrand himself was not credited until 1822, with no evidence he ever claimed it).

## Joseph Fouché {#fouche}

**Standing: established by research, with the quoted exchange flagged as secondhand and datable only to 1815.** A former Jacobin "de-Christianizer" and regicide who voted for Louis XVI's execution in January 1793 and built a fearsome reputation as a Terror-era representative-on-mission. Confirmed as Minister of Police early in the Consulate, he built an extensive surveillance apparatus so intrusive that Talleyrand, Lucien, and Joseph Bonaparte jointly pressured for its formal suppression in September 1802 (later revived by 1804). Mutual distrust with Napoleon was constant: reinstated in 1804 but never fully trusted, on the belief that Fouché kept private dossiers even on the Emperor. A famous exchange — Napoleon: "Monsieur Duke of Otranto, I ought to have you hanged!"; Fouché: "Sire, I do not share Your Majesty's opinion" — is recorded only via Lucien Bonaparte's later recollection and should be treated as reported anecdote, not verbatim transcript. It also cannot be used in an 1805-set scene at all: the address "Duke of Otranto" is a title Fouché did not receive until 1809, and the secondary literature places the exchange in the Hundred Days of 1815, when Napoleon suspected him of dealing simultaneously with Metternich and Wellington. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#naming-conventions`, which forbids retrospective titles, the line belongs to 1815 and no earlier.

Sources: [napoleon-empire.org — "Joseph Fouché"](https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/fouche.php) (the 1802 pressure campaign and its 1804 reversal); the "I ought to have you hanged" exchange remains sourced only to Lucien Bonaparte's later personal recollection, with no independent corroboration located, and is treated as reported anecdote rather than verbatim transcript accordingly.

## Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès {#cambaceres}

**Standing: established by research, with one common misattribution corrected.** Second Consul (1799–1804), then Arch-Chancellor of the Empire, later Duke of Parma (1808). Appointed for his deep legal expertise, he was the principal architect of the Napoleonic Civil Code (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`), procedurally meticulous and personally cautious — valued by Napoleon precisely for restraining his impulsiveness. Openly known to be attracted to men, unmarried, and surrounded by a household of young male secretaries and companions without particular effort at concealment, tolerated at the highest levels of the regime. He is often, incorrectly, credited with personally decriminalizing sodomy in France; in fact the National Constituent Assembly had already removed it from criminal law in 1791, before his rise to power — his own toleration reflects social and political practice, not a personal legal reform, and this canon does not attribute the 1791 decriminalization to him.

Sources: Wikipedia, "French Penal Code of 1791," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Penal_Code_of_1791, and Wikipedia, "Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques-R%C3%A9gis_de_Cambac%C3%A9r%C3%A8s — establishing that the National Constituent Assembly's penal code of 1791 simply omitted sodomy from its list of offences, years before Cambacérès held national office, and that the Code he did draft was the *civil* code, which did not govern sexual offences at all. The specialist scholarship correcting this misattribution is Michael Sibalis, "The Regulation of Male Homosexuality in Revolutionary and Napoleonic France, 1789–1815," in Jeffrey Merrick and Bryant T. Ragan Jr. (eds.), *Homosexuality in Modern France* (Oxford University Press, 1996); this canon has not obtained that chapter and names it as the standing reference rather than citing it as read.

## Dominique Vivant Denon {#denon}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 4 January 1747, Givry, near Chalon-sur-Saône. A multi-talented courtier, artist, and diplomat under the *ancien régime* with prior missions to St Petersburg (1772), Stockholm (as secretary to Ambassador Vergennes), and Switzerland (1775, where he met Voltaire at Ferney), followed by a Naples posting running from attaché in 1776 to chargé d'affaires until 1785; accompanied the Egyptian expedition (1798–99, `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-egypt`), producing on-the-spot sketches under fire, joining the Institut d'Égypte in Cairo, and pushing into Upper Egypt as far as the Nile cataracts with Desaix's column, then published the influential *Voyage dans la basse et la haute Égypte* (1802), helping ignite European "Egyptomania." Appointed 19 November 1802 director-general of museums, effectively head of the Musée Napoléon (the Louvre). This canon corrects a date it previously carried: his tenure did not end in 1814 but survived the First Restoration of that year intact, ending only on 3 October 1815, when he resigned to the restored Louis XVIII after Waterloo and the Allies' demand for restitution of the art he had helped acquire. The Louvre's Denon Wing is named for him.

Sources: napoleon.org, "DENON, Dominique-Vivant, baron (1747–1825), directeur du louvre," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/denon-dominique-vivant/ (Charles-Otto Zieseniss, originally published in *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien*, no. 357, February 1988; birth date and place, the diplomatic missions, the Egyptian expedition and Desaix's Upper Egypt column, and the tenure's actual end on 3 October 1815 — the basis for the correction above); vivantdenon.fr, "Le Consulat et l'Empire," https://www.vivantdenon.fr/biographie/le-consulat-et-l-empire/, a site dedicated specifically to Denon, independently confirming both the 19 November 1802 appointment and the 3 October 1815 resignation, and stating explicitly that his functions were maintained by Louis XVIII through the First Restoration; Wikipedia, "Dominique Vivant Denon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique_Vivant,_Baron_Denon, cross-checked for the 19 November 1802 appointment date.

## Géraud-Christophe-Michel Duroc {#duroc}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 25 October 1772, three years younger than Napoleon, from an impoverished Lorraine family; joined Napoleon as aide-de-camp during the first Italian campaign. Reserved, patient, and sweet-natured by contemporary account — the temperamental opposite of Napoleon and one of very few men able to check his angry impulses. Governor of the Tuileries, then formally appointed Grand Marshal of the Palace on 18 May 1804 — this canon corrects a date it previously carried of "July 1804": the appointment fell on the same day the Senate proclaimed the Empire — running the imperial household; also used as a trusted diplomat on missions to Prussia, Russia, and Denmark. Napoleon reportedly called him a friend "of twenty years' standing" who was "never anything but a comfort to me," a line this canon has located only via secondary quotation (Roberts) rather than the published *Correspondance* itself, and flags accordingly. For later reference: wounded by a ricocheting cannonball at the Combat of Reichenbach near Görlitz on 22 May 1813 — a rearguard action fought two days after Bautzen itself, not at Bautzen as this canon previously stated — and died of the wound some twelve hours later, in the early hours of 23 May 1813, a loss Napoleon took especially hard.

Sources: napoleon.org, "DUROC, Géraud-Christophe-Michel, général (1772–1813), grand maréchal du Palais," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/duroc-geraud-christophe-michel/ (Jacques Garnier, *Dictionnaire Napoléon*, ed. Jean Tulard, Fayard, 1999, corrected per Duroc biographer Danielle Meyrueix; reproduces the *Le Moniteur*, 30 May 1813 account of the death in full); napoleon.org, "Le grand maréchal du Palais Duroc," https://www.napoleon.org/jeunes-historiens/napodoc/le-grand-marechal-du-palais-duroc/, confirming the 18 May 1804 appointment date — the basis for the correction above; Britannica, "Géraud-Christophe-Michel Duroc, duke de Frioul," https://britannica.com/print/article/174344, confirming death 23 May 1813 at Markersdorf near Görlitz, Silesia; historyofwar.org, "Combat of Reichenbach, 22 May 1813," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/combat_reichenbach.html, for the wounding at Reichenbach/Markersdorf on 22 May and death roughly twelve hours later — the basis for the Bautzen correction above. The "friend of twenty years' standing" quotation is carried by Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life* (per `settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-roberts`), and has not been independently verified against the *Correspondance générale*.

## Anne-Jean-Marie-René Savary {#savary}

**Standing: established by research.** Sent by Napoleon on 25 November 1805 to the Allied headquarters at Olmütz — a week before Austerlitz (2 December 1805), not immediately before it, this canon's own correction against a date it previously compressed — for a calculated diplomatic feint: ostensibly to negotiate an armistice, actually to reconnoiter Allied dispositions and to persuade Tsar Alexander and the Austrians that Napoleon feared battle and wanted terms, deliberately acting nervous and evasive. The ruse worked, convincing Allied command that Napoleon's position was weak and encouraging the aggressive dispositions that led to their defeat; full campaign context, including the mission's precise chronology and Napoleon's own letters, is at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-diplomatic-feint`.

Sources: historyofwar.org, "General Anne Jean Marie René Savary, duc de Rovigo (26 April 1774–1833)," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_savary.html (the dual-purpose mission and the deliberate nervous, evasive performance); Wikipedia, "Battle of Austerlitz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Austerlitz ("General Savary was sent to the Allied headquarters at Olmütz... on 25 November 1805... while secretly examining the Allied forces' situation" — the basis for the date correction above); frenchempire.net, "Anne-Jean-Marie-René Savary (1774–1833)," https://www.frenchempire.net/biographies/savary/, corroborating the armistice-negotiation cover story.

## Armand de Caulaincourt {#caulaincourt}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact month of his 1811 recall from St. Petersburg disputed between sources.** Born 9 December 1773, Caulaincourt, Picardy (died 19 February 1827, Paris), son of a general from an old Picardy noble family. Sent to St. Petersburg on a diplomatic mission in 1801 by Foreign Minister Talleyrand before becoming one of Napoleon's aides-de-camp; appointed Master of the Horse (Grand Écuyer) in June 1804, a post he holds until 1813, and created Duke of Vicenza in 1808. Ambassador to Russia from November 1807 into 1811 (Britannica gives February 1811; historyofwar.org gives May 1811; napoleon-empire.org gives June 1811 — this canon does not silently pick one and instead notes the range), where he worked incessantly to preserve the Franco-Russian peace against what he read as Napoleon's arbitrary drift toward confrontation, and developed a genuine personal friendship with Tsar Alexander I in the process — knowledge and a relationship this canon establishes as making him the one man in Napoleon's circle who argued in advance, from direct first-hand acquaintance with Russia rather than staff assumption, that the 1812 invasion would fail. He is later Foreign Minister, 20 November 1813 to 1 April 1814, and again briefly during the Hundred Days, 20 March to 22 June 1815. Per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-caulaincourt`, historians rate his memoirs the most reliable of the major memoirists precisely because he wrote from contemporaneous notes rather than distant retrospect, and this canon treats that same clear-eyed, unflattering candor as his defining trait as a character, not only as a documentary source: he tells Napoleon what he believes to be true rather than what Napoleon wants to hear, and stays anyway. His younger brother, Auguste-Jean-Gabriel de Caulaincourt, took command of the II Reserve Cavalry Corps at Borodino after its commander was killed, led the charge that carried the Great Redoubt, and was killed entering the position himself (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-chronology`); when the news reached headquarters, Napoleon asked Armand directly whether he wished to retire, and Armand refused. This canon treats the loss as inseparable from the sustained viewpoint access he is granted from 1812 onward (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#focal-figures`): the clarity this canon rates his testimony for is the clarity of a man who had already paid Russia's price, in his own family, before Moscow ever fell.

Sources: [historyofwar.org — "General Armand-Augustin-Louis, marquis de Caulaincourt"](https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_caulaincourt_armand.html); [napoleon-empire.org — "Armand-Augustin-Louis de Caulaincourt"](https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/caulaincourt.php); Wikipedia, "Armand-Augustin-Louis de Caulaincourt," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armand-Augustin-Louis_de_Caulaincourt, citing Jean Hanoteau's 1933 edition of the memoirs for the family and military background, for the precise Foreign Minister dates (20 November 1813 – 1 April 1814; 20 March – 22 June 1815) and the 1808 Duke of Vicenza creation — this canon does not adopt that same page's claim that Caulaincourt succeeded Duroc as Grand Marshal of the Palace in January 1813, since Duroc did not die until 23 May 1813 (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#duroc`) and the office in fact passed to Bertrand (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#bertrand`); that specific claim is treated as a sourcing error in the fetched summary and not repeated.

## General Henri-Gratien Bertrand {#bertrand}

**Standing: established by research, with one detail this canon previously assumed — a brief return to France before choosing exile — not adopted for lack of a source.** Born 22 March 1773, Châteauroux (died 31 January 1844, same city). Appointed the third and final Grand Marshal of the Palace at the end of 1813, succeeding Duroc (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#duroc`) in the office that ran Napoleon's household; he accompanied Napoleon continuously through the exile on Elba in 1814 and the return in 1815, and this canon does not adopt a story of any interruption in that accompaniment, having found no source for one despite having assumed it in an earlier pass. Condemned to death in absentia by the restored Bourbon government in 1816 for following Napoleon during the Hundred Days, he lived under that sentence throughout the entire Saint Helena exile before receiving amnesty on his return to France after Napoleon's death — a real, documented legal cost to a loyalty every source this canon has read describes as total: he wants Napoleon served and protected before he wants anything for himself, and this canon establishes no instance of him acting from personal ambition the way Montholon's record (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#montholon`) repeatedly does. He and his household lived apart from Longwood itself, at Hutt's Gate roughly a hundred meters away (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-household`), which left him comparatively detached from the worst of the household's internal feuding — not from indifference but because, unlike the Montholons, he had his own family to attend to and did not need Napoleon's favor to secure his own position. He returned to France with the household after the death, served as a deputy in 1830, and took part in the 1840 mission that brought Napoleon's remains home in the *retour des cendres* (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-retour-des-cendres`); he is buried at Les Invalides.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Henri Gatien Bertrand," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Gatien_Bertrand; napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?", https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/who-was-present-at-napoleons-death/, read via `fetch.sh`; `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-household`, established by research.

## General Charles-Tristan de Montholon {#montholon}

**Standing: established by research for the biographical record; the paternity of his wife's 1816 daughter and the arsenic-poisoning accusation against him are both explicitly disputed and not resolved as fact.** Born 21 July 1783, Paris (died 21 August 1853). His pre-exile record is substantially more compromised than a "checkered" reputation suggests: he falsely claimed to have taken part in the 1800 Hohenlinden campaign while in fact facing expulsion for corruption around the same period, lost his commission after only seven days in 1814 on a charge of taking money meant to pay his troops, and a commanding officer later swore under oath that a wound he claimed in action had never actually occurred — a documented pattern of fabrication and embezzlement predating Saint Helena by more than a decade, not a single youthful lapse. He wants Napoleon's trust and the material security that trust confers, and got both: over the final years he eased Bertrand out of the closest daily confidence, became Napoleon's principal companion at the very end, helped write his will, and was its most heavily rewarded beneficiary among the household (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-death`). His wife, Albine de Montholon, gave birth to a daughter, Hélène-Napoléone, on 18 June 1816; no source this canon has read settles whether Napoleon or Montholon himself was the father, and this canon records the question as genuinely open rather than adopting either reading, including a later biographer's own speculative inference that Napoleon's use of Albine as a mistress, with Montholon's knowledge, would explain the family's subsequent rewards — a speculation this canon flags as that author's own, not as established fact. Because he was Napoleon's closest attendant at the very end and stood to gain the most from the will he helped draft, modern arsenic-poisoning theorists including Ben Weider and Sten Forshufvud name Montholon specifically as the "most likely suspect" if poisoning occurred; this canon's own standing on that theory, fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`, treats it as a named minority position contradicted by the clinical absence of arsenic-specific symptoms (extreme weight loss of the arsenic pattern, nail loss, fatty liver degeneration, skin pigmentation changes, Mees' lines, renal failure) in the documented medical record, and this entry does not reopen that standing — it records only that Montholon is the specific person the minority theory names, and why.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Charles Tristan de Montholon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tristan_de_Montholon; napoleon.org, "Who was present at Napoleon's death?", as above; Barbara Krajewska, "Arsenic and the Emperor," napoleon.org, read via `fetch.sh`, for the clinical rebuttal and the direct quotation of the poisoning question; `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`, established by research, unchanged by this entry.

## Relation: Napoleon and Caulaincourt {#relation-napoleon-caulaincourt}

**Standing: established by research.** **His side:** genuine trust in Caulaincourt's competence and honesty, extended further than to almost any other adviser precisely because Caulaincourt's warnings are not self-interested — he has nothing to gain and his own reputation to lose by opposing the Russian campaign, and Napoleon takes him on the retreat sledge in 1812 specifically because he wants a companion who will speak plainly rather than flatter him in catastrophe. **Caulaincourt's side:** loyalty that survives repeated, documented disagreement — he argues against the Russian invasion beforehand, is overruled, and remains at Napoleon's side through the retreat and the abdication negotiations of 1814 regardless, a pattern this canon treats as loyalty to the man and the state rather than agreement with the decision. This canon establishes their relationship as the clearest instance in the work of a subordinate telling Napoleon an unwelcome truth and being kept close for it rather than dismissed — a genuine exception to the pattern recorded at `settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-limits`, where Napoleon otherwise struggles to update his trust in a man once it is set, and this canon treats Caulaincourt's case as the one instance where being proven right by events actually deepens rather than merely tests that trust.

Sources: [archive.org — Caulaincourt, *With Napoleon in Russia: Memoirs of General de Caulaincourt, Duke of Vicenza*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.58850/2015.58850.With-Napoleon-In-Russia-The-Memoirs-Of-General-De-Caulaincourt-Duke-Of-Vicenza_djvu.txt) (full digitized text, confirming he was the sole occupant of Napoleon's sledge on the 1812 retreat); `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-caulaincourt` and the biographical facts above, each established by research.

## Relation: Napoleon and Talleyrand {#relation-napoleon-talleyrand}

**Standing: established by research.** **His side:** heavy reliance on Talleyrand's diplomatic finesse and social polish, useful for legitimizing the new imperial court to old-regime Europe, combined with open, documented distaste for his corruption; Napoleon accuses him in 1805 of being "too soft" on defeated Austria, rejecting Talleyrand's proposed lenient settlement after Ulm. **Talleyrand's side:** growing private contempt and alarm, already articulated by October 1805 in his rejected equilibrium memorandum and reported to Vienna by Metternich as an explicit private intent to resist Napoleon's "destructive projects" — the ideological break, that Napoleon's imperial ambition is becoming reckless and undiplomatic, is traceable to 1805, years before Talleyrand's overt 1807–08 turn toward the Tsar.

Sources: [Hoover Institution — "The Indispensable Talleyrand"](https://www.hoover.org/research/indispensable-talleyrand) (the "too soft" accusation and Metternich's report); Seth J. Browner, "Visionaries in Opposition: Napoleon, Talleyrand, and the Future of France" (Trinity College senior thesis, 2017), [digitalrepository.trincoll.edu/theses/621](https://digitalrepository.trincoll.edu/theses/621/), for the 17 October 1805 Strasbourg memorandum by name.

## Relation: Napoleon and Fouché {#relation-napoleon-fouche}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** A relationship of mutual, permanent distrust that nonetheless persists because each finds the other useful: Napoleon needs Fouché's surveillance apparatus and Fouché needs Napoleon's protection from the many enemies his Terror-era record has made him. Neither side pretends to warmth; this canon establishes the relationship's register as watchful transaction rather than any variety of loyalty.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#fouche` above, established by research; the "watchful transaction" characterization itself is this canon's reading of that record.

## Relation: Talleyrand and Fouché {#relation-talleyrand-fouche}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** Open and durable rivalry between the two most powerful ministers of the regime — later documented instances (their 1814–15 collusion despite public enmity) postdate this canon's primary window, but the underlying antagonism, rooted in competing claims to indispensability and mutual contempt for the other's methods (Talleyrand's venal diplomacy versus Fouché's surveillance state), is present from the early Consulate onward and available for any scene set across the work's full span.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#talleyrand` and `#fouche` above, each established by research. Chateaubriand's characterization of their 1815 partnership — "Tout à coup une porte s'ouvre : entre silencieusement le vice appuyé sur le bras du crime, M. de Talleyrand marchant soutenu par M. Fouché" ("vice leaning on the arm of crime") — is confirmed at its exact source: *Mémoires d'outre-tombe*, tome 4, describing the July 1815 Second Restoration scene at Saint-Denis, [fr.wikisource.org — Page:Chateaubriand - Mémoires d'outre-tombe t4.djvu/69](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Chateaubriand_-_M%C3%A9moires_d%27outre-tombe_t4.djvu/69); cited here as evidence of the relationship's durable public reputation rather than as an 1805-period document.

## Relation: Napoleon and Bertrand {#relation-napoleon-bertrand}

**Standing: established by research.** His side: the closest thing this canon establishes to unconditional trust extended to a household servant rather than to a field commander — Bertrand is given control of physical access to Napoleon at Longwood (it is Bertrand who turns Hudson Lowe away at their first attempted meeting for lack of an appointment, `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-lowe-relationship`), and Napoleon's late-exile decision to exclude him from the room while dictating his will to Montholon instead (`settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-death`) is therefore a real, felt demotion rather than a neutral administrative choice. Bertrand's side: loyalty this canon establishes as owing nothing to what Napoleon could still give him — by 1821 Napoleon had no throne, no patronage, and no future left to offer, and Bertrand had already paid a death sentence, under Bourbon law, for following him this far. He does not compete for favor the way Montholon does, not because he wants Napoleon's trust less, but because his sense of what he is owed was already settled before the exile began.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#bertrand` above and `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-household`, `#sthelena-lowe-relationship`, and `#sthelena-death`, each established by research.

## Relation: Napoleon and Montholon {#relation-napoleon-montholon}

**Standing: established by research, with the underlying paternity and poisoning questions each left open per `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#montholon`.** His side: real, growing reliance on Montholon specifically because Montholon made himself indispensable to the daily texture of the exile — company, flattery, and, this canon records without resolving, possibly his wife — in a way Bertrand's more detached, family-anchored loyalty did not supply. Montholon's side: a documented pattern, predating Saint Helena, of attaching himself to whatever institution or patron could still reward him, and Napoleon in decline was exactly such a patron for a man with a history of financial disgrace and no other prospects; the will he helped draft and was principally rewarded by is the clearest evidence this canon has that the relationship, on his side, never stopped being transactional even where it also became genuine personal intimacy. This canon does not resolve whether Montholon's devotion was sincere, calculated, or — as with much of this canon's cast — both at once; it records only that the material outcome favored him more than it favored anyone else in the household, and that the fact itself is what later gave the poisoning theory its specific name.

Sources: this synthesis rests on `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#montholon` above and `settings/020-saint-helena.md#sthelena-household` and `#sthelena-death`, each established by research.
