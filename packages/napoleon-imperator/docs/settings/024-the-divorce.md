# The Divorce

This file gives the divorce from Joséphine the granular, scene-level detail `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage` dates but does not carry, and does not repeat the relational detail already fixed at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`: the decision's build-up, the civil ceremony's actual words, the annulment's specific canonical grounds, and Joséphine's settlement and life after. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`, this canon builds the domestic and dynastic crisis of 1806-1811 to the same scene-level standard as the 1805 campaign and the collapse. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## The decision: build-up and consultation, 1806-1809 {#divorce-decision}

**Standing: established by research.** The idea of a divorce circulated for years before the December 1809 act, not merely from the acute 1809 succession crisis. As early as April 1804, per Comte Roederer's own memoirs, Joséphine told Joseph Bonaparte apropos the hereditary succession law that if Napoleon established heredity he would divorce her to have children, and those children would push Joseph out of the succession — evidence the anxiety long predates any documented approach to her directly. The birth of Charles Léon, Napoleon's son by Éléonore Denuelle de la Plaigne, on 13 December 1806, first proved he, not necessarily she, could father children (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`). By 1 September 1807 Joséphine was writing to her son Eugène that Murat was intriguing toward a divorce, and on 19 November 1807 Fouché's own police bulletin to Napoleon reported the dissolution of the marriage was being discussed openly at court, among the princes, in every circle — confirmation the question was court-wide well before any formal French initiative. On 30 November 1807, Fouché went further and approached Joséphine directly at Fontainebleau, telling her the public good required Napoleon have children and that she would best serve the state by demanding the sacrifice herself; she refused him flatly — "je regarde mon union avec l'Empereur comme écrite dans le livre du destin" (I regard my union with the Emperor as written in the book of fate) — and this episode is independently corroborated from the Austrian side: Metternich reported the same approach to Vienna the same day. Napoleon reprimanded Fouché in writing for acting without authorization, both directly and via Maret, though he did not disown the underlying idea. At Erfurt in October 1808, Napoleon told Talleyrand plainly: "Ma destinée l'exige et la tranquillité de la France me le demande. Je n'ai pas de successeur... c'est moi qui dois fonder une dynastie; je ne puis la fonder qu'en m'alliant à une princesse appartenant à l'une des grandes maisons régnantes de l'Europe. L'empereur Alexandre a des sœurs" (my destiny requires it, and the tranquility of France demands it of me; I have no successor; it is I who must found a dynasty; I can found it only by allying myself with a princess of one of Europe's great reigning houses; the Emperor Alexander has sisters) — floating the Russian option to Talleyrand first, before Metternich or Austria entered the picture (`settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md#marriage-negotiation`). Caulaincourt's own memoirs record a parallel conversation in which Napoleon frames the coming decision as a test of Alexander's friendship as much as a personal sacrifice: "Je l'aime, Joséphine. Je ne serai jamais plus heureux. Mais ceci révélera l'opinion des souverains sur un acte qui serait un sacrifice pour moi. Ma famille, Talleyrand, Fouché, tous les hommes d'État me le demandent au nom de la France" (I love her, Joséphine; I will never be happier; but this will reveal the sovereigns' opinion of an act that would be a sacrifice for me; my family, Talleyrand, Fouché, every statesman asks it of me in the name of France). Napoleon's own bluntest private formulation of the underlying logic — that he needed to "épouser un ventre" (marry a womb), not make a love match — is recorded by biographer Paul Ganière, though Fondation Napoléon director Thierry Lentz's own scholarship explicitly disputes this as the whole motive, noting Napoleon "n'épousa pas qu'un « ventre »." The precipitating final shock, per Ganière, was the 12 October 1809 assassination attempt against Napoleon at Schönbrunn by the Saxon student Friedrich Staps, which sharpened his sense of dynastic fragility in the absence of an heir.

## The civil dissolution, 15 December 1809 {#divorce-civil-dissolution}

**Standing: established by research.** The evening before, on 30 November 1809, after a tense private dinner at which Napoleon informed Joséphine, she suffered a nervous collapse and had to be carried to her apartments by Napoleon and the chamberlain M. de Bausset; the next day Napoleon told Hortense, "Mon parti est pris. Il est irrévocable. La France entière veut le divorce" (my decision is made; it is irrevocable; all of France wants the divorce). The Assemblée de famille convened at the Tuileries at 9:00 p.m. on 15 December 1809, attended by Napoleon, Joséphine, Louis and Hortense, Jérôme, Pauline, Caroline, Eugène, Murat, Julie Clary, Cambacérès, and Regnault Saint-Jean d'Angély — sources vary slightly on the exact attendee list, and this canon records the range rather than resolving it to one roster. At 9:15 p.m. the two announced the decision in the Salle du Trône to assembled dignitaries; Napoleon's declaration, as printed the following day in the Moniteur, closed with a promise: "Elle a été couronnée de ma main; je veux qu'elle conserve les rangs et le titre d'Impératrice couronnée" (she was crowned by my own hand; I wish her to keep the rank and title of crowned Empress). Joséphine's reply, same source: "Avec la permission de notre auguste et cher époux, je dois déclarer que ne conservant aucun espoir d'avoir des enfants... je me plais à lui donner la plus grande preuve d'attachement et de dévouement qui ait jamais été donnée sur la terre... Mais la dissolution de mon mariage ne changera rien aux sentiments de mon cœur: l'empereur aura toujours en moi sa meilleure amie" (with the permission of our august and dear husband, I must declare that, retaining no hope of having children, I take pleasure in giving him the greatest proof of attachment and devotion ever given on this earth; but the dissolution of my marriage will change nothing in the sentiments of my heart: the Emperor will always have in me his best friend). A privy council convened at 10:00 p.m. to draft the *sénatus-consulte*; the Senate adopted it the next morning, 16 December, by 76 votes to 7 with 4 abstentions. The decree's operative text — read directly rather than paraphrased — is deliberately careful with its verb: Article I states the marriage "est dissous," is dissolved, never "divorced," because the 1806 imperial household statutes (Article 7) had themselves forbidden divorce to members of the imperial family, and Civil Code Article 277 barred divorce by mutual consent where the wife was 45 or older (Joséphine was 46); "dissolution" avoided both legal bars while also avoiding any implication that a papal annulment was needed for what was, on this reading, purely a civil act. Article II preserved her title and rank as Impératrice-Reine couronnée; Article III fixed her domaine at an annual rent of 2,000,000 francs on the state treasury; Article IV bound Napoleon's successors to honor any further gifts made through the civil list.

## The ecclesiastical annulment, January 1810 {#divorce-annulment}

**Standing: established by research.** A civil dissolution did not by itself free Napoleon to remarry in the Church, so a separate canonical process followed. On 22 December 1809, Cambacérès convened the Officialité (ecclesiastical tribunal) of Paris to seek agreement for a religious annulment, informing the two officiaux and two promoteurs that Napoleon intended to remarry, and specifically to a Catholic — ruling out an Orthodox Russian bride from the outset of this internal consultation, even while the Russian option was still being pursued diplomatically (`settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md#marriage-negotiation`). The officiaux initially refused jurisdiction, on the traditional ground that only the Pope could dissolve a sovereign's marriage; an ecclesiastical commission convened at Cardinal Fesch's residence on 26 December ruled the Officialité competent after all. Two canonical grounds were argued, both resting on the private religious ceremony of 1 December 1804 — the night before the coronation, conducted by Fesch in the Tuileries's interior chapel: a defect of form (the absence of the parties' own parish priest and of the four legally required witnesses) and a defect of consent (that Napoleon had been pressured into the ceremony by Joséphine and had not freely consented). Napoleon's own account of that 1804 ceremony, recorded in Cambacérès's memoirs: "Le Pape m'ayant fait prévenir qu'il ne pourrait couronner l'Impératrice si je ne renouvelais mon mariage en présence d'un prêtre, je fis appeler le cardinal Fesch, qui nous donna la bénédiction nuptiale dans la chapelle intérieure... tout se passerait sans témoins, sans qu'il en fût retenu acte" (the Pope having warned me he could not crown the Empress unless I renewed my marriage in the presence of a priest, I sent for Cardinal Fesch, who gave us the nuptial blessing in the interior chapel; everything would take place without witnesses, with no record kept of it). Hearings opened 6 January 1810 before diocesan, metropolitan, and primatial (Lyon) jurisdiction in sequence; four witnesses were heard in a single afternoon session, 11:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. — Cardinal Fesch, Marshal Berthier, Grand Marshal Duroc, and Talleyrand. Fesch testified he had obtained a papal dispensation to bless the marriage without witnesses, but also that Napoleon had told him beforehand the ceremony was purely to placate Joséphine, was to be kept "as secret as confession," and that Napoleon wanted "point de témoins" (no witnesses) — Fesch had initially objected, "point de témoins, point de mariage" (no witnesses, no marriage), before relenting via the dispensation; the other three witnesses testified Fesch lacked proper canonical faculties and that the ceremony was purely circumstantial, never meant to bind the future. On 9 January the diocesan promoter, who privately judged the "lack of consent" argument weak against a man "so widely feared," nonetheless recommended annulment on the defect-of-form ground; the metropolitan promoter confirmed the ruling 11 January. The Moniteur announced the result 14 January: "[Le tribunal] a déclaré, par sentence du 9 du courant, la nullité, quant au lien spirituel, du mariage" (the tribunal declared, by sentence of the 9th of this month, the nullity, as to the spiritual bond, of the marriage), the metropolitan officialité confirming on the 12th — wording the diocesan promoter himself later complained blurred the distinction between the civil and spiritual bonds, a complaint that changed nothing. The tribunal's competence itself remained a live sensitivity beyond Paris: before conducting the proxy wedding in Vienna, the Archbishop of Vienna raised doubts about the validity of the annulment procedure and had not even been shown the Paris Officialité's paperwork, requiring Metternich and the French ambassador Otto to personally reassure him days before the ceremony (`settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md#marriage-proxy-wedding`). Separately, thirteen cardinals — including Ercole Consalvi — boycotted the religious wedding itself on 2 April 1810 in protest, an episode tied to that later date rather than to the January annulment and recorded at `settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md#church-excommunicate-empire`.

## Joséphine's settlement and position, 1810-1814 {#divorce-settlement}

**Standing: established by research.** Reconciling two figures sometimes quoted separately: the *sénatus-consulte* itself (`#divorce-civil-dissolution` above) fixed a state pension of exactly 2,000,000 francs a year; her biographical notice in the *Dictionnaire Napoléon* separately describes her effective total income, once combined with the revenue from the 750-hectare Malmaison domain (woods, meadows, farms, and cultivated land) and further civil-list gifts from Napoleon, as "un douaire de trois millions" — the commonly cited roughly-3-million-franc figure this canon's other files note without a precise state-decree source. Her household was reduced to thirty-six persons. She retained ownership of Malmaison, confirmed to her from 16 December 1809, the day after the family assembly, and the adjoining estate of Bois-Préau, which Napoleon had authorized her to purchase on 23 September 1809. She was also granted the Château de Navarre near Évreux and the Élysée Palace — but jealousy from the new Empress Marie-Louise, married 2 April 1810, forced an exchange of the Élysée for the more distant Château de Laeken near Brussels. Her social position after 1810 was a genuinely reduced one, not merely a formal demotion: she spent most of her time at her preferred Malmaison, made occasional forced visits to the "inconfortable et vilain" (uncomfortable and ugly, per her own biography) Château de Navarre specifically to placate Marie-Louise, traveled to the Alps and Switzerland (buying the Château de Prégny), and visited her son Eugène, viceroy of Italy, in Milan; Napoleon continued to write to her and to visit occasionally, and courtiers were encouraged not to abandon her. She learned of the birth of the King of Rome (20 March 1811, `settings/026-the-king-of-rome.md#kingofrome-birth`) while at Navarre, and — through the intervention of Mme de Montesquiou, the infant's governess — was allowed to see the child privately at Bagatelle, without Marie-Louise's knowledge. Her final months, the forced departure from Malmaison for Navarre on 29 March 1814 as Allied troops approached, her return per the *Journal des Débats* of 16 April, her reception of Tsar Alexander I at Malmaison as her self-declared protector, and her death 29 May 1814 after a walk with him at Saint-Leu are already fixed at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-1813-1814` and `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine` and are not repeated here.

Sources: napoleon.org, "BEAUHARNAIS, Joséphine de (1763-1814)," by Nicole and Gérard Hubert, in the *Dictionnaire Napoléon* (Fayard, 1999), read in full via the fetch helper — the sole source for the reconciled settlement figures (the 2-million-franc state pension against the roughly-3-million "douaire" once Malmaison's revenue and further gifts are included), the 36-person household, the Malmaison/Bois-Préau/Navarre/Élysée-for-Laeken property history, her post-1810 travel and social pattern, and the private Bagatelle meeting with her grandson arranged by Mme de Montesquiou.
