# Waterloo and Saint Helena

This file owns the terminal campaign and captivity boundary for the twenty-volume life arc.

**Status:** Source-supported political and logistical setting. Napoleon's return reopened an imperial state through a small landing, roads, defections, administration, and coalition response.

The direct Hundred Days chronology records work on the Inconstant beginning 17 February 1815, Napoleon's landing at Golfe-Juan on 1 March, the failed attempt to rally the 87th at Antibes, the route through Gap and Corps, and the 7 March Grenoble encounter. The Fondation route account describes a force of about 1,100 loyal soldiers and the 20 March entry into Paris. These dates and force descriptions do not prove that every locality welcomed him. A proclamation had to travel; officers had to choose; depots had to issue equipment; horses, guns, money, and civil clerks had to be found; Bourbon officials could flee, wait, or resist. The return is therefore a reconstruction of command under pressure, not an instant restoration of the 1812 army.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Waterloo is a field of mud, ridges, roads, corps arrival, orders, allied defense, Prussian movement, and contested clock meanings.

Wellington's report dated 19 June is a headquarters account written after the battle; the National Archives catalogue preserves that report and variants, while participant recollections and later maps describe other event boundaries. “The attack” may mean an order issued, a formation moving, first contact, a reinforcement, or a crisis recognized at headquarters. Rain and mud change artillery movement; a ridge hides a reserve; a courier crosses fire with uncertain receipt; a corps can arrive with information already obsolete. The setting therefore refuses one delayed-attack explanation and preserves source ownership for every time. Later scenarios can make a precise choice only when the selected source supports that event meaning.

**Status:** Work canon grounded in the campaign chronology. The final campaign is a race among armies whose roads, bridges, messengers, weather, and reports produce different information states.

French headquarters cannot see the whole coalition. A report may identify a column without proving its strength or destination; a road may be open to cavalry but blocked to artillery; a courier may arrive after the described situation has changed. Wellington's dispatches show the allied command communicating about the 15–18 June operation from its own position, while the French side has to infer Prussian movement through scouts, prisoners, and reports. The setting keeps French, Anglo-Allied, and Prussian knowledge separate. A commander chooses action under the information available then, not the map later assembled by historians. Roads, bridges, messengers, and weather are therefore causal constraints, not scenic background.

**Status:** Source-supported for abdication and surrender as distinct political acts; legal interpretation and private motive remain contested.

The chronology separates Waterloo on 18 June, abdication on 22 June, Rochefort on 3 July, and the later British transfer. Abdication changes the constitutional claim and provisional government; it does not itself place a person in allied custody. Surrender changes movement and bargaining; it does not erase family security, ministerial action, or the fate of soldiers. Each document has a date, audience, and coercive context. A proclamation may claim legality while ministers, troops, Bourbon officials, and allied governments act on different assumptions. The gap between text and enforcement is not a philosophical afterthought: it determines who controls passports, ports, guards, and the next government.

**Status:** Source-required domain. The interval between defeat and Saint Helena is a documented chain of custody, negotiations, ports, ships, guards, provisions, and competing legal claims.

The Hundred Days chronology records Rochefort on 3 July, Napoleon boarding the Bellerophon on 15 July, transfer to the Northumberland on 7 August, arrival at Saint Helena on 16 October, the Briars stay, and Longwood on 10 December. These are not interchangeable locations. At each stage someone receives the person, records terms, controls movement, carries papers, provides food and security, and answers to a government. A ship is a workplace and confinement system with weather, watch rotations, water, medical needs, and a chain of command. Conflicting accounts of surrender or transfer remain attributed to speaker, audience, and circumstance; the setting does not jump from political defeat to an island vista.

**Status:** Source-supported controlled environment. Longwood was an island prison, household, archive, and political theatre shaped by access, letters, medicine, weather, staff conflict, and British regulation.

Control was exercised through passes, visitors, mail, provisions, guards, doctors, servants, and the governor's administrative decisions rather than walls alone. The captive's dictated campaigns and memoir material are evidence of recollection and self-fashioning; British reports have their own security and administrative interests. Las Cases, Bertrand, Montholon, Gourgaud, physicians, guards, local workers, and residents did not occupy the same position or see the same day. A scene at Longwood must give labor and constraint to the person who carries food, controls a gate, copies a letter, or observes illness. Saint Helena is not a blank interval around a solitary mind; it is a controlled social and material environment that produces the surviving archive.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Illness, medical observation, death, burial, and later memory must be separated from retrospective myth.

The Fondation death dossier records Napoleon's death at Longwood at 5:49 p.m. on 5 May 1821, an autopsy on 6 May, preliminary mask impressions on 7 and 9 May, and burial on 9 May under British directions with French and English witnesses. Those are administrative and observed events; they do not make every symptom or disputed cause certain. Medical notes are observations through a body, household, and institutional relationship. The manuscript may render pain, weakness, treatment, and uncertainty, but must not invent a final quotation or settle a debated diagnosis. Death closes this biographical settings arc and hands reception, commemoration, and competing legends to the dedicated memory boundary.
