# The Imperial Court

This file records the imperial court as a working institution at its height, circa 1806-1811 — its etiquette and household offices, the residences it rotated through as a physical system, and Napoleon's own daily working regime inside it — that any scene of court life, access, or administration in this period must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`. It does not repeat Napoleon's personal habits and physical person, already fixed at `settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-habits`, or the Paris residences already fixed at `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-paris`; it extends both with the institutional and out-of-Paris material neither carries. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Etiquette, grand household offices, and access {#court-etiquette-access}

**Standing: established by research, with the rank-by-rank breakdown of some offices' successive holders incomplete in this canon's research.** The court was explicitly reconstructed on Ancien Régime and Bourbon precedent; per Jean Tulard's own "cour impériale" entry in the *Dictionnaire Napoléon*, it was, "à l'époque même de son apogée," even at the height of its own moment, "un objet de sarcasmes et de railleries" to contemporaries, yet substantially fulfilled its social and economic functions regardless. Two men successively held the office of Grand Chambellan: Talleyrand and, later, Pierre de Montesquiou-Fezensac — this canon has not located exact tenure dates for the succession and does not assert one. The formal precedence order is fixed by the Sunday chapel procession: pages and their governor; the day's aide and maître des cérémonies; equerries, prefects of the Palace, chamberlains; the governor of the Palace, the day's chamberlain and equerry, the aide-de-camp on duty; then, immediately preceding the Emperor, the Grand-Maître des cérémonies, Grand Veneur, Grand Écuyer, Grand Chambellan, and Grand Maréchal du Palais; then Napoleon himself, followed by the Guard's colonel-general on duty, the Grand Aumônier, and the princes. The Grand Aumônier's specific functions: bishop of the court wherever it travels, administering sacraments to the imperial family, baptizing and marrying them in Napoleon's presence, granting dispensations from abstinence, presenting Napoleon's prayer book at services, and — per this canon's specific source — able to "se trouver à son lever et à son coucher" (be present at his rising and retiring); the office also appointed army, navy, and Invalides chaplains and administered the fidelity oath to bishops. Grand Maréchal du Palais, the office running the household day to day, passed from Duroc (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#duroc`) — mortally wounded near Görlitz, Silesia, 22 May 1813 — to Bertrand (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#bertrand`), appointed 19 November 1813; Caulaincourt held Grand Écuyer from June 1804 (`settings/023-the-imperial-nobility.md#nobility-recipients`). The Empress's household mirrored the Emperor's: her dame d'honneur functioned as the female analogue to the Grand Chambellan, and her dame d'atours ran her wardrobe — Mme de La Rochefoucauld was Joséphine's first dame d'honneur and Émilie de Lavalette (née Beauharnais) her dame d'atours, and Général de Nansouty, Joséphine's Premier chambellan, became Premier écuyer of the Emperor himself in 1808. The routine mechanism of access was the daily levée at nine in the morning, per Méneval's own account: officers attended to receive their orders, and those in immediate contact with Napoleon by rank or employment used the levée's hour to pay their respects or obtain a few words with him — the ordinary, rank-gated channel for informal audience, distinct from set-piece diplomatic receptions, which are separately attested (a formal military review followed immediately by "une audience assez longue" for the diplomatic corps on at least one documented occasion).

## The residences as a working system {#court-residences}

**Standing: established by research.** Beyond the Paris seats already fixed — Tuileries as principal seat of government, Saint-Cloud as preferred suburban residence, Malmaison as Joséphine's own retained property (`settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-paris`) — the court operated a wider seasonal rotation, per Méneval's own account: "Il chassait, selon Méneval, presque toutes les semaines, moins par goût que pour faire de l'exercice" (he hunted, according to Méneval, almost every week, less from taste than to get exercise). When in Paris, hunts took place at Saint-Cloud or Trianon, in the Bois de Boulogne, the forest of Saint-Germain, or the woods of Versailles. Larger hunting sojourns took the court to Rambouillet, where Napoleon resided a fortnight to three weeks at a stretch, and to Compiègne for a comparable span; the longest court season by far was at Fontainebleau, where the court stayed "pendant six semaines ou deux mois, en septembre ou en octobre" (for six weeks or two months, in September or October), and Napoleon "tenait une cour nombreuse et brillante," held a numerous and brilliant court. Baron de Bausset, also quoted in this canon's source, describes the field logistics of a hunting day: a tent pitched in the forest for luncheon to which the whole traveling party was invited, ladies following by calèche, roughly eight to ten guests seated to dinner. Mme de Rémusat, quoted in the same account, describes Napoleon commissioning matching hunting habits for the court ladies from the couturier Leroy, each princess assigned a distinct color — the Empress in amaranth velvet embroidered in gold, the Queen of Holland in blue and silver, Madame Murat in pink and silver, Princess Borghèse in lilac and silver — a specific, dated illustration (the anecdote's context is a March 1810 Compiègne outfitting) of the display machinery the court maintained even in its recreational register. This canon's research did not establish Malmaison's precise role, if any, in this later working rotation — it recedes after the 1809 divorce into Joséphine's private property rather than a seat the reconstituted court itself used — nor a systematic account of how ministries, archives, and couriers physically moved with the court between these residences; both are flagged as gaps rather than invented.

Sources: "La Cour Impériale (deuxième partie): Le Grand Veneur," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, quoting Méneval on the hunting rotation and Fontainebleau's season length, and quoting Bausset and Mme de Rémusat for the field logistics and the matched hunting-habit anecdote; `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-paris`, established by research, for the Paris-proper residences this file extends rather than repeats.

## Napoleon's daily working regime {#court-daily-regime}

**Standing: established by research.** This entry records the institutional and working-method dimension of Napoleon's day that `settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-habits` does not carry, drawing directly on Baron Méneval's own memoirs, written by Napoleon's private secretary from 1802 to 1813. Méneval's own framing states the pattern continued essentially unchanged from the Consulate into the Empire despite the vastly greater scale of business: "Although the interior and exterior affairs of the Empire had taken a great development since the rupture of the peace of Amiens, the Emperor's usual way of living had changed very little." Napoleon kept no fixed hours "neither for his work, nor his meals, nor for sleep." He typically came to his work-room at seven in the morning, already dressed for the entire day. The levée followed at nine (`#court-etiquette-access` above). Lunch, taken alone in a small drawing-room adjoining the work-room, "rarely lasted more than ten minutes, but... he prolonged when he had leisure," sometimes using it to receive scientists, writers, and artists in conversation. The Council of Ministers met every Wednesday at noon, running until dinner, with the Arch-Chancellor Cambacérès and the Chief Treasurer present. Dinner was nominally served at six, "but when the Emperor was busy he let the hour go by"; he dined alone with the Empress except on Wednesdays, when ministers were invited, and Sundays, a family meal, and by Méneval's account "preferred the simplest dishes," drank only Chambertin mixed with water, never touched liqueurs, and took one cup of coffee after dinner and one at lunch, never more. His sleep was broken rather than continuous, especially on campaign, and he could return from an hour's sleep "as wide awake and as clear in his head as if he had slept quietly the whole night"; Méneval records being forbidden to be woken before seven even on nights Napoleon himself rose to work, so that on such mornings Méneval would find his own writing-table already covered with annotated reports and drafted replies by the time Napoleon returned from the nine o'clock levée. He bathed daily at irregular hours, a habit he "considerably modified" once his physician Corvisart warned that frequent, prolonged hot baths were weakening and predisposed to obesity. His characteristic working method was rapid task-switching across unrelated business rather than literal simultaneity: "Napoleon would deal with in turn, at one sitting, matters relating to war, to diplomacy, to finance, to commerce, to public works, and so on; and rested from one kind of work by engaging in another," with, in Méneval's telling, "no confusion of ideas, no fatigue" — Napoleon's own explanation for the method: "When I want to interrupt one piece of work, I close the drawer in which it is, and I open another. The two pieces of business never get mixed up together, and never trouble or tire me. When I want to go to sleep, I close up all the drawers, and then I am ready to go off to sleep." He dictated rather than wrote, since "his hand could not follow the rapidity of his conceptions": when an idea matured he would rise and pace the room's full length in a slow, unbroken, measured tone for the whole span of the dictation, sometimes betraying inspiration only by nervously twisting his right sleeve, addressing whichever secretary answered first — "his secretary... or, in his absence, either the second secretary or the Secretary of State or General Duroc, or sometimes the aide-de-camp on duty, according to the kind of work in which he was engaged" (`settings/008-grande-armee.md#command-staff`). He kept red-morocco-bound monthly land- and sea-force strength reports on his writing table, updated on the first of each month, and "read them through with delight," retaining exact unit compositions from memory though frequently misremembering proper names and — sometimes deliberately — miscalculating troop totals upward. Méneval also records Napoleon's own recollection that he had "constantly worked sixteen hours a day" since his school years, a self-report rather than a specific measurement of this 1806-1811 window. His personal maxim on the public-versus-private register of the court's own display, which this canon treats as governing how the household as a whole was run: "Be economical and even parsimonious at home; be magnificent in public" — reflected concretely in his own clothing allowance, which he voluntarily cut from 60,000 to 20,000 francs.
