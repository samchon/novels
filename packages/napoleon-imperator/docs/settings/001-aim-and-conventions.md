# Imperator: Aim, Scale, and Conventions

This file states what the work is for and how it is written before any world fact is recorded. It binds every later file: an aim the work does not serve, a scale it does not reach, or a convention it does not keep is a defect in that later file, not a freedom in it.

## Governing aim {#governing-aim}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** *Imperator* means to earn the reader's understanding of how total, deliberate, personally exercised power is actually run day to day by one man who is very good at it, at the cost paid by the people who run it with him and the people it is run upon — and to make Napoleon Bonaparte legible neither as monster nor as genius-alibi but as a specific temperament (appetite for total information and total control, contempt for uncertainty he has not personally inspected, genuine charisma, genuine cruelty of neglect rather than sadism) meeting a specific historical opportunity. The work leads with this understanding over spectacle, over romance, and over moral verdict; battle, court, and family scenes are all instruments of it, not attractions in their own right. Other goods — the pleasure of a well-staged battle, the texture of Napoleonic material culture, the tragedy of individual marshals and monarchs — are served throughout, but none of them is allowed to become the thing the work spends its space on at the aim's expense. A scene earns its place by showing power being exercised, resisted, misjudged, or paid for; a scene that only depicts uniforms, geography, or famous events without that pressure does not belong regardless of its historical color.

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Intended scale {#intended-scale}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** *Imperator* is a long-form biographical novel covering Napoleon Bonaparte's adult life from his rise to command (Toulon, 1793) to his death at Longwood (1821), with his Corsican origin and family formation as necessary background rather than a fully dramatized childhood arc. It is the deliberate outlier of this benchmark for scale and research burden: canon must support a multi-volume-equivalent work, not a single campaign novella. The work commits to three periods carrying staged, scene-level detail — the Third Coalition and Austerlitz of 1805, the imperial peak and its domestic and dynastic crisis of 1806–1811, and the collapse running 1812 Russia through Waterloo and Saint Helena, 1812–1821 — and treats the intervening campaigns (Jena–Tilsit, the Peninsular War, Wagram) as fully canonical but summarized rather than scene-by-scene, exactly as a biography compresses well-documented but non-pivotal years. Which of these periods the finished work opens on, and in what order the reader meets them, is a storyline decision this file does not make, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`; this canon's task is only to ensure that whichever period is dramatized first has no fact, date, rank, distance, or relationship it must invent, and `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md` are accordingly built to that full standard regardless of where the storyline eventually begins. This settings corpus is written to the fuller standard throughout: every period the work might eventually dramatize is given canon sufficient for scene-level use, even where the current storyline commitment does not plan to dramatize it, because later work may reveal a stronger reason to expand scope, and settings must not force an invention at that point.

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Focal figures {#focal-figures}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** The work's sustained point-of-view figure is Napoleon himself, tracked closely enough that the reader inhabits his calculation and his blindness alike. Four figures carry sustained secondary viewpoint access across the work — meaning the narration may enter their perception and interiority in scenes where Napoleon is absent or where their reading of him differs from his own self-account: Marshal Louis-Alexandre Berthier (the machinery of command, proximity without intimacy), Joséphine de Beauharnais through 1809 and Marie-Louise of Austria from 1810 (the throne's private and dynastic cost), and Armand de Caulaincourt from 1812 onward (the clearest-eyed witness available in the primary record, per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-caulaincourt`). A wider ensemble — the marshals of `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md`, the ministers of `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md`, and the foreign crowns of `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md` — is drawn in full enough depth to bear scene-level weight and occasional viewpoint, but the work does not commit in advance to giving any of them sustained viewpoint status; that remains open to storyline decision.

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Viewpoint and narrative conventions {#viewpoint-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** The work is written in close third person, past tense, moving among the focal figures named above at chapter or sequence boundaries rather than within a scene. No figure narrates in first person and no figure is granted knowledge they could not plausibly have at that point in the timeline — a viewpoint figure does not anticipate a defeat, a betrayal, or a death before its documented or dramatized moment, even where the reader (via `settings/002-chronology.md`) already knows it is coming; dramatic irony is built from that gap, not erased by giving characters foreknowledge. Historical hindsight, retrospective judgment, and modern moral vocabulary are not voiced by the narration; when the work needs to render a fact modern readers will find morally stark — the reinstatement of slavery (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#slavery-reinstatement`), the legal subordination of wives under the Code (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`), the scale of the campaign dead — it renders it through the concrete texture of what a character does, orders, or witnesses, not through authorial commentary layered on top.

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Naming and honorific conventions {#naming-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** Napoleon is called "Bonaparte" in narration and by other characters through the coup of 18 Brumaire (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-brumaire`) and "Napoleon" thereafter, matching the historical shift in how contemporaries addressed him after he became head of state; after the imperial proclamation of May 1804 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-empire-proclaimed`) formal address by subordinates is "Sire" or "Your Majesty," and intimate address by the handful of figures entitled to it (see `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-lannes`) is rendered as the informal register their historical relationship supports, never invented for a figure the record does not attest it for. Titles are given as they stood at the story's calendar moment, not retrospectively: a figure who becomes King of Naples in 1808 is not called that in a 1805 scene. French, German, Austrian, and Russian names are given in the francophone/anglophone spelling this canon fixes (`settings/002-chronology.md` and the figure files use "Joséphine," "Kutuzov," "Bagration," "Weyrother," "Francis"); place names use the period's working name with the modern equivalent noted once at first use in each file where it matters militarily (e.g., Austerlitz / Slavkov u Brna, per `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-terrain`).

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Calendar and dating conventions {#calendar-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, resting on an established fact.** The French Republican Calendar was in official use from late September 1792 until Napoleon abolished it effective 1 January 1806, per `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-brumaire` and `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-empire-proclaimed`. This work dates every scene and every settings fact in the Gregorian calendar throughout, including the period 1793-1805 when Republican dating was in force, because a biography spanning 1793-1821 that switched dating systems mid-work would burden every downstream layer with silent conversion. Where a Republican date is historically load-bearing — 13 Vendémiaire Year IV (5 October 1795), 18-19 Brumaire Year VIII (9-10 November 1799), 12 Frimaire Year XIV (3 December 1805, the date of the 30th Bulletin) — this canon gives both forms at first use, and a character speaking in period may use the Republican form as period color, but no settings fact is dated in Republican terms alone.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "The Republican calendar," https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/the-republican-calendar/ (abolition decreed 9 September 1805, effective 1 January 1806); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "13 Vendémiaire Year IV," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/counter-revolution/13_vendemiaire.php; for the 12 Frimaire Year XIV equivalence, Fondation Napoléon's own edition of the document, "30e Bulletin de la Grande Armée (12 frimaire an XIV — 3 décembre 1805)," https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/30e-bulletin-de-la-grande-armee-12-frimaire-an-xiv-3-decembre-1805/, which carries both datings in its title; dates cross-verified against `settings/002-chronology.md`.

## Language, dialogue, and translation conventions {#language-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** All narration and all dialogue are rendered in English regardless of the language a scene's participants would historically have spoken (French among the French court and army; German or French at the Austrian court; Russian, French, or German among the Russians, per `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#alexander-i`). Where a specific document or reported utterance survives in a specific wording — Napoleon's proclamation before Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`), his rebuke to Murat after Hollabrunn (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-murat`) — the work translates that documented wording rather than inventing new dialogue to carry the same beat, and flags in the citing settings entry when a widely repeated line is disputed or unverifiable so no downstream layer treats legend as document. Untranslated period vocabulary (ranks, corps names, honorifics such as "Sire," institutional names such as "Grande Armée," "Conseil d'État") is kept in French or the relevant language where an English equivalent would flatten a distinction the work needs (e.g., keeping "maréchal d'Empire" distinct from a merely translated "marshal" where the imperial dignity itself is the point).

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Standing and sourcing conventions {#standing-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction.** Every H2 in every settings file opens with one of exactly four standing markers, each stated in the identical bolded form used in this file: "established by research," "disputed between sources" (naming the disputants and the range), "decided for this fiction" (an authorial choice among genuinely open historical possibilities, or a fact belonging wholly to invented texture), or "not yet decided" (a clause stating specifically why the record does not settle the matter). A marker is never dissolved into prose elsewhere in the entry, and a fact's marker does not drift between files: where `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md` labels a source's reliability, every other file's use of that source carries the same standing. Every H2 whose claims are externally checkable closes with a Sources line; H2s that are purely decided-for-fiction texture close with a line stating that explicitly rather than a fabricated citation.

Sources: this is an authorial decision, not an externally checkable claim.

## Units, currency, and measurement conventions {#measurement-conventions}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, resting on established facts.** Distances are given in kilometers with period miles noted at first use where a source cites miles (per `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md`); the work does not convert period sources' round or approximate figures into false precision. Currency is given in French francs of the franc germinal standard established 28 March 1803 (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#franc-germinal`) for all Napoleonic-era financial facts from 1803 onward; pre-1803 sums (Toulon-era pay, the Bonaparte family's Corsican finances) are noted as pre-reform livres where the source specifies. Troop strengths, casualty figures, and other externally disputed quantities are given as ranges with named sources on each side per `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship`, never silently rounded to a single false-precise number; where this canon must commit to one figure for downstream arithmetic to work (a march distance, a corps strength used to plan a scene), the committed figure is marked decided-for-fiction within the established range and the reason for the choice is stated.

Sources: this is an authorial decision resting on `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#franc-germinal` and `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship`, both established by research.
