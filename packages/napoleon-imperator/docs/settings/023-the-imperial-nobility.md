# The Imperial Nobility of 1808

This file records the titled nobility Napoleon created to sit beside — and, in real tension with — the Legion of Honor's merit principle and the Revolution's 1789 abolition of hereditary privilege, that any scene touching rank, precedence, land, or the court's social settlement from 1808 onward must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Legal genesis: from the 1806 *sénatus-consulte* to the decree of 1 March 1808 {#nobility-genesis}

**Standing: established by research.** The nobility was built in stages, not created whole in 1808. The Constitution of Year XII (1804) had already created "princes français" around Napoleon; decrees of 30-31 March 1806 granted territorial titles to Pauline Bonaparte (Guastalla), Talleyrand (Bénévent), Murat (Clèves-et-Berg), Berthier (Neufchâtel), and Bernadotte (Ponte-Corvo), while reserving a stock of further "grands fiefs" for future use. The *sénatus-consulte* of 14 August 1806 made this power constitutional, explicitly reserving to the Emperor the right to create "les autres titres que Sa Majesté pourrait ériger à l'avenir" (whatever other titles His Majesty might in future establish). On 28 May 1807, four days after the capitulation of Danzig, Napoleon made Marshal Lefebvre hereditary Duke of Danzig — no sovereignty, no territorial revenue attached to the city itself, purely a merit title, and this canon treats it as a deliberate Rubicon: Napoleon chose a marshal of humble, non-noble origin specifically to demonstrate that the new dignity rested on merit rather than birth. The decree of 1 March 1808 did not itself invent the system; per Thierry Lentz, it "ne fit dès lors que préciser les modalités d'application" of the 1806 *sénatus-consulte* — it specified mechanics, citing the 1806 act in its own preamble, rather than establishing the principle anew.

Sources: Thierry Lentz, "28 mai 1807: Le maréchal Lefebvre devient duc de Dantzig," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, for the 1804-1808 staged legal genesis, the March 1806 territorial-title decrees, the 14 August 1806 *sénatus-consulte*'s text, and the Lefebvre creation as the deliberate "Rubicon" moment.

## Titles and hierarchy {#nobility-hierarchy}

**Standing: established by research.** The March 1808 decree "fit revivre les titres de prince et d'altesse sérénissime" (revived the titles of prince and serene highness) for the grands dignitaires of the Empire, and gave the eldest son of such a dignitary the right to bear the title duc de l'empire once his father had endowed a majorat producing 200,000 francs a year. Grands dignitaires could similarly endow younger sons with majorats carrying the rank of comte or baron. Income thresholds, each requiring a fixed fraction permanently committed to the title's endowment: comte required 30,000 francs annual income (one-third to endowment); baron required 15,000 francs (also one-third to endowment) and was available to office-holders such as presidents of electoral colleges after three sessions in post; chevalier, open to members of the Legion of Honor, required 3,000 francs annual income and was transmissible by primogeniture. Titles descended male-line by primogeniture, legitimate, natural, or adoptive descent all accepted; a celibate archbishop designated a nephew as heir. Napoleon deliberately excluded the rank of marquis, believing — per one specialist source — it "avait été ridiculisé dans les comédies de Molière," ridiculed beyond recovery by Molière's comedies. The oath every title-holder swore (Article 37): "Je jure d'être fidèle à l'empereur et à sa dynastie, d'obéir aux constitutions, lois et règlemens de l'empire, de servir Sa Majesté en bon, loyal et fidèle sujet et d'élever mes enfans dans les mêmes sentimens de fidélité et d'obéissance et de marcher à la défense de la patrie toutes les fois que le territoire sera menacé ou que Sa Majesté irait à l'armée" (I swear to be faithful to the emperor and his dynasty, to obey the constitutions, laws, and regulations of the empire, to serve His Majesty as a good, loyal, and faithful subject, to raise my children in the same sentiments of fidelity and obedience, and to march to the defense of the fatherland whenever the territory is threatened or His Majesty takes the field). This canon states explicitly, per Alfred Maury's contemporary scholarly analysis, what the decree deliberately did not do: it was not a restoration of feudal nobility, carried no seigneurial jurisdiction over any locality, and a separate law of 20 July 1808 forbade a title-holder from even taking the name of a commune as a family surname; per Annette Surrault's archivally-grounded article on General Bertrand, a majorat-holder "restait soumis aux lois civiles et pénales comme tout citoyen ordinaire" (remained subject to civil and penal law like any ordinary citizen), and a majorat funded from imperial dotations reverted to the Crown if the male line died out — as happened to Bertrand's own majorat in 1882, when his son died without heirs.

Sources: Alfred Maury, "Les Titres nobiliaires en France avant et depuis la révolution," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, 3e période, tome 54 (1882), pp. 781-823, full text read at fr.wikisource.org — the primary locator for the title hierarchy, the income thresholds, the primogeniture and adoption rules, the exclusion of "marquis," the 20 July 1808 surname law, and the Article 37 oath text, quoted directly; Annette Surrault, "De la Pologne au Berry: le général Bertrand, grand propriétaire foncier," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, for the majorat-holder's ordinary civil-law status and the 1882 reversion of Bertrand's own majorat. This canon has not located a standalone transcription of the 1 March 1808 decree itself on Wikisource despite searching, and both sources above are read as specialist secondary treatments rather than the primary decree text.

## Majorats: the endowment system {#nobility-majorats}

**Standing: established by research.** A majorat was not an honorific gesture; Maury frames it as functionally parallel to the Legion of Honor, "une récompense de la même nature... destinée à unir plus étroitement au trône impérial ceux auxquels elle était conférée" (a reward of the same nature, meant to bind more closely to the imperial throne those on whom it was conferred), needed so a titled family could hold its rank without falling into the financial decline that would undercut the prestige Napoleon wanted his new elite to project both domestically and to foreign courts. The land funding these endowments came overwhelmingly from conquered or annexed territory. Surrault's archivally-grounded case study of General Bertrand gives concrete figures: Bertrand's first dotation (30 June 1807, at the "camp impérial de Tilsit") was the domaine de Goszczyn in the Department of Warsaw, confiscated from Prussia — one of twenty-seven landed properties Napoleon distributed to marshals and generals in that single batch, Bertrand's valued at 1,096,976 francs (producing 30,000 francs a year, the fourth most valuable of the batch), against Marshal Davout's, the most valuable at 4,831,238 francs. Bertrand's second dotation (March 1808) was the domaine de Schermeke near Magdeburg in the new Kingdom of Westphalia, drawn on lands confiscated from the King of Prussia and producing 25,000 francs a year; a third (19 November 1813) came from confiscated ecclesiastical property in the newly created Department of Taro in Italy. Installation on these estates was not always smooth: Polish tenants resisted French appropriation — for Marshal Lannes's Polish property near Kalisz "il fallut menacer d'utiliser la force militaire" (it was necessary to threaten the use of military force) — and Bertrand's own domain suffered an arson that killed twenty-four cows and a calf.

Sources: Annette Surrault, "De la Pologne au Berry: le général Bertrand, grand propriétaire foncier," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper — the sole source for the Bertrand and Davout dotation figures, the Tilsit and Westphalian and Taro grants, the Polish tenant resistance on Lannes's property, and the arson on Bertrand's domain; Maury, "Les Titres nobiliaires en France," Wikisource, for the majorat-as-Legion-of-Honor-parallel framing.

## Who received titles, and the scale of the creation {#nobility-recipients}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact rank-by-rank breakdown of the roughly 3,000 titles not located in this canon's research and treated as a gap rather than invented.** Dated examples: Talleyrand, prince de Bénévent, 5 June 1806; Lefebvre, duc de Dantzig, hereditary, 28 May 1807; Davout, duc d'Auerstaedt, 28 March 1808, then prince d'Eckmühl, 15 August 1809, conferred at Schönbrunn after the battle (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-davout`); Caulaincourt, Grand Écuyer from June 1804, duc de Vicence, 7 June 1808 (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#caulaincourt`); Berthier, prince de Neufchâtel per the March 1806 decrees, and — per a tertiary source this canon has not upgraded — prince de Wagram in 1809 as a victory title after the Fifth Coalition campaign; Bertrand, comte de l'Empire, 19 March 1808, with letters patent dated 21 September 1808 at Saint-Cloud. Overall scale, per historian Natalie Petiteau's own doctoral research: "L'anoblissement par l'Empire ne touche cependant qu'un groupe restreint de quelque 3,000 individus" (ennoblement under the Empire touched only a restricted group of some 3,000 individuals) — of whom 67.9% were military men, including previously-noble officers reconfirmed in rank, and 60% of the strictly new creations were of bourgeois origin. This canon found no documented instance of a refusal of an imperial title in its research; the nearest adjacent case, Caulaincourt's public attack over his role in the 1804 Enghien affair, is reputational controversy over his conduct, not a refusal of the ducal honor itself, and this canon does not stretch it into a refusal anecdote — a downstream unit should treat a title-refusal scene as invented rather than documented unless a better source is found.

## Tension with the Legion of Honor and the Revolution's settlement {#nobility-tension}

**Standing: established by research.** The Legion of Honor (`settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#legion-of-honor`), founded 19 May 1802, had been explicitly framed as merit "without distinction of birth," a single insignia — per its own promotional rhetoric — honoring "tous et chacun, sans distinction de classe, d'origine, de grade ou de religion," everyone alike regardless of class, origin, rank, or religion; the 1808 nobility sits in real, acknowledged tension with that founding claim, and with the Revolution's own August 1789 abolition of hereditary privilege. Napoleon's original intent for the new nobility was that it remain a creature of merit and service alone, open in principle to any Legion of Honor recipient — but by 1810 he reversed course under pressure chiefly from Cambacérès, moving to co-opt the surviving old nobility as well. Chancellor Pasquier's own memoirs record the boldness of the original exclusionary plan: "c'était une conception bien hardie que celle de donner à la France une nouvelle noblesse en présence de celle qui, quoique abrogée par la loi, était encore vivante dans tous les souvenirs" (it was a bold conception indeed to give France a new nobility in the presence of the old one which, though abolished by law, was still alive in every memory). Napoleon's own instruction to Cambacérès, dictated 14 June 1810: "Il convient, par des mesures sages et indirectes, de faire participer toute la France aux avantages de l'institution des titres héréditaires. S'il n'en est pas ainsi, cette institution ne sera jamais nationale" (it is fitting, through wise and indirect measures, to let all of France share in the advantages of the hereditary-title institution; otherwise the institution will never be national) — with the explicit qualifying instruction, on which old nobles to admit, "il faut avant tout qu'ils aient conservé de la fortune" (above all, they must have retained their fortune). Cambacérès's report of 30 June 1810 argued the diplomatic value of the fusion directly: "on n'oserait plus sourire d'une cour où des Montmorency, des La Rochefoucauld et des Mortemart accepteraient les 'fonctions de service' à côté des nouveaux nobles" (no one would dare smile any longer at a court where the Montmorencys, the La Rochefoucaulds, and the Mortemarts accepted service functions alongside the new nobles). Napoleon nonetheless reserved the top of the pyramid for his own creations: "Je ne veux d'autres ducs que ceux que j'ai créés ou que je pourrai créer encore. Si je fais quelques exceptions à l'égard de l'ancienne noblesse, ces exceptions seront très restreintes et ne s'appliqueront qu'à des noms historiques" (I want no other dukes than those I have created or may yet create; if I make some exceptions for the old nobility, they will be very restricted and will apply only to historic names). Reaction split predictably: old nobles who had "proved their rallying" through army, administrative, court, or electoral service sought and obtained imperial titles, while impoverished but well-named old families were propped up with dotations rather than titles — Napoleon reportedly explaining, "Ces noms appartiennent à la France, à l'histoire. Je suis le tuteur de leur gloire, je ne les laisserai pas périr" (these names belong to France, to history; I am the guardian of their glory, I will not let them perish), a remark this canon has traced only through Petiteau's own footnoted citation of a 1980 specialist-journal article, not opened directly. Baron Ernouf's contemporary biography of Maret records the visible social awkwardness some new title-holders themselves felt: on Fouché and Merlin specifically, "il est vrai que les titres nobiliaires et les habits brodés de certains fonctionnaires ne cadraient guère avec leurs antécédents... [ils] devaient se paraître suspects à eux-mêmes" (it is true that the noble titles and embroidered coats of certain officials sat oddly with their antecedents; they must have appeared suspect even to themselves).
