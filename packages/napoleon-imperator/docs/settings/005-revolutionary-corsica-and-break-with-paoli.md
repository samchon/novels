**Status:** Source-supported for the Revolution's effect on Napoleon's generation and for the documented correspondence beginning in 1784; contested in retrospective claims that the young officer possessed one settled ideology from the first news of Paris.

The Revolution changes the value of every earlier resource. The chronology places Napoleon's third Corsican stay between September 1789 and February 1791, Paoli's return on 17 July 1790, and the fourth stay between September 1791 and May 1792; those dates make political reception a sequence, not a single announcement. Noble recognition can become liability; French military service can signify opportunity, occupation, or republican service; Paoli's memory can become a living political allegiance after his return; and the family's property and offices can be judged by competing authorities. Napoleon is not an observer outside these changes. He is a young officer with a Corsican family, a French commission, and a need to interpret events before he knows their outcome.

The settings canon separates Parisian chronology from Corsican reception. A law, decree, or speech reaches Ajaccio through letters, newspapers, ships, officials, and rumor. A family member may know that an institution has fallen without knowing what the local consequences will be. Scenarios must preserve delay and translation; no Corsican household receives a national event as an instant, complete broadcast.

The novel does not use “republican” as a synonym for virtuous or “royalist” as a synonym for reactionary. Each label carries a faction, a legal claim, and a risk. Napoleon's later choices must arise from concrete allies, commands, family safety, and political judgment rather than from a textbook conversion scene.

**Status:** Source-supported for Paoli's return as a decisive fact in Corsican politics and for Napoleon's attempt to engage with him; contested in the emotional sincerity and strategic calculation assigned to every letter.

Paoli's return converts a remembered hero into a living political authority. The itinerary records his return in triumph in 1790, while the chronology dates Napoleon's welcome to 17 July. Napoleon's childhood image of Paoli now meets a man with followers, opponents, and a program that may not align with French republican power or the Bonaparte family's interests. The political test is therefore personal and institutional at once.

Napoleon's approach to Paoli must be staged through letters, intermediaries, public declarations, and factional observation. The chronology dates the Letter to Buttafuoco to 23 January 1791 and its copies being sent to Corsica, including Paoli, on 14 March; the correspondence study interprets those texts and their context rather than giving direct access to every motive. A letter can flatter, ask, conceal, or test; its rhetoric does not reveal the whole sender's mind. The setting accepts the study's double-movement interpretation—seeking Paoli's favor while maintaining relations with French power—as an interpretation, not a simple confession of hypocrisy.

The family becomes a political unit. Joseph, Lucien, Letizia, and other relatives may differ in courage, language, and risk tolerance. A public action in Ajaccio can expose a household far beyond the person who took it. Scenes must show who knows a plan, who is absent, and what a faction can infer from a uniform, portrait, or letter.

**Status:** Source-supported for the eventual break between Napoleon and Paoli and for the Bonaparte family's flight from Corsica in 1793; bounded in exact dates of each private disagreement and in the motives reconstructed from later memoirs.

The break is a process of incompatible authority. Paoli's legitimacy is rooted in Corsican political memory and personal following; Napoleon's advancing route lies through the French Republic and its army. The sources do not give one uncontested “break” minute: the critical itinerary describes the split through Paoli's removal after accusations connected with Lucien, while the correspondence chronology records the Convention's 1793 decree against Paoli and the resistance that followed. A family trying to remain safe between them can become suspect to both. The storyline must identify the moment a letter, vote, command, or public accusation changes the available choices; settings does not pick a dramatic argument that the record does not preserve.

The Bonaparte family's flight from Ajaccio in 1793 is a material consequence. The chronology dates the flight to 11 June and arrival at Toulon to 13 June; the itinerary's Calvi section says the family boarded ships there on 11 June and records a local welcome, while its Casa Bonaparte section describes sacking and partial burning after the family left. These are complementary route/memory records, not a complete manifest. The age, route, vessel, possessions, and shelter of each family member require their own sourced owners. The setting does establish displacement, loss of local security, and the transformation of Corsica from home into a political danger.

The flight must not be narrated as a clean national conversion. Exile can produce grief, anger, pragmatism, and a new dependence on French institutions simultaneously. Napoleon's military service becomes a way to recover influence and support relatives, but the exact balance between family rescue and personal ambition remains a storyline question supported by dated actions.

The French army offers a young Corsican officer a career that local faction cannot fully control. Rank, pay, mobility, and access to generals can protect a family that has lost its position on the island. The same service also makes Napoleon an agent of a state that many Corsicans experience as coercive. The novel must hold both truths in the same scene when the viewpoint can perceive them.

Military service converts personal skill into institutional power through orders, reports, supplies, and legal authority.
