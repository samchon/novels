**Status:** Source-supported for the Revolution's effect on Napoleon's generation and for the documented correspondence beginning in 1784; contested in retrospective claims that the young officer possessed one settled ideology from the first news of Paris.

The Revolution changes the value of every earlier resource. Noble recognition can become liability; French military service can signify opportunity, occupation, or republican service; Paoli's memory can become a living political allegiance after his return; and the family's property and offices can be judged by competing authorities. Napoleon is not an observer outside these changes. He is a young officer with a Corsican family, a French commission, and a need to interpret events before he knows their outcome.

The settings canon separates Parisian chronology from Corsican reception. A law, decree, or speech reaches Ajaccio through letters, newspapers, ships, officials, and rumor. A family member may know that an institution has fallen without knowing what the local consequences will be. Scenarios must preserve delay and translation; no Corsican household receives a national event as an instant, complete broadcast.

The novel does not use “republican” as a synonym for virtuous or “royalist” as a synonym for reactionary. Each label carries a faction, a legal claim, and a risk. Napoleon's later choices must arise from concrete allies, commands, family safety, and political judgment rather than from a textbook conversion scene.

**Status:** Source-supported for Napoleon's recurring movements between mainland service and Corsica in the late 1780s and early 1790s; bounded in the exact household scenes and in the private balance between French and Corsican identity.

Return is not restoration. The young officer goes back to an island changed by his absence, while he himself carries school credentials, a commission, debts, books, and the experience of mainland institutions. Family members may expect protection or income; local political actors may see his uniform as evidence of allegiance; he may see both home and service as necessary. A scene should make at least one of these demands concrete rather than announcing “divided identity” as abstraction.

Napoleon's letters from the learning period show a growing effort to understand how politics will affect his family. The correspondence study describes him trying to approach Paoli while recognizing that Charles's earlier choice to work with French power may have left the family exposed. This is a critical interpretation of letters and context, not a direct quotation of every motive. The canon retains the pressure while labelling the inference.

The travel itself matters. Ships, weather, passports, military leave, money, and the route from a mainland garrison to Ajaccio determine whether a return is possible. A letter written from Valence cannot be staged as a conversation in Ajaccio. Later scenes must preserve which family member is present, who carries news, and whether Napoleon's leave is authorized or improvised.

**Status:** Source-supported for Paoli's return as a decisive fact in Corsican politics and for Napoleon's attempt to engage with him; contested in the emotional sincerity and strategic calculation assigned to every letter.

Paoli's return converts a remembered hero into a living political authority. Napoleon's childhood image of Paoli now meets a man with followers, opponents, and a program that may not align with French republican power or the Bonaparte family's interests. The political test is therefore personal and institutional at once.

Napoleon's approach to Paoli must be staged through letters, intermediaries, public declarations, and factional observation. A letter can flatter, ask, conceal, or test; its rhetoric does not reveal the whole sender's mind. The correspondence study identifies a double movement in which Napoleon seeks Paoli's favor while maintaining relations with the French side. The setting accepts this as a source-based interpretation and refuses to make it a simple confession of hypocrisy.

The family becomes a political unit. Joseph, Lucien, Letizia, and other relatives may differ in courage, language, and risk tolerance. A public action in Ajaccio can expose a household far beyond the person who took it. Scenes must show who knows a plan, who is absent, and what a faction can infer from a uniform, portrait, or letter.

**Status:** Source-supported for the eventual break between Napoleon and Paoli and for the Bonaparte family's flight from Corsica in 1793; bounded in exact dates of each private disagreement and in the motives reconstructed from later memoirs.

The break is a process of incompatible authority. Paoli's legitimacy is rooted in Corsican political memory and personal following; Napoleon's advancing route lies through the French Republic and its army. A family trying to remain safe between them can become suspect to both. The storyline must identify the moment a letter, vote, command, or public accusation changes the available choices; settings does not pick a dramatic argument that the record does not preserve.

The Bonaparte family's flight from Ajaccio in 1793 is a material consequence. The Fondation article describes Letizia fleeing with Louis and Jérôme when Ajaccio became hostile. The age, route, vessel, possessions, and shelter of each family member require their own sourced owners. The setting does establish displacement, loss of local security, and the transformation of Corsica from home into a political danger.

The flight must not be narrated as a clean national conversion. Exile can produce grief, anger, pragmatism, and a new dependence on French institutions simultaneously. Napoleon's military service becomes a way to recover influence and support relatives, but the exact balance between family rescue and personal ambition remains a storyline question supported by dated actions.

**Status:** Work decision grounded in the documented commission, letters, and exile. Napoleon's republican service is a route of authority and family survival as well as an ideological field; exact interior motives remain contested.

The French army offers a young Corsican officer a career that local faction cannot fully control. Rank, pay, mobility, and access to generals can protect a family that has lost its position on the island. The same service also makes Napoleon an agent of a state that many Corsicans experience as coercive. The novel must hold both truths in the same scene when the viewpoint can perceive them.

Military service converts personal skill into institutional power through orders, reports, supplies, and legal authority. Napoleon's artillery knowledge gives him a specialized language, but the Republic's political crisis determines where that knowledge can be used. A junior officer cannot simply appoint himself to a decisive command; he needs recommendations, a vacancy, a superior's trust, and a situation in which his proposal is heard.

Family communication remains delayed. Letizia and the younger children cannot see a battlefield report at the moment Napoleon receives it; Napoleon cannot instantly know whether a letter has reached Ajaccio or a refugee vessel. The settings graph later needs each letter, leave, and appointment attached to the correct location and knowledge state.

Toulon is not the automatic reward for a political conversion. It is the next environment in which artillery skill, republican command, factional suspicion, and opportunity interact. Napoleon arrives with a commission, technical education, correspondence, and the experience of a family displaced by Corsican conflict. He does not arrive with the authority or knowledge of the later general.

The boundary protects both layers. Settings for Toulon must own the port, forts, roads, batteries, commanders, naval and land logistics, and sources for the decisive action. Storylines will decide when the reader learns that Napoleon's proposal matters; scenarios will stage gun movement, reconnaissance, reports, and command response; manuscripts will render the breakthrough in English prose. This file ends at the threshold.

The childhood return now has a complete pressure arc: island annexation, family adaptation, school displacement, technical education, bereavement, professional entry, revolution, Paoli's return, family flight, and republican service. The arc remains contingent. At each stage, a different letter, illness, order, or political decision could have altered the route.
