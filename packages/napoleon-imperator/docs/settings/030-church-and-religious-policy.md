# Church and Religious Policy

This file owns religion as institution, belief, property, record, and political settlement, handing regional religious histories to their campaign owners.

**Status:** Source-supported and regionally qualified. Revolutionary policy altered property, clergy, and public worship unevenly.

The Fondation's pre-Concordat study describes two Catholic churches coexisting: the constitutional church formed by clergy who accepted the 1791 Civil Constitution, and refractory or non-juring networks with different legitimacy and local reach. It records constitutional strength in some cities, weaker rural implantation, hidden bells later restored, and sanctuaries whose practice never wholly ceased. These facts prevent a single national rupture or restoration. A parishioner may attend a public service, shelter a priest, use a civil register, or preserve a family rite while officials argue over building ownership. The setting separates belief from institutional obedience and identifies who controls property, records a marriage or burial, collects a payment, and enforces a decree.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “L'état religieux de la France à la veille de la signature du Concordat,” lines 77–83 and 114–120, https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/letat-religieux-de-la-france-a-la-veille-de-la-signature-du-concordat/; Gallica, Napoleonic and revolutionary collections, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte/.

**Status:** Primary-text anchored. The Concordat permits public Catholic worship while embedding appointment, oath, salary, and publication in state authority.

The Concordat was signed on 15 July 1801 and became state law with the 8 April 1802 Organic Articles. The text recognizes Catholicism as the religion of the great majority rather than a state religion, permits public worship subject to public-order regulations, has the First Consul nominate bishops while the Pope gives canonical institution, requires an episcopal oath of fidelity, and provides government remuneration. The Organic Articles add that Roman documents cannot be received, printed, or put into effect without government authorization. This is a settlement and an administrative regime, not a simple spiritual reconciliation. A bishop's signature, a state salary, a papal bull, a parish ceremony, and a police decision are separate evidence objects.

**Status:** Work canon grounded in law. Religious policy becomes tangible through appointments, wages, buildings, registers, and family records.

The Concordat's Article XIV provides government remuneration for bishops, curés, and desservants; the Organic Articles put seminaries and ecclesiastical establishments under authorization and Council of State supervision. Those rules create office, payment, training, and appeal routes but do not tell us whether a village accepts a new priest or repairs a disputed church. A marriage, burial, baptism, or school interaction can involve parish clergy, civil officials, family witnesses, and property holders with different powers. The setting therefore names the certifying institution, building custodian, ledger, and enforcement authority. Public episcopal obedience cannot be used as proof that a household changed belief or that an old owner recovered a confiscated building.

**Status:** Work canon with source-critical variation. Army religion is a practice of institutions, bodies, and territory, not one private belief.

An oath, chaplain's service, funeral, or public prayer can stabilize a unit while leaving individual belief unknown. The 1808 Jewish decree instructs rabbis to encourage obedience, defense of the homeland, and conscription, and to ensure civil marriage or divorce precedes the religious ceremony. That is a legal and administrative mechanism, not evidence that every Jewish soldier or rabbi experienced service identically. Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, Muslim, Orthodox, and local communities also meet different jurisdictions and custodians across the empire. Settings identify who conducts a rite, who controls the building, which law governs the record, and who bears the military or political consequence; no public oath becomes proof of inner conviction.

**Status:** Primary-text anchored and source-critical. Recognition and civil equality operate through distinct, supervised institutions.

The Organic Articles organize French Reformed churches through pastors, local consistories, and synods, with a consistorial church per six thousand members and a hierarchy of five consistories to a synod. The 17 March 1808 Jewish decree creates a central consistory, requires approval and correspondence, regulates synagogue funds and unauthorized prayer assemblies, reports Israelite conscripts annually, and directs rabbis to require civil proof before religious marriage or divorce. These provisions can expand public organization while narrowing autonomous interpretation and increasing surveillance. The setting names the jurisdiction, language, communal record keeper, property rule, and official who interprets the phrase. “Toleration” is therefore not a universal experience and cannot erase inherited local custom.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Religious reconciliation stabilized government while leaving surveillance, property, confessional, and local conflicts active.

The critical religious history says Bonaparte sought peace and understood that clergy could serve policy, while official painting presented him as restoring the altars. It also records continued police action against preachers in 1801 and Organic regulation of processions where Protestant populations were strong. The Concordat's public language and its state controls therefore coexist. A clergyman may accept office without accepting every imperial aim; a parishioner may attend without changing belief; a former owner may litigate without regaining a building. The setting carries these mixed motives forward and treats ceremony as an observable political act, not proof that spiritual conflict has ended.

**Status:** Work boundary with source-supported mechanics. Imperial policy travels through officials but enters sacred spaces that already have custodians, schedules, and memories.

The pre-Concordat study records bells hidden by villagers and later restored, renewed pilgrimages to Notre-Dame de la Délivrande and Notre-Dame d'Auray, relics of Saint-Denis recovered from private custody, and Organic restrictions on processions in towns with strong Protestant communities. These examples make a sacred place material: someone holds keys, objects, registers, money, and a schedule; a procession occupies a street; police can authorize or stop it; a cemetery has families and jurisdiction. The same rule cannot describe a church, mosque, synagogue, chapel, shrine, or cemetery in Egypt, Iberia, Italy, or German lands. Regional owners must specify local law and community authority rather than use a generic religious reaction.

**Status:** Work boundary. This file supplies national policy mechanics; regional religious histories belong to their campaign and occupation owners.

Every later use must name the local authority, community, law, sacred building, record keeper, and route by which a national rule arrives. The Concordat may establish a public settlement, but it does not tell a village how to divide a church, an army how to bury its dead, or a Jewish consistory how to respond to a particular official. Private belief remains distinct from public compliance; property and registers remain distinct from ceremony. Downstream settings inherit that tension and must attach each local claim to its own evidence chain. No single Concordat, Organic Article, or imperial decree makes every region religiously identical.
