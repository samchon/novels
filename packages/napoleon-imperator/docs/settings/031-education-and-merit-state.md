**Status:** Source-supported. Schools form state personnel and citizens through organized curriculum, selection, and unequal access.

The educational history describes the public system as a means of creating an institutional corps, while the primary documents require fidelity to the Emperor and imperial monarchy from members of the University. This is a state claim about useful knowledge and loyalty, not proof that every classroom functioned alike. A student needs a teacher, timetable, room, books, food, and time away from labor; an examination needs an examiner, standard, and record; a certificate opens some careers without erasing family money or patronage. The setting therefore distinguishes announced reform from a functioning school, and school attendance from later administrative power. Geography, gender, rank, and cost remain active filters in every cohort.

**Status:** Work canon with source-critical limits. Examinations can measure performance while opportunity and appointment remain socially distributed.

Napoleon's own education and career show technical advancement, institutional opportunity, and patronage rather than one simple formula. A vacancy may be filled after an examination, recommendation, family introduction, military performance, or a combination no surviving letter fully reveals. The record may prove the appointment while leaving the influence behind it uncertain. A later setting must therefore name the candidate's training, examiner, score or demonstrated work where available, recommender, office, and competing claimant. Merit is neither an invisible superpower nor a fiction: it operates inside unequal access to books, tutors, travel, language, and leisure. The novel must not claim that every imperial official earned office alone or that every appointment was arbitrary.

**Status:** Source-required domain. A decree cannot teach a lesson without rooms, books, instruments, teachers, money, and time.

The institutional history notes that finding suitable buildings and recruiting teachers delayed lycée creation: forty-five were progressively planned, but thirty-five opened by 1808. That gap is the educational mechanism. A curriculum lists languages, history, rhetoric, logic, and mathematics; a student still needs a desk, text, instructor, food, uniform, transport, and release from agricultural or domestic labor. Fees and distance can exclude a child before an examination; a missing instrument can make technical instruction nominal. Settings therefore identify who owns the building, funds the staff, supplies the text, chooses the language, and records attendance. Examination success is an intellectual and social-material event, not a floating measure of talent.

The Fondation's account of Madame Campan records her appointment to the first Maison Impériale Napoléon on 5 September 1807 and describes instruction in history, geography, grammar, living languages, mathematics, religion, and continuing study beyond sixteen or seventeen. Her school model was not an empty domestic ornament, yet its students' routes differed from the male military and administrative pipeline. **Status:** Work canon grounded in institutional history. Lycées create disciplined cohorts and future personnel over time, with failure and resentment alongside selection.

The institutional account describes lycée teaching in ancient languages, history, rhetoric, logic, and elements of mathematical and physical science, while noting that the first schools required time to find buildings and teachers. The later University system did not erase those school histories; it organized public instruction through academies, rectors, inspectors, and examinations. A pupil therefore has a cohort, school, dormitory or household, uniform, teacher, timetable, disciplinary code, and failure condition before becoming an officer or clerk. The setting permits pride, resentment, illness, expulsion, and uneven preparation. It does not let a later storyline cite “Napoleonic education” as a single causal explanation for an individual's skill.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Technical schooling expands capacity through trained practice, but training is not field competence by itself.

The University history describes a centralized system with a grand-master, chancellor, treasurer, inspectors, thirty-two academies, and 400,000 francs in funded revenue, supplemented by examination and student fees. Such architecture can train engineers, scientists, officers, and administrators, but it still requires instruments, maps, books, reliable data, transport, money, and orders. A student who passes an examination has demonstrated a bounded performance; an engineer who surveys a road or artillery officer who lays a battery has demonstrated a field task under material pressure. Later scenes must show that test and its failure conditions. “Merit” never floats free of labor, tools, institutions, and the authority willing to use the result.

**Status:** Work boundary. National architecture supplies constraints; local and institutional reform must be researched by their own owners and dates.

The 1802 lycée law, 10 May 1806 University law, and 17 March and 17 September 1808 decrees mark stages, not an instant completed system. The institutional history records 35 lycée openings by 1808 despite the larger planned network and describes the University’s academies, finance, and personnel. Écoles centrales, lycées, École polytechnique, military schools, primary schools, and Campan's imperial houses are related but not interchangeable. No decree supplies equal education by itself.
