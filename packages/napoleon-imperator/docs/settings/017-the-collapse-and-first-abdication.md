# The Collapse and the First Abdication, 1813–1814

This file gives the German campaign of 1813 and the campaign of France of 1814 the depth `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-leipzig` and `#later-first-abdication-elba` summarize but do not carry to scene-level, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`. It does not repeat Leipzig's own strength figures or the Treaty of Fontainebleau's territorial terms, both already established at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-leipzig` and `#later-first-abdication-elba`; it supplies the campaigns and the abdication process around them. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Rebuilding the army after Russia {#collapse-rebuild-1813}

**Standing: established by research for the concentration figures; decided for this fiction that "Marie-Louises" is available period-appropriate vocabulary, resting on the well-attested cavalry shortfall the term describes.** By the end of March 1813, Napoleon had concentrated roughly 200,000 men in the angle formed by the Elbe and the Saale, drawing on conscription pushed hard across the satellite states of the Confederation of the Rhine and on the 1811–12 National Guard cohorts as a reservoir of half-trained men for new battalions. The army's defining weakness was cavalry: 1812 had destroyed the mounted arm almost completely (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-retreat`), and the new army could not replace trained horses or riders at anything like the rate it replaced infantry. This canon has not located the specific term "Marie-Louises" — the popular name for the very young 1813 conscripts, after the regent empress who signed their call-up decrees — in the source it consulted for the 1813 concentration figures, but treats the underlying fact the term describes (a young, inexperienced army with a severe cavalry deficit) as established, and the vocabulary itself as decided-for-fiction period color resting on that established fact.

Sources: Wikipedia, "German campaign of 1813," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_campaign_of_1813.

## Lützen and Bautzen {#collapse-lutzen-bautzen}

**Standing: established by research, with total strengths disputed between sources and given as ranges.** At Lützen, 2 May 1813, fought on open cavalry-suited plains, Napoleon's forces (Ney, Soult, Marmont, Bertrand, Macdonald, Eugène) numbered 78,000–145,000 with 372 guns against a Coalition army (Wittgenstein in command, with Blücher, Tsar Alexander, and Frederick William III present) of 70,000–96,000 with 552 guns and superior cavalry and artillery. French casualties ran 19,655–22,000 against Allied losses of 11,500–30,000. It was a French win, but Napoleon needed only two more hours of daylight to make it decisive; nightfall, combined with the lack of cavalry and faulty reconnaissance, prevented pursuit. At Bautzen, 20–21 May, Russo-Prussian forces under Wittgenstein, Barclay de Tolly, and Blücher held two defensive lines east of the Spree, anchored on the town of Bautzen and a chain of lakes; Napoleon's 144,000 faced roughly 96,000–97,000 Coalition troops. French casualties ran 20,000–25,000 against Coalition losses of 11,000–20,000 — another tactical win, again not decisive, this time because Ney's confused positioning left the door open for the Allies to escape rather than be cut off and encircled. Peter Wittgenstein assumed supreme Allied command after Kutuzov's death in April 1813 and led at both battles before laying down the command following these defeats and taking a corps command instead at Dresden and Leipzig; Barclay de Tolly succeeded him as supreme Allied commander thereafter.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Lützen (1813)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_L%C3%BCtzen_(1813); "Battle of Bautzen (1813)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bautzen_(1813); "Peter Wittgenstein," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Wittgenstein.

## The Armistice of Pläswitz {#collapse-armistice-plaswitz}

**Standing: established by research.** Agreed 4 June 1813 and scheduled to run to 10 July, the Armistice of Pläswitz was extended to 10 August; its terms conceded all of Saxony to Napoleon in return for territory along the Oder. During the truce, Metternich concluded the Treaty of Reichenbach with the Allies on 27 June, under which Austria would join the Coalition if Napoleon failed to meet specified conditions by a set date; he did not meet them, the armistice lapsed without renewal, and Austria declared war on 12 August. Napoleon later described the armistice as the greatest mistake of his life — a self-assessment this canon records as his own retrospective verdict rather than a documented contemporaneous judgment, since it gave Austria, Prussia, and Russia six weeks in which to reorganize and coordinate their forces while his own army, still recovering from Russia, gained comparatively less.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Armistice of Pläswitz," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_Pl%C3%A4switz.

## Dresden, Kulm, and Katzbach: victories that did not matter {#collapse-dresden-kulm-katzbach}

**Standing: established by research.** Within the same five days, Napoleon won a real victory that changed nothing, and two of his subordinates lost battles that did. At Dresden, 26–27 August 1813, Napoleon (with Ney, Marmont, and Murat), 100,000–135,000 strong, defeated Schwarzenberg's Coalition army of 200,000–215,000; French losses ran roughly 10,000 against Coalition losses of 38,000–40,000 (15,000 killed or wounded, 25,000 captured or missing, forty guns and fifteen colours lost). General Jean Victor Marie Moreau — once a French general, by 1813 a Coalition military adviser recently returned from exile in the United States — was mortally wounded in the battle and died 2 September at Louny. Despite the scale of the win, it did not lead to the coalition's collapse. Two days earlier, at Katzbach, 26 August, Macdonald's 60,000–75,000 stumbled into Blücher's and Osten-Sacken's 80,000–95,000 at nine in the morning after crossing the swollen Katzbach river; flooding destroyed bridges and fords, muskets were too wet to fire, and the action was decided by cold steel before French cavalry and two divisions were routed at 6:30 p.m. by Blücher's whole army, with hundreds drowning in the Katzbach and the Raging Neisse during the retreat. French losses ran to roughly 30,000 by 1 September (12,000 killed or wounded, 18,000 captured) plus 103 guns, against Coalition losses of roughly 22,000 across the whole campaign; news of Blücher's win, per the historical judgment this canon adopts, "revitalized the worried Allied leadership, effectively negating Napoleon's concurrent victory at Dresden." At Kulm, 29–30 August, Vandamme's 32,000–37,000 pursued too far beyond Dresden and were cut off by a growing Coalition force under Osterman-Tolstoy, Yermolov, Kleist, Colloredo-Mansfeld, and Barclay that reached roughly 103,000; his inexperienced troops, formed into squares, could not hold, and French losses ran 13,000–25,000 plus prisoners and eighty-two of eighty-four guns, with Vandamme himself captured, against Coalition losses of 11,000–12,319.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Dresden," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dresden; "Battle of Katzbach," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Katzbach; "Battle of Kulm," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kulm.

## The Elster bridge and the destruction of Poniatowski {#collapse-elster-bridge}

**Standing: established by research.** Leipzig's own scale, dates, and disputed strength figures are fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-leipzig`; this entry records the retreat's specific disaster. On the night of 18 October 1813, with the battle going against him, Napoleon promoted Prince Józef Poniatowski to Marshal of the Empire — the only foreign-born officer ever given the rank — and Poniatowski swore he would fight to the last stand, which he did. The retreat plan routed the whole army through Leipzig and across a single bridge over the Elster, with Reynier's VII, Poniatowski's VIII, and Macdonald's XI Corps ordered to hold the city as rearguard; Napoleon failed to order the construction of additional bridges. On the afternoon of 19 October, General Dulauloy, tasked with destroying the bridge once the army had crossed, delegated the job to Colonel Montfort, who in turn delegated it to a corporal unaware of the planned timing. The corporal lit the fuses at one in the afternoon while the bridge was still crowded with retreating French troops and Oudinot's rearguard was still fighting in the city; the resulting explosion and panic caused a rout that killed thousands and delivered some 30,000 French troops into captivity. Oudinot and Macdonald both swam the river to safety. Poniatowski, hampered by wounds already taken that day, attempted to cross the Elstermühlgraben, was probably shot by his own allies by mistake in the confusion, and drowned — dying 19 October 1813, aged fifty; his remains were moved to Wawel Cathedral, Kraków, in 1817.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Leipzig," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leipzig, whose account of the Elster bridge disaster is attributed there to David G. Chandler, *The Campaigns of Napoleon* (1966), pp. 935–936, and to Tim J. Bowden (1990), p. 191 — this canon has not opened either directly, consistent with its existing non-access to Chandler recorded at `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`, and repeats the episode as Wikipedia's own synthesis; "Józef Poniatowski," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Poniatowski, read directly for the date, age, and reburial detail.

## Hanau {#collapse-hanau}

**Standing: established by research.** At Hanau, 30–31 October 1813, an Austro-Bavarian force of roughly 42,000 under Wrede (33,000 infantry, 9,000 cavalry, ninety-four guns) tried to block the retreating army's line of march; French forces of 17,000–30,000 (roughly 20,000 actively engaged) defeated them, with French losses of 4,500–5,000 against Austro-Bavarian losses of 6,000 killed or wounded plus 4,000 captured. Wrede's flawed deployment — most of his army positioned with the river at its back, foreclosing its own retreat — combined with dense forest that let the French mass unseen and fifty French guns against his twenty-eight; by 31 October Hanau was in French hands and the retreat's line of march to the Rhine lay open.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Hanau," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hanau.

## The campaign of France {#collapse-campaign-france-1814}

**Standing: established by research, with strengths disputed between sources.** The Coalition invaded France from January 1814. At La Rothière, 1 February — Napoleon's one clear defeat of the campaign — Blücher commanded tactically under Schwarzenberg's overall direction; strength figures diverge sharply between historians (Petre gives roughly 85,000 Coalition against 45,100 French; Bodart gives 123,000 against 41,000), and casualties likewise (Smith: 4,600 French killed or wounded plus 1,000 captured and seventy-three guns lost, against 6,000–7,000 Coalition; Petre: roughly 6,000 on each side, with the French losing 2,000–3,000 captured and fifty to sixty guns). The defeat demonstrated, in the judgment this canon adopts, "that the Emperor was not invincible." What followed reversed that impression sharply: the Six Days' Campaign, 10–15 February 1814, saw Napoleon's roughly 30,000 defeat Blücher's Army of Silesia of 50,000–56,000 in four successive actions — Champaubert (10 February, Olsufiev's Russian IX Corps destroyed, roughly 4,000 Coalition casualties against 200 French, Olsufiev captured), Montmirail (11 February, the Osten-Sacken/Yorck vanguard defeated, 4,000 Coalition casualties against 2,000 French), Château-Thierry (12 February, the same forces beaten again, 1,250 Prussian and 1,500 Russian casualties and nine guns lost against roughly 600 French), and Vauchamps (14 February, Blücher's main body defeated near Étoges, 7,000 Prussian casualties and sixteen guns lost against roughly 600 French) — eliminating some 20,000 enemy troops in six days at a fraction of the cost inflicted, a tactical performance widely treated as among the finest of his career even as it could not, by itself, change the campaign's strategic arithmetic. At Arcis-sur-Aube, 20–21 March, Napoleon (with Oudinot, Ney, and Sébastiani), growing from 18,000 to 28,000–30,000 across the two days, faced a much larger Coalition force under Schwarzenberg, Tsar Alexander, Wrede, and the Crown Prince of Württemberg (74,000–107,900); French losses ran 3,000–4,200 plus three guns against Allied losses of 3,000–4,000, and Napoleon, recognizing he had blundered into a battle against overwhelming numbers, ordered a masked retreat and disengaged successfully.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of La Rothière," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_La_Roti%C3%A8re; "Six Days' Campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Days%27_Campaign; "Battle of Arcis-sur-Aube," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arcis-sur-Aube.

## The fall of Paris {#collapse-fall-paris}

**Standing: established by research.** On 29 March 1814 a Coalition army of some 200,000 attacked the Belleville and Montmartre heights above Paris; Marie-Louise fled the city that evening with the King of Rome. Joseph Bonaparte, defending with only 38,000 troops, authorized Marshal Marmont to negotiate a capitulation, and Allied forces entered the city 31 March to shouts of "Vive les Bourbons! Vivent les souverains! Vivent nos libérateurs!" On 2 April the Senate voted to depose Napoleon and his entire family in the *Acte de déchéance de l'Empereur*. Marmont's own corps had fought a genuine fighting retreat to the commanding position of Essonne, inflicting real losses on the enemy, before he separately contacted the Allies and reached a secret agreement that, with Mortier and Moncey, surrendered their forces once surrounded. The betrayal entered the language directly: the verb "raguser," from his later title Duke of Ragusa, became a household word in French meaning "to betray," and — per a widely repeated account this canon records without independent verification of its precise wording — thirty years later, as an old man living in Venice, children in the street would point and say, "There goes the man who betrayed Napoleon."

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Paris (1814)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Paris_(1814); "Napoleon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon, "Fall" section; "Auguste Marmont," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Marmont.

## Fontainebleau and the abdication {#collapse-fontainebleau-abdication}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact night of the suicide attempt not fully settled between sources.** Napoleon retreated to Fontainebleau with an army of 40,000–60,000 and contemplated a march on Paris. On 4 April 1814, Marshal Ney became the spokesman for what historians call the marshals' revolt, telling Napoleon the army would not march on the capital; Napoleon's reported reply, "the army will obey me!", drew Ney's answer, "the army will obey its chiefs." Napoleon signed a first, conditional abdication that same day in favor of his son, Napoleon II, with Marie-Louise as regent — the surviving text, of three drafted that day, states that "the Emperor Napoleon, faithful to his oath, declares that he is ready to descend from the throne, to leave [France], and even to lay down his life for the welfare of his country, which is inseparable from the rights of his son, those of the regency of the Empress, and the maintenance of the laws of the empire." Tsar Alexander demanded an unconditional abdication instead, and Napoleon reluctantly complied on 6 April. The Treaty of Fontainebleau's terms — Elba as a sovereign principality, Marie-Louise's Italian duchies, the 2,500,000-franc family pension, the 400-man guard ceiling — are already fixed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-first-abdication-elba`; this canon repeats here only the signing sequence, already noted there as well: the plenipotentiaries (Caulaincourt, Macdonald, and Ney for France) signed the treaty in Paris on 11 April, and Napoleon personally ratified it on 13 April.

In the interval between signing and ratification, Napoleon attempted suicide with poison he had carried since nearly being captured during the retreat from Russia (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-retreat`); its potency had weakened with age, and he survived. This canon does not fully settle the exact night: the most-cited secondary account places the attempt "the following night" after the 11 April treaty, which read literally would put it the night of 11–12 April, while the conventional dating in popular biography — which this canon has not been able to independently verify against a primary or tertiary source in this research pass — gives the night of 12–13 April. This canon treats 12–13 April as the working date, consistent with the more commonly cited convention, but flags the 11 April treaty text's own phrasing as the source of the ambiguity rather than presenting either night as fully settled.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Napoleon," "Fall" section, as above, which attributes the suicide-attempt account there to Frank McLynn, *Napoleon: A Biography* (1997), pp. 593–594 — this canon has not opened McLynn's book directly and repeats the account, including its own ambiguity about the exact night, only as Wikipedia reports it; "Michel Ney," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ney, whose account of the 4 April confrontation is attributed there to David Gates, *The Napoleonic Wars 1803–1815* (2003), p. 259 — likewise not opened directly by this canon; "Treaty of Fontainebleau (1814)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Fontainebleau_(1814); The Napoleon Series, "Napoleon's Addresses," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/napoleon/speeches/c_speeches13.html, for the first-abdication text, cross-referenced against Charles-Éloi Vial, "4, 6 et 11 avril 1814: les trois actes d'abdication de Napoléon Ier," *Napoleonica. La Revue*, 19 (2014), for the correct 4 April date of the first abdication act, which the napoleon-series.org page itself mislabels "April 2"; `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-first-abdication-elba`, established by research, for the treaty's territorial and financial terms.

## Farewell to the Old Guard {#collapse-farewell-guard}

**Standing: established by research, wording treated as a directly sourced primary text.** On 20 April 1814, in the Cour du Cheval-Blanc at Fontainebleau, Napoleon addressed the Old Guard before departing for Elba. The full text, from the same public-domain 1897 translation this canon already uses for the Austerlitz and Borodino proclamations (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`; `settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-proclamation`): "Soldiers of my old guard, I bid you farewell. For twenty years I have constantly accompanied you on the road to honor and glory. In these latter times, as in the days of our prosperity, you have invariably been models of courage and fidelity. With men such as you our cause could not be lost; but the war would have been interminable; it would have been civil war, and that would have entailed deeper misfortunes on France. I have sacrificed all my interests to those of the country. I go, but you, my friends, will continue to serve France. Her happiness was my only thought. It will still be the object of my wishes. Do not regret my fate; if I have consented to survive, it is to serve your glory. I intend to write the history of the great achievements we have performed together. Adieu, my friends. Would I could press you all to my heart." The text continues: "Napoleon then ordered the eagles to be brought, and, having embraced them, he added: 'I embrace you all in the person of your general. Adieu, soldiers! Be always gallant and good.'" The general who received the eagles on the Guard's behalf, and whom Napoleon embraced, was General Jean-Martin Petit, then commanding the Old Guard.

Sources: Wikisource, "Napoleon's Addresses/Part V," https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_V, read directly for the full public-domain text; Wikipedia, "Jean-Martin Petit," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Martin_Petit, which attributes Petit's identity and role there to Andrew Roberts, *Napoleon: A Life* (2014), p. 764 — this canon has not opened Roberts's book to that specific page and repeats the identification as Wikipedia reports it, consistent with how this canon already treats Roberts as a named-scholarship anchor rather than a directly page-verified source at `settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-ambition`; napoleon.org, image caption for the Jazet engraving after Horace Vernet's painting of the scene, Musée national du château de Malmaison, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/images/the-adieux-de-fontainebleau-detail/, for a slightly different published-translation variant of the opening lines, noted here but not adopted over the Wikisource text.
