**Status:** Source-supported for the army's shortages and Napoleon's use of speed and supply; exact strengths vary by return.

The Army of Italy begins exhausted, short of uniforms, ammunition, food, horses, and reliable transport. A critical Army of Italy study distinguishes a March 1796 theoretical strength of 116,626 from 51,665 unavailable (including 38,119 sick), 24,170 in rear garrisons, and a field force of roughly 66,483; these are one study's returns, not a timeless exact OOB. Bonaparte's 27 March proclamation openly turns deprivation into a promise of fertile provinces, honor, glory, and wealth; rhetoric does not fill a magazine. Every march consumes shoes, animals, cartridges, and time; requisition changes civilian relations and can produce resistance. Scenes must give units uneven readiness rather than a uniform “French army.”

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Napoleon's victory reports are valuable evidence for official claims and command language, not neutral casualty or civilian records.

A dispatch compresses a battle to persuade a superior; a bulletin compresses it to persuade the public. The first Italian campaign also produced newspapers intended to maintain army morale and inform France of the general-in-chief's victories, so “public report” includes an active institutional audience rather than an abstract press. **Status:** Source-supported campaign constraint. Napoleon's early Italian success depends on separating opponents before they can combine, not on defeating one undifferentiated coalition.

The army must move through roads, passes, river crossings, and towns while enemy headquarters exchange orders.
