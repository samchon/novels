## Army of Italy condition and resources {#army-of-italy-condition-and-resources}

**Status:** Source-supported for the army's shortages and Napoleon's use of speed and supply; exact strengths vary by return.

The Army of Italy begins exhausted, short of uniforms, ammunition, food, horses, and reliable transport. A proclamation promising glory and resources does not fill a magazine. Every march consumes shoes, animals, cartridges, and time; requisition changes civilian relations and can produce resistance. Scenes must give units uneven readiness rather than a uniform “French army.”

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The life of Napoleon I: a timeline for 6 years old +,” https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/chrono-consulat-1er-empire/; Fondation Napoléon, “Napoleon’s ‘Grande Armée’ (1),” https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/napoleons-grande-armee-1/.

## Speed and concentration {#speed-and-concentration}

**Status:** Work canon grounded in campaign history. Napoleon's operational method seeks to separate enemy armies and concentrate against one before another can intervene; terrain and information limit the method.

Speed is a system of roads, guides, staff work, food, bridges, weather, and marching discipline. A column that arrives first may be hungry or dispersed. Reports from scouts and local officials are partial. The storyline must connect each concentration to a decision and an information state, not treat “maneuver” as magic.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/; Fondation Napoléon, “The correspondence of Napoleon,” https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.

## Arcole and Rivoli as distinct battles {#arcole-and-rivoli-as-distinct-battles}

**Status:** Source-supported for Arcole and Rivoli as major victories; exact scene ownership, orders, and casualties remain separate.

Arcole's marshes, bridges, and repeated attempts cannot be merged with Rivoli's heights, roads, and artillery geometry. Rivoli's victory depends on concentration and terrain, not a generic heroic charge. Each battle owner must distinguish direct orders, later bulletins, memoirs, and modern reconstruction.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The life of Napoleon I: a timeline for 6 years old +,” https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/chrono-consulat-1er-empire/; Fondation Napoléon, “The Boyhood and Youth of Napoleon,” https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-boyhood-and-youth-of-napoleon/.

## Victory, extraction, and political capital {#victory-extraction-and-political-capital}

**Status:** Work canon. Italian victories create money, prestige, correspondence, and political leverage while imposing deaths, requisitions, and occupied populations.

The army's success yields captured material and negotiating power, but extraction can alienate civilians and allies. Napoleon's dispatches frame victories for the Directory; soldiers and local populations experience them differently. Later settings must preserve this dual ledger and avoid presenting bulletin language as neutral fact.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The correspondence of Napoleon,” https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/; Fondation Napoléon, “Napoleon’s ‘Grande Armée’ (1),” https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/napoleons-grande-armee-1/.

## Dispatches and bulletin truth {#dispatches-and-bulletin-truth}

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Napoleon's victory reports are valuable evidence for official claims and command language, not neutral casualty or civilian records.

A dispatch compresses a battle to persuade a superior; a bulletin compresses it to persuade the public. The novel must identify whether a sentence comes from an order, a private letter, a staff return, a later memoir, or a modern synthesis. Exact quotations require the named edition and translation.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The correspondence of Napoleon,” https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/; Fondation Napoléon, correspondence publication history, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/publication-of-the-complete-correspondance-of-napoleon-i-initial-results-of-the-fondation-napoleons-grand-project/.
