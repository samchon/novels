The constitutional text gives the consuls and First Consul powers over ministers, officers, local administration, promulgation, and revenue, but the installation chronology places the legislative bodies and consular offices into operation through later appointments and meetings. The morning after a coup therefore contains forms, seals, orders, and frightened clerks, not an instant administrative reset. Secretaries, departmental staff, soldiers, printers, judges, and messengers carry paper authority into practice; their labour lets an executive claim continuity while foreign replies, pay, tax receipts, and local resistance test whether that claim is true. Local implementation remains a downstream record question rather than an invented uniform result.

**Status:** Source-critical. The regime's narrative of saving the Republic must be presented alongside intimidation, censorship, arrests, and exclusion.

A proclamation can call troops protectors while a deputy experiences force. Bonaparte's 19 Brumaire text is evidence for what the new authority wanted readers to believe, not independent proof that every deputy consented. The critical legal study dates the Constitution to 13 December, notes its later entry into force and the plebiscite announced on 7 February 1800, and therefore blocks a shortcut from rescue rhetoric to completed legality. Later bulletins and memoirs may celebrate order; police, parliamentary, and administrative records can show fear, exclusion, or opposition. The settings layer preserves the contradiction for downstream focalization: public protection, legal form, and experienced coercion must remain separate claims with separate source standing.
