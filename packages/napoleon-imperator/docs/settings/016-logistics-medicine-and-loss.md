**Status:** Work canon. Armies move on grain, meat, water, forage, carts, magazines, and requisition; captured territory is not an infinite warehouse.

Staff officers must locate supplies, protect them, issue them, and account for spoilage or theft. Every campaign inherits a dated depot, route, issue, consumption, and local-cost state rather than an infinite warehouse.

**Status:** Historical principle. Cavalry, guns, ambulances, and baggage compete for horses and roads.

The horse study describes the animal as central to both movement and traction and gives a September 1805 ratio of roughly one horse per 5.57 men, while also warning that roads, gradients, losses, and vehicle loads constrain what a train can carry. **Status:** Source-required domain. Wounds, epidemics, exposure, and medical evacuation alter effective strength and family life.

The medical-service studies distinguish Larrey's flying ambulance from Percy's Wurtz: one evacuates wounded toward the rear, the other brings surgical personnel and dressings nearer the field. Their use was uneven; the flying ambulance did not generalize across the army until 1812 and collection of wounded remained poor. The Egypt service account records ships, instruments, medicines, staff, and planned ambulances while also describing surgery without anesthesia and infection risk. A casualty may lie on the field, reach a dressing station, travel by cart, die in a hospital, return, or disappear from records. Campaign writing must show the route and its failure, not a clean transition from wound to number.
