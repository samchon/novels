# Family and Relations

This file gives figure-level units for Napoleon's parents, siblings, wives, and stepchildren, and relation-level units for the bonds among them, per `principles/settings.md#figures-and-relations`. Dated family events are cross-referenced to `settings/002-chronology.md` rather than repeated here.

## Carlo Buonaparte and Letizia Ramolino {#family-parents}

**Standing: established by research, with one attributed remark flagged as disputed.** Carlo Buonaparte (b. 29 March 1746, d. 24 February 1785, of stomach cancer, at Montpellier) was a Corsican lawyer of minor, cash-poor nobility, initially aide to independence leader Pasquale Paoli, who reconciled with French rule after the 1768 annexation (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-corsica-annexation`) and became assessor to the royal court at Ajaccio, then, from 1777, Corsica's noble representative at Louis XVI's court. As part of the 1777 Corsican noble deputation to Versailles he secured Napoleon's free admission to Brienne — the single enabling act of his son's entire career (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-education`). Letizia Ramolino Bonaparte (b. 24 August 1750, d. 2 February 1836, Rome), "Madame Mère," was the formidable matriarch who raised eight surviving children through wartime hardship and, from 1785, widowhood — thrifty, stoic, and unsentimental by every account, and famously skeptical of the family's later meteoric fortune. The remark attributed to her regarding the imperial coronation, which she pointedly did not attend — "Pourvu que cela dure" ("Provided it lasts") — is widely repeated but its earliest traceable sources are early-twentieth-century secondary works rather than a contemporary document; historians generally treat it as characteristic even if not verbatim. She owes her son's later position a debt she never fully credits as durable; he owes her, and knows he owes her, the practical competence in running a household on almost nothing that let the family survive their father's death.

Sources: Britannica, "Carlo Maria Buonaparte," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Carlo-Maria-Buonaparte (b. 29 March 1746, d. 24 February 1785, Montpellier); Britannica, "Letizia Buonaparte," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Letizia-Buonaparte, and Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Maria-Letizia Ramolino-Buonaparte," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/laetitia_ramolino.php — both giving her birth as 24 August 1750, correcting the 1749 date carried by several popular summaries; histoire-en-citations.fr, "Madame Mère: « Pourvu que cela dure »," https://histoire-en-citations.fr/WP/madame-mere-pourvu-que-cela-dure/, which traces the remark no earlier than *Mercure de France*, vol. CXXXI (1919), confirming it is not a contemporary document.

## Joseph Bonaparte {#family-joseph}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 7 January 1768, Ajaccio, the eldest surviving brother. Trained as a lawyer (Pisa, 1787); calm, methodical, and an effective negotiator who preferred salons and literature to the battlefield — he was the lead architect of the 1800 Convention of Mortefontaine (signed at his own estate) and lead negotiator of both Lunéville (1801) and Amiens (1802). By December 1805 he holds no throne: he is a French imperial prince since 1804, a Senator since 2 August 1802, Grand Elector of the Empire (created 18 May 1804, concurrent with the Empire's own founding — not 1805, a date this canon previously carried in error), and — per Fondation Napoléon's own biography — appointed "Lieutenant Commander-in-Chief of the Armée de Naples" only at the close of the 1805 campaign, before exchanging that uniform for the crown of Naples on 31 March 1806. His kingships (Naples 1806, Spain 1808, the latter detailed at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-peninsular-begins`) come later. This canon does not attribute those titles to him in any 1805-set material.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "BONAPARTE, Joseph (1768–1844), roi de Naples puis roi d'Espagne," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/bonaparte-joseph/ (birth, law studies, the Mortefontaine/Lunéville/Amiens negotiations, the refusal of the Italian crown, and the Armée de Naples appointment at the close of the 1805 campaign); the French Senate's own archive, "BONAPARTE Joseph, ex roi d'Espagne," https://www.senat.fr/pair-de-france/bonaparte_josephpf1319.html, and the Assemblée nationale's Base Sycomore, https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/sycomore/fiche?num_dept=15883 (Grand Elector 28 Floréal Year XII = 18 May 1804 — the correction recorded above; sénateur de droit 22 Thermidor Year XII = 10 August 1804); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "List of Senators during the First French Empire," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/institutions/list-senators-first-french-empire.php (original Senate appointment 2 August 1802, sénatorerie of Brussels 1803–1804).

## Lucien Bonaparte {#family-lucien}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 1775. A fervent Jacobin in the Revolution and President of the Council of Five Hundred in 1799, where his dramatic intervention was instrumental in enabling the Brumaire coup (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-brumaire`). Intellectually independent and genuinely alarmed at Napoleon's monarchical drift, the estrangement between the brothers crystallized specifically over Lucien's October 1803 marriage to Alexandrine de Bleschamp, widow "Jouberthon" — a banker's widow Napoleon considered beneath Bonaparte dynastic dignity, their son already born in May 1803. Napoleon refused to recognize the marriage and pressed for its annulment; rather than comply, Lucien went into self-imposed exile to Rome in 1804, refusing all imperial honors. By December 1805 this breach is established and public: Lucien lives abroad, estranged, pursuing scholarship, still privately hoping for eventual reconciliation and a place in the succession.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "Lucien Bonaparte (Prince of Canino), 1775–1840, Minister," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/lucien-bonaparte-prince-of-canino-1775-1840-minister/ (birth 21 May 1775, Ajaccio; Jacobin activity and the Council of Five Hundred presidency from October 1799; his role at Saint-Cloud on 19 Brumaire; the confrontation with Napoleon over the Bleschamp/Jouberthon marriage; departure for Rome in April 1804); Fondation Napoléon genealogy database, "Alexandrine JACOB de BLESCHAMP," https://www.napoleon.org/wp-content/themes/napoleon/annexes/genealogie/detaillee/fr/detail/54.html (civil marriage dated 26 October 1803, at Chamant); Wikipedia, "Charles Lucien Bonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lucien_Bonaparte (their son born 24 May 1803, before the marriage was formalized).

## Elisa Bonaparte (Bacciochi) {#family-elisa}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 1777. Married to Felice Baciocchi, a largely decorative husband she outranks and dominates; widely regarded as the sibling most intellectually like Napoleon — disciplined, authoritarian, and a genuinely competent administrator. Napoleon made her sovereign Princess of Piombino in March 1805 and added Lucca later that year, so by December 1805 she rules the small Principality of Piombino and Lucca, running an elaborate court modeled on Paris despite a population of roughly 145,000. Her later elevation to Grand Duchess of Tuscany (1809) postdates this canon's primary window.

Sources: Britannica, "Élisa Bonaparte," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Elisa-Bonaparte; Wikipedia, "Principality of Lucca and Piombino," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Lucca_and_Piombino, whose population figure of roughly 145,000 is footnoted to the period source *Instituzioni di geografia fisica e politica per uso del primo Collegio reale* (1808), vol. 1, p. 74.

## Louis Bonaparte {#family-louis}

**Standing: established by research for the marriage's origin and its strain, upgraded to a primary-text citation for Napoleon's 1807 letter; the "eight days to consent" figure remains resting on weaker secondary sourcing, flagged below.** Born 4 September 1778, Ajaccio. Married Hortense de Beauharnais, Joséphine's daughter, on 4 January 1802 — per Fondation Napoléon's own account, "a maneuver by Joséphine, anxious to further unite the two families," and one Napoleon insisted upon. Hortense was reluctant — a popular-biography figure of roughly eight days to consent circulates, sourced to Thera Coppens, *Hortense: de vergeten koningin van Holland* (Meulenhoff, 2006), p. 126, which this canon has not itself opened and knows only through a secondary aggregator quoting it, so the figure is reported rather than independently confirmed — and the marriage was strained almost immediately, with mutual accusations and frequent separations despite producing three sons (Napoléon-Louis-Charles, 1802–1807; Napoléon-Louis, 1804–1831; and Louis-Napoléon, the future Napoleon III). Napoleon acknowledged the dysfunction directly in the *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier*, letter no. 12291, "Au Roi de Hollande," Finkenstein, 4 April 1807: "Vous avez la meilleure femme et la plus vertueuse, et vous la rendez malheureuse" (you have the best and most virtuous wife, and you make her unhappy) — a document this canon has now opened directly, correcting two errors it previously carried on secondary-aggregator authority alone: the date is 4 April 1807, not 2 May 1807 (the eldest son of the marriage in fact died at The Hague on 5 May 1807, likely the source of the garbled "2 May" figure), and the wording is "la meilleure femme et la plus vertueuse," the best and most virtuous wife, not the looser "excellent wife" this canon previously repeated. By December 1805 the marriage is three years old and already visibly strained; Louis holds no throne (King of Holland comes only in 1806).

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "BONAPARTE, Louis, (1778–1846), roi de Hollande, comte de Saint-Leu," https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/biographies/bonaparte-louis-1778-1846-roi-de-hollande-comte-de-saint-leu/ (birth, the marriage as Joséphine's maneuver, the three sons, and the mutual infidelity); Fondation Napoléon, "BEAUHARNAIS, Hortense de," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/beauharnais-hortense-de/ (the marriage's origin in Joséphine's intrigues, per Napoleon's own later account, and Hortense's reluctant compliance); *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier*, tome XV (Paris: Plon & Dumaine, 1858–70), full text read directly at archive.org, https://archive.org/details/correspondancede15napouoft, letter no. 12291, p. 24, for the corrected date and wording above — this canon retracts its prior reliance on History of Royal Women's secondary quotation of Coppens (2006) for this specific letter now that the primary text has been opened directly, though the "eight days" figure remains sourced only to that secondary aggregator's quotation of Coppens and is not independently confirmed by this canon against either primary text.

## Pauline Bonaparte {#family-pauline}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 1780. A famed beauty, vivacious and pleasure-seeking, previously married to General Leclerc (d. 1802, Saint-Domingue, during the campaign detailed at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#slavery-reinstatement`). Married Prince Camillo Borghese 6 November 1803 at Château de Mortefontaine — a match secured as much for Borghese wealth and Vatican-linked status as for affection; the marriage was not close, and Pauline's affairs continued after it, straining relations with her husband. She later posed for Canova's *Venus Victrix* (commissioned by Borghese, executed 1805–08), contemporaneous with this canon's primary window.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Venus Victrix (Canova)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_Victrix_(Canova)); [Wikipedia — Pauline Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Bonaparte).

## Caroline Bonaparte {#family-caroline}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 1782. Married Joachim Murat 20 January 1800 (civil) and 4 January 1802 (religious) — Napoleon initially opposed the match, favoring other candidates, and was talked around by Joséphine; full detail on the courtship and rivals is at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-murat`. Widely regarded by historians as the most ambitious, politically calculating, and independently power-hungry of the Bonaparte sisters — she used the marriage system for her own and Murat's advancement rather than merely submitting to it, and her maneuvering is credited with helping secure his successive titles.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Caroline Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Bonaparte); Britannica, "Caroline Bonaparte."

## Jérôme Bonaparte {#family-jerome}

**Standing: established by research, with the marriage's status corrected against this canon's own December 1805 window.** Born 15 November 1784, Ajaccio, the youngest sibling. Married American Elizabeth "Betsy" Patterson of Baltimore on 24 December 1803 without family or imperial consent (he 19, she 18, and the Civil Code required parental consent under 20). Napoleon was furious, ordered Jérôme back to France, argued the marriage invalid for lack of a legal guardian's consent, and barred the pregnant Elizabeth from landing on the Continent; the couple's son was born in Camberwell, London, in early July 1805 (sources give the 5th or the 7th). This canon corrects an error it previously carried about the marriage's status in its own December 1805 window: the state had already repudiated the marriage well before then — Napoleon issued an imperial decree refusing to recognize it in March 1805, months before this canon's setting date, not after it — so by December 1805 the civil position is settled against the marriage even though the religious question is not: Pope Pius VII refused to annul it, on the ground that a validly performed marriage between baptized Christians of different confessions, though disapproved by the Church, remains valid, and Elizabeth continues to call herself Madame Bonaparte. The fuller Council of State annulment follows in 1806 (commonly dated to that October), and Jérôme's remarriage to Princess Catherine of Württemberg is a civil ceremony of 22 August 1807 at the Tuileries — so 1807 is correct only for the remarriage, not for the annulment.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "BONAPARTE, Jerome," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/bonaparte-jerome (birth; the 24 December 1803 marriage; Napoleon's June 1804 order to separate and return to France; Elizabeth barred from disembarking, pregnant; the son's birth 7 July 1805; the family council's later ruling, after Jérôme's reconciliation with Napoleon on 6 May 1805, that the 1803 marriage was legally void); Wikipedia, "Elizabeth Patterson Bonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Patterson_Bonaparte (Pius VII's refusal to annul and its stated ground; the son born 5 July 1805 at 95 Camberwell Grove, London — the one-day variance against the napoleon.org date is left open rather than resolved); Wikipedia, "Jérôme Bonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Bonaparte, and frenchempire.net, "Jérôme Bonaparte (1784–1860)," https://www.frenchempire.net/biographies/bonaparte3/ (the March 1805 imperial decree refusing to recognize the marriage — the basis for the correction above — and the 22 August 1807 remarriage at the Tuileries).

## Joséphine de Beauharnais {#family-josephine}

**Standing: established by research, with the 3-million/11-million-franc death-debt figure now actively withdrawn as unsupported by the primary text this canon cites for it, rather than merely unconfirmed.** Born Marie-Josèphe-Rose Tascher de la Pagerie, 23 June 1763, Martinique, daughter of an impoverished plantation aristocrat. Married Alexandre, Vicomte de Beauharnais, at the parish church of Noisy-le-Grand on 13 December 1779 (guillotined 1794, during which she herself was imprisoned and narrowly escaped execution); two children by that marriage, Eugène (b. 1781) and Hortense (b. 1783), given their own entry below. Chronically and severely in debt across her life — Knapton's own chapters 19–21 (1809–1813) document the pattern directly and in her own words: Napoleon's 2-million-franc *sénatus-consulte* settlement (`settings/024-the-divorce.md#divorce-settlement`) plus a further 1 million granted for her "continuing debts," and a 1811 letter in which Napoleon scolds her for running into fresh debt on a 3-million-franc income — but this canon directly opened Knapton's chapter 22 in this research pass, which covers her actual 1814 death and its immediate aftermath, and found no debt or estate figure there at all, and no chapter contains "3 million" against "11 million" anywhere in the reachable text; the widely repeated 3-million/11-million death-estate figure is accordingly withdrawn as a claim this canon's own cited source does not support, not merely retained as unconfirmed, and a downstream unit should not repeat it without a different, independently checked source — Napoleon repeatedly discovered and paid off her debts across her life, only for new ones to accumulate; contemporaries nonetheless credited her with genuinely fine taste even amid the excess. During the Italian campaign she conducted an affair with the young hussar officer Hippolyte Charles: Napoleon wrote bitterly about it from Italy as early as 1796–97, but the confrontation itself was triggered when Joseph Bonaparte informed him in March 1798, and the affair continued into 1799 before ending that autumn — provoking real fury and a period of estrangement (including his own retaliatory affair with Pauline Fourès in Egypt, displayed publicly on his June 1799 return from Syria) that stopped short of divorce and was followed by reconciliation once the two were reunited. Full marriage and succession detail is under the relation below.

Sources: Ernest John Knapton, *Empress Josephine* (Harvard University Press, 1963), ch. 3 "A Marriage is Arranged" (the 13 December 1779 marriage to Alexandre de Beauharnais), ch. 12 "Joséphine Infidèle" (the Hippolyte Charles affair's timeline and Napoleon's retaliatory Fourès affair), and chs. 19–22 ("Malmaison, c'est Joséphine," "Divorce," "Seclusion," "Finale," this last covering her 1814 death directly), full text read directly at penelope.uchicago.edu, https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/People/Josephine/KNAEJO/ — the 2-million-franc *sénatus-consulte* grant, the further 1-million-franc debt allowance, and the 1811 scolding letter are each in chs. 19–20; chapter 22's own account of the death contains no debt or estate figure, which is the basis for the withdrawal above; Kate Williams, *Josephine: Desire, Ambition, Napoleon*, reviewed at Fondation Napoléon, https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/josephine-desire-ambition-napoleon/ (birth 23 June 1763, Martinique; imprisonment during the Terror); `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-marriage-josephine`.

## Eugène and Hortense de Beauharnais {#family-beauharnais-children}

**Standing: established by research.** Joséphine's children by her first marriage: Eugène de Beauharnais (b. 1781), raised within Napoleon's household and given real military and administrative responsibility as he matured, and Hortense de Beauharnais (b. 1783), married to Louis Bonaparte in the arranged 1802 match detailed at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-louis`. Both remain formally within the Bonaparte dynastic structure even after the eventual Joséphine divorce, since Napoleon's affection for and reliance on Eugène in particular outlasts the marriage that connected them.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "BEAUHARNAIS, Eugène de," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/beauharnais-eugene-de/ (his cultivation by Napoleon from 1797, when he received his brevet as auxiliary sub-lieutenant and became ADC to General Bonaparte; his own admission in his Memoirs that "my only duties were various front-line reports"; his later 1806 adoption and viceroyalty of Italy, noted here only for continuity since both postdate this canon's primary window); Fondation Napoléon, "BEAUHARNAIS, Hortense de," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/beauharnais-hortense-de/ (Hortense born 10 April 1783, a premature birth later described as "the pretext for the separation of her parents"; her 4 January 1802 marriage to Louis per `#family-louis`).

## Marie-Louise of Austria {#family-marie-louise}

**Standing: established by research.** Born 12 December 1791, Vienna, eldest daughter of Emperor Francis (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`) and his second wife Maria Theresa of Naples-Sicily. Documented as a girl as calm, industrious, and obedient, raised in a sheltered, secluded court upbringing amid intense anti-Napoleon sentiment; at Austerlitz, aged 13, she fled to Hungary with the imperial family as French forces occupied Vienna; period biography (Imbert de Saint-Amand) records her as raised to regard Napoleon as a satanic "Antichrist" figure, though the specific further line "would be the most terrible of martyrdoms" could not be traced to that or any other locatable source in this canon's research and should be treated as unverified rather than a documented remark (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`). She married Napoleon by proxy and in person in 1810 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage`), aged 18, and bore their son, the King of Rome, in March 1811. After Napoleon's first abdication she was awarded sovereignty over the duchies of Parma, Piacenza, and Guastalla by the Treaty of Fontainebleau, holding the title Duchess of Parma from 11 April 1814 until her death; she returned to Vienna with her son, ignored Napoleon's entreaties to join him on Elba, and became estranged from him after he reportedly threatened to have her abducted to join him. During the Hundred Days she remained in Austria, showing no interest in Napoleon's return. Count Adam Adalbert von Neipperg, whom she had first met at Dresden in May 1812 and whom Vienna appointed as her escort and adviser in 1814, became her lover; she bore him two children before their marriage in September 1821, four months after Napoleon's death, and the two governed Parma together, more liberally than most Italian princes of the era, until Neipperg's death in 1829. She died in Parma in 1847. This canon treats her documented estrangement from Napoleon after 1814 as the clearest counter-evidence available to any downstream reading of her 1810–14 marriage as one of settled personal devotion; whatever affection existed in that period did not survive the loss of the throne that had required it.

Sources: [habsburger.net — "Marie Louise after Napoleon's fall from power"](https://www.habsburger.net/en/chapter/marie-louise-after-napoleons-fall-power); Wikipedia, "Marie Louise, Duchess of Parma"; Imbert de Saint-Amand, *The Happy Days of the Empress Marie Louise* (period biography), full text at [fulltextarchive.com](https://www.fulltextarchive.com), directly opened, for the Hungary flight and the "Antichrist" characterization; `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`.

## Relation: Napoleon and Marie-Louise {#relation-napoleon-marie-louise}

**Standing: established by research, with the degree of genuine personal affection in the marriage's early years flagged as disputed among historians.** **His side:** the marriage is contracted explicitly for dynastic and diplomatic utility — an Austrian archduchess secures both an unimpeachable royal bloodline for his heir and a temporary alignment with the Habsburgs, per Francis's own cold calculation recorded at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i` — but Napoleon is reported to have been genuinely, even surprisingly, taken with her once married, and his letters to her during the 1812 and 1813–14 campaigns carry real personal warmth rather than only dynastic formality. **Her side:** raised from childhood to regard Napoleon as a monstrous enemy of her house (`settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-marie-louise`), she nonetheless settles into the marriage and, by most accounts, into real if untested contentment as Empress and mother — a contentment this canon treats as genuinely held rather than performed, but as resting entirely on the throne's stability, since it does not survive his fall: she declines to join him in exile, refuses his later entreaties, and her attachment shifts decisively to Neipperg once Austrian sovereignty over Parma removes any political reason to maintain the marriage. This canon does not permit a downstream unit to resolve this relation into either a simple political marriage or a simple love match; both readings are true of different years within it.

Sources: [archive.org — *The Letters of Napoleon to Marie-Louise*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.81167/2015.81167.The-Letters-Of-Napoleon-To-Marie-louise_djvu.txt) (primary translated correspondence, showing daily letters and sustained personal warmth through the 1812 and 1813–14 campaigns); `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-marie-louise`; `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`, both established by research.

## Relation: Napoleon and Joséphine {#relation-napoleon-josephine}

**Standing: established by research.** They met in 1795 through Paul Barras, then a Directory leader and Joséphine's lover; the traditional account, genuinely contested in degree but not in substance, holds that Barras was tiring of her and passed her toward his rising protégé, while she in turn gave Napoleon entrée into fashionable Directory society and Barras's patronage helped secure his Army of Italy command shortly after the romance began — a strategic value to Napoleon beyond affection. They married 9 March 1796 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-marriage-josephine`) in a ceremony with several verified irregularities: both falsified their ages, one witness was legally underage, and the officiating official lacked proper authority — defects that later proved procedurally convenient when annulment was sought. **His side:** an early, near-obsessive passion, extensively documented in his 1796 Italian-campaign letters, that had by 1805 cooled into something more politically calculating without disappearing — he forgave her debts repeatedly, delayed divorce for years after first suspecting her infertility, and grieved sincerely at her 1814 death; but the imperial throne makes the absence of an heir an acute state problem by 1805, not merely a private one, and the succession question is visibly sharpening well before the formal 1809 crisis. Contemporaries and Napoleon himself increasingly suspected her infertility rather than his own, since she had already proven fertile with Alexandre de Beauharnais; this suspicion was later confirmed when Napoleon fathered children by Éléonore Denuelle de la Plaigne (1806) and, decisively, Marie-Louise (1811). **Her side:** genuine and escalating fear of displacement as her childlessness becomes politically legible; real social precariousness despite the crown, dependent on Napoleon's continued favor; and chronic debt-driven anxiety that compounds her insecurity, driving further extravagant spending as a coping and status-signaling behavior even as it deepens the very vulnerability she fears. The formal divorce (15 December 1809, `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage`) proceeded on the stated public rationale of sacrificing personal happiness for the succession the state required; she retained the title Empress and Malmaison, and Napoleon continued paying her debts and an allowance afterward. She died 29 May 1814 of a severe respiratory/throat illness — popularly rounded to "pneumonia," though the contemporary autopsy Knapton cites describes "extreme inflammation of the whole trachea with a large gangrenous spot on the larynx" more specifically — reportedly contracted from a chill during a walk with Tsar Alexander I, who visited her at Malmaison after Napoleon's first abdication; Napoleon learned of it in exile on Elba.

Sources: Ernest John Knapton, *Empress Josephine* (Harvard University Press, 1963), ch. 9 (the 1796 marriage irregularities: falsified ages, an underage witness, an officiating official lacking proper authority) and ch. 22 (the 1814 death and autopsy), full text at [penelope.uchicago.edu](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/People/Josephine/KNAEJO/), directly opened; [Wikipedia — Joséphine de Beauharnais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9phine_de_Beauharnais); [Wikipedia — Éléonore Denuelle de La Plaigne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89l%C3%A9onore_Denuelle_de_La_Plaigne) (on the 13 December 1806 birth of Charles Léon, which established Napoleon's own fertility and so shifted the presumption of infertility onto Joséphine); `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-marriage-josephine`.

## Relation: Napoleon and his siblings, generally {#relation-napoleon-siblings}

**Standing: decided for this fiction, consistent with established biographical consensus.** **His side:** consistent dynastic instrumentalization — marriages arranged or vetoed for state utility (Louis and Hortense forced together to solve succession anxiety; Jérôme's American marriage annulled against his will; Lucien's marriage the proximate cause of exile; Caroline and Pauline married to consolidate marshal and princely alliances) — paired with an expectation of unquestioning obedience to the family's new function of legitimizing the imperial project. **Their side:** documented resentment threads through multiple siblings independently rather than as a single grievance — Lucien's principled and personal revolt into self-exile; Louis's open unhappiness in his assigned marriage; Hortense's reluctant, coerced consent; Jérôme's initial refusal to abandon Elizabeth Patterson. Individual ambition also cuts against Napoleon's control rather than merely submitting to it: Caroline in particular is independently power-hungry, using the marriage system for her own and Murat's advancement. This canon treats the family as a genuine population of distinct wills Napoleon must manage rather than as a uniform bloc of loyal or disloyal relatives, and does not permit a downstream unit to flatten any one sibling's specific grievance into a generic "family resentment."

Sources: this synthesis rests on the individual sibling entries above (`#family-elisa`, `#family-pauline`, `#family-caroline`, `#family-lucien`, `#family-louis`, `#family-jerome`), each independently established by research; the general pattern itself is this canon's characterizing reading of those attested facts, not an independently documented claim.
