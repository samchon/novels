# Scholarship and Archival Compilations

This file gives its own addressable unit to every modern historian, scholarly article, order-of-battle compilation, and archival research project this canon relies on beyond the sources `014-sources-and-historiography.md` already covers. It also carries out the first of two structural repairs this canon's evidence base required: `014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship` lumped six named historians — Chandler, Roberts, Esdaile, Broers, Lieven, and Mikaberidze — into a single aggregate entry, so that a unit citing it cited a shelf rather than a specific historian's own standing. That aggregate entry is retired here in favor of one unit per historian this canon actually relies on elsewhere in the corpus. Michael Broers is not among them: an audit of every settings file found no citation of his three-volume biography anywhere outside the retired aggregate paragraph itself, so per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions` and the rule that a work nothing in the canon actually uses does not get an entry, Broers is not carried forward. `014-sources-and-historiography.md` itself is otherwise unchanged, its other nine units and their anchors intact; every citation elsewhere in this corpus that pointed at `#source-modern-scholarship` now points at the specific historian's unit below. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions`, a source's standing here binds every other file's use of it.

## David Chandler {#source-chandler}

**Standing: established by research, and never itself opened by this canon.** David G. Chandler, a Sandhurst instructor from 1980–94, wrote *The Campaigns of Napoleon* (1966), the classic single-volume operational-military narrative of Napoleon's campaigns; a Telegraph obituary called it "unlikely to be improved upon, despite a legion of rivals." This canon states plainly, rather than only implying through silence, that it has never opened the book: the archive.org copy is access-restricted controlled digital lending, and every citation to Chandler across this corpus — for Austerlitz strength figures, the Smolensk and Berezina casualty ranges, the Elster bridge disaster at Leipzig — reaches this canon only as a later source (typically Wikipedia) reports a figure attributed to him, never from this canon's own reading of a page. A downstream unit should treat "per Chandler" claims throughout this corpus as carrying that specific, uniform limitation, not as independently verified against his text.

Sources: Wikipedia, "David G. Chandler," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_G._Chandler; *The Campaigns of Napoleon* (1966), archive.org, access-restricted controlled digital lending, not opened by this canon.

## Andrew Roberts {#source-roberts}

**Standing: established by research.** Andrew Roberts's *Napoleon: A Life* (published in the UK as *Napoleon the Great*, 2014) was the first major biography to draw on the newly completed Fondation Napoléon *Correspondance générale*, then standing at some 33,000 published letters — smaller than the roughly 40,500 the completed project eventually published (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance`). It is explicitly and self-consciously sympathetic and rehabilitative, foregrounding Napoleon's energy, charisma, and administrative and legal achievement; reviewing Charles Esdaile's *Napoleon's Wars* for the *Literary Review*, Roberts himself wrote that "another subtitle might have been: 'Napoleon – The Case for the Prosecution,'" a useful marker of how far his own interpretive stance sits from Esdaile's (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-esdaile`). This canon treats specific Roberts quotations and page-cited claims as established, but treats claims this canon knows only as a secondary source's attribution to Roberts — several of which recur across this corpus — with the same caution any unopened-page citation carries. Roberts's separate campaign study, *Waterloo: Napoleon's Last Gamble* (2005), is a distinct title this canon has likewise not opened directly, known only through Wikipedia's citation of it for the Hougoumont-attack quotation this canon verified independently against Wellington's own despatch (`settings/028-primary-documents-and-official-texts.md#source-wellington-waterloo-despatch`).

Sources: napoleon.org, "Two Reviews of 'Napoleon the Great' by Andrew Roberts," https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/press-review/two-reviews-of-napoleon-the-great-by-andrew-roberts/; Andrew Roberts, "The Emperor of Spin," *Literary Review*, October 2007 (issue 348), https://literaryreview.co.uk/the-emperor-of-spin; *Napoleon: A Life* (2014) and *Waterloo: Napoleon's Last Gamble* (2005), neither opened directly by this canon beyond quotations carried in secondary sources.

## Charles Esdaile {#source-esdaile}

**Standing: established by research.** Charles Esdaile is this canon's leading critical and revisionist counterweight to Roberts, foregrounding the pan-European human cost of the Napoleonic wars over operational brilliance and explicitly skeptical of a "Napoleon-as-genius" framing. His *Napoleon's Wars: An International History, 1803–1815* (Allen Lane, 2007) is the general work this canon cites for that critical stance, and *The Peninsular War* (2001) is a distinct, dedicated monograph this canon cites specifically for Peninsular casualty figures — French deaths approaching a quarter of a million, total French casualties around 500,000 across the war — and for his revisionist complication of the romantic "guerrilla" narrative, noting many irregular bands were as much banditry as patriotism.

Sources: Andrew Roberts, "The Emperor of Spin," *Literary Review*, October 2007, reviewing Charles Esdaile, *Napoleon's Wars: An International History, 1803–1815* (Allen Lane, 2007); Charles Esdaile, *The Peninsular War* (2001), per `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-peninsular-war`.

## Dominic Lieven {#source-lieven}

**Standing: established by research.** Dominic Lieven's *Russia Against Napoleon* (Allen Lane, 2009) is this canon's key Russian-archival corrective to Franco-centric historiography, written explicitly to counter Western narratives of 1812–1814 using previously unused Russian archives, and winner of both the Wolfson Prize for History and the Prix Napoléon. This canon has not been able to open the book directly: the archive.org copy returned HTTP 401 (controlled digital lending) to every access attempt in this research pass, so this canon's use of Lieven rests on a scholarly review of the book rather than the text itself.

Sources: Dominic Lieven, *Russia Against Napoleon* (Allen Lane, 2009), archive.org, controlled digital lending, not opened by this canon; David A. Bell, review of Lieven, *H-France Review*, vol. 13, no. 183 (2013), https://www.h-france.net/vol13reviews/vol13no183bell.pdf, this canon's actual access to Lieven's argument.

## Alexander Mikaberidze {#source-mikaberidze}

**Standing: established by research, with access levels varying sharply between his several distinct works.** Alexander Mikaberidze, a historian at LSU Shreveport and Fellow of the Royal Historical Society, draws on Russian, Georgian, and other non-Western-European archival and linguistic sources, functioning as a global and Eastern-Front corrective to older Franco-centric accounts, analogous to Lieven's. He is this canon's single most heavily and most *directly* used scholar among the six historians the retired aggregate entry named: *The Burning of Moscow: Napoleon's Trial by Fire, 1812* (Pen &amp; Sword, 2014) is read in full and quoted directly throughout `settings/016-borodino.md` and parts of `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md`, and his two Napoleon Series order-of-battle compilations — "French Order of Battle at Borodino" and "Russian Order of Battle at Borodino" — are likewise read in full and used for corps composition and commanders. This canon has found no citation anywhere in this corpus to a Mikaberidze order-of-battle compilation specifically for the Berezina crossing; the Berezina casualty figures this canon carries are instead attributed to Chandler and Bodart, not to Mikaberidze. Three further Mikaberidze works are named but not opened directly: *The Napoleonic Wars: A Global History* (2020), known to this canon only through Wikipedia's citation of its p. 539 casualty figures; *Kutuzov: A Life in War and Peace* (Oxford University Press, 2022), known only through Gregory Afinogenov's review of it in the *New York Review of Books* (19 October 2023); and "Napoleon's Lost Legions: The Grande Armée Prisoners of War in Russia," *Napoleonica. La Revue*, 21 (2014), located via its DOI record but not read in full text.

Sources: Alexander Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow: Napoleon's Trial by Fire, 1812* (Pen &amp; Sword, 2014), archive.org id `nap-the-burning-of-moscow-napoleons-trial-by-fire-1812`, read directly; Alexander Mikaberidze, "French Order of Battle at Borodino" and "Russian Order of Battle at Borodino," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles/1812/Russia/Borodino/Mikaberidze/OrdersofBattle/, read directly; Alexander Mikaberidze, *The Napoleonic Wars: A Global History* (2020), p. 539, cited via Wikipedia, not opened directly; Gregory Afinogenov, "Field Maneuvers," *New York Review of Books*, 19 October 2023, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2023/10/19/field-maneuvers-mikhail-kutuzov/, reviewing Alexander Mikaberidze, *Kutuzov: A Life in War and Peace* (Oxford University Press, 2022), the latter not opened directly; Alexander Mikaberidze, "Napoleon's Lost Legions: The Grande Armée Prisoners of War in Russia," *Napoleonica. La Revue*, 21 (2014), pp. 35–44, https://doi.org/10.3917/napo.153.0035, located but not read in full text. His two Austerlitz-era Russian-archival translations are their own unit at `settings/028-primary-documents-and-official-texts.md#source-russian-archival-returns`.

## Eli Heckscher, *The Continental System* {#source-heckscher}

**Standing: established by research.** Eli F. Heckscher's *The Continental System: An Economic Interpretation* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1922) is the standard earlier monograph on the Continental Blockade's economics, and the single most heavily relied-upon source in `settings/021-the-continental-system.md`, read directly and in full at archive.org rather than through a secondary paraphrase. This canon adopts Heckscher's own framing of the System's trajectory as "the self-destruction of the Continental System," and follows his independent attribution of much of the post-1810 economic crisis to British over-shipment into newly opened South American markets and to American non-intercourse laws, not to the blockade alone. One specific limitation is flagged rather than left implicit: this canon's knowledge of the First Milan Decree's content comes only through Heckscher's summary of it, not an opened primary text (`settings/028-primary-documents-and-official-texts.md#source-continental-system-decrees`).

Sources: Eli F. Heckscher, *The Continental System: An Economic Interpretation* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1922), full OCR text read directly at archive.org, https://archive.org/download/continentalsyste00heck/continentalsyste00heck_djvu.txt.

## Roger Dufraisse {#source-dufraisse}

**Standing: established by research.** Roger Dufraisse's "Politique douanière française, blocus et système continental en Allemagne," a napoleon.org article read in full via the fetch helper, is this canon's source for the Continental System's German enforcement texture — Tönning and Frankfurt as smuggling hubs, the Rothschild and Cologne merchant fortunes built on evasion, and Bourrienne's own 6,000-smuggler estimate for Hamburg. Dufraisse's own phrase, that enforcement in practice was largely "lettre morte," is this canon's preferred characterization of the gap between the System's legal text and its actual operation. His article in turn cites Marcel Dunan and Jean Clinquart, and at second hand François Crouzet and Silvia Marzagalli's *Les boulevards de la fraude* (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-crouzet-marzagalli`); none of these three is independently opened by this canon.

Sources: Roger Dufraisse, "Politique douanière française, blocus et système continental en Allemagne," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper.

## Crouzet and Marzagalli, *Les boulevards de la fraude* {#source-crouzet-marzagalli}

**Standing: established by research, not independently opened by this canon.** François Crouzet and Silvia Marzagalli's *Les boulevards de la fraude* (Septentrion, 1999) is a modern monograph on Continental System smuggling this canon has not opened directly; it is named consistently as the standing reference for smuggling-cost and insurance-premium estimates across three different citing articles (Dufraisse, Branda) without ever itself being read, and a downstream unit should not treat a Crouzet-and-Marzagalli figure as independently verified by this canon.

Sources: François Crouzet and Silvia Marzagalli, *Les boulevards de la fraude* (Septentrion, Lille, 1999), p. 262, cited via Roger Dufraisse and Pierre Branda's articles, not independently opened by this canon.

## Crouzet, *L'économie britannique et le blocus continental* {#source-crouzet-1958}

**Standing: established by research, with the book's own conclusions not independently verified.** François Crouzet's *L'économie britannique et le blocus continental* (1958) is the standard later monograph on the Continental System's effect on the British economy, framing its inquiry as whether the blockade could compel England to yield. This canon could not independently verify Crouzet's own conclusions, having reached only a single page of a contemporary review of the book rather than the book itself, and a downstream unit should not attribute a specific verdict on that question to Crouzet without a fuller reading than this canon has performed.

Sources: Claude Fohlen, review of François Crouzet, *L'économie britannique et le blocus continental*, *Revue économique* 10-2 (1959), p. 298 only, via Persée, https://www.persee.fr/doc/reco_0035-2764_1959_num_10_2_407355 — the sole page this canon could retrieve; the book itself not independently opened.

## Pierre Branda {#source-branda}

**Standing: established by research.** Pierre Branda's two articles for the *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien*, both read in full via the fetch helper, are this canon's source for the Continental System's domestic economic consequences: "Les conséquences économiques du Blocus continental" (no. 472, sept.–oct. 2007) for the licensing figures, the Oberkampf and Richard-Lenoir textile-industry detail, and the German-export and cotton-shortage figures; "1812, l'année de tous les défis" (no. 492, July–Sept. 2012) for the 1810–1811 credit crisis, including Branda's own direct verdicts — Paris bankers losing "plus d'un million et demi de francs" in the Simons bank affair, an estimated 60,000–70,000 deaths across twelve months of bad harvests, and his explicitly cautionary judgment that "la mesure du maximum avait retardé le retour à la normale" (the price-control measure delayed the return to normal). Branda's articles in turn quote the economic historians Louis Bergeron and Silvia Marzagalli on the crisis's limited structural damage to French capitalism; neither is independently opened by this canon beyond Branda's quotation of them.

Sources: Pierre Branda, "Les conséquences économiques du Blocus continental," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 472 (sept.-oct. 2007), pp. 21–30; Pierre Branda, "1812, l'année de tous les défis," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 492 (July-Sept. 2012), pp. 26–33; both reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper.

## Othenin d'Haussonville {#source-dhaussonville}

**Standing: established by research.** Comte Othenin d'Haussonville's "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat (1800-1814)," published in the *Revue des Deux Mondes* in 1865, is the single dominant secondary source of `settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md`, read in full at fr.wikisource.org and cited in every H2 section of that file. It reconstructs the annexation of the Papal States, the excommunication, the Pope's arrest and captivity at Savona and Fontainebleau, and the 1811 national council, quoting extensively from the *Correspondance de Napoléon Ier* (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance`), Cardinal Pacca's memoirs, and General Radet's own accounts (`settings/029-eyewitness-memoirs.md#source-arrest-testimonies`). This canon flags a specific, recurring limitation: every decree and correspondence quotation this canon draws from d'Haussonville is read only through his own reproduction of it, not independently verified against a second edition of the Correspondance or a Bulletin des lois text. This canon also corrects its own earlier error here: the "monument de papier" critique of the Second Empire Correspondance edition (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-correspondance`) is not d'Haussonville's — that is Jacques-Olivier Boudon's, published by the École nationale des chartes — and this canon previously conflated the two.

Sources: Othenin d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat (1800-1814)," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, 2e période, tome 56 (1865), chs. 10–24, read in full at fr.wikisource.org.

## Peter Hicks {#source-hicks}

## Thierry Lentz {#source-lentz}

**Standing: established by research.** Thierry Lentz, director of the Fondation Napoléon, is cited across this corpus for five distinct articles, each read in full via the fetch helper: "28 mai 1807: Le maréchal Lefebvre devient duc de Dantzig," this canon's source for the imperial nobility's staged 1804–1808 legal genesis and the Lefebvre creation as its deliberate "Rubicon" moment; "Bullet Point #32: Did Napoleon really intend to invade Britain?"; "Bullet Point #35 – How did Napoleon manage to lose the Battle of Waterloo?" Co-authored with Pierre Branda, *Napoléon, l'esclavage et les colonies* (Fayard, 2006) is this canon's source for rejecting a "genocide" framing of the 1802 Guadeloupe reconquest.

Sources: Thierry Lentz, "28 mai 1807: Le maréchal Lefebvre devient duc de Dantzig"; "Bullet Point #32: Did Napoleon really intend to invade Britain?"; "Bullet Point #35 – How did Napoleon manage to lose the Battle of Waterloo?" ## Paul Ganière {#source-ganiere}

**Standing: established by research.** Paul Ganière is cited across four distinct outputs, all reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper. "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 300 (1978), pp. 7–16, is by far the most heavily used, functioning as the narrative spine of `settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md`: the journey itinerary, the Braunau handover, the Courcelles-sur-Vesle meeting and its dialogue, Marie-Louise's letter to her father, and — per this canon's own citation, though see the caution recorded at `settings/029-eyewitness-memoirs.md#source-constant` — the Compiègne wedding-night exchange with Cardinal Fesch. This canon characterizes the article's own genre precisely: published in the Fondation Napoléon's own specialist journal and drawing on court memoirs and Marie-Louise's own correspondence, it is a scholarly secondary synthesis whose interpretive narration is Ganière's own voice, while the directly quoted letters and dialogue within it are primary material he reproduces. Ganière's *Dictionnaire Napoléon* entry (ed. Tulard, 1999, p. 1140) is a separate, distinct output, cited for Marie-Louise's reply to Metternich. His "épouser un ventre" characterization of the marriage's motive is disputed directly by Thierry Lentz (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-lentz`). A fourth, unrelated Ganière piece, "DUBOIS, Antoine, (1756-1837), médecin," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 362 (1988), pp. 51–52, supplies a third variant wording of Napoleon's "sauvez la mère" instruction at the King of Rome's birth.

Sources: Paul Ganière, "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 300 (1978), pp. 7–16; Ganière's *Dictionnaire Napoléon* entry (ed. Tulard, 1999), p. 1140; Dr. Paul Ganière, "DUBOIS, Antoine, (1756-1837), médecin," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 362 (1988), pp. 51–52; all reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper.

## Bernard Cornwell's Waterloo history and his separate Waterloo novel {#source-cornwell}

**Standing: established by research, the two works kept explicitly distinct.** Bernard Cornwell wrote two entirely different works about Waterloo that this canon must not conflate. *Waterloo: The History of Four Days, Three Armies and Three Battles* (2014) is a work of narrative nonfiction history, cited via Wikipedia's synthesis for its characterization of Marshal Soult's chief-of-staff order to Grouchy as "almost impenetrable nonsense" — not independently opened by this canon, and explicitly distinguished from an archival monograph by the citing source itself. His separate historical novel, *Waterloo*, is fiction, and this canon has found a specific claim about a deliberate Gneisenau-authored delay in the Prussian march to the battlefield traceable only to that novel rather than to any primary or secondary historical account it could verify; a downstream unit must not present that dramatized reluctance as established fact.

Sources: Bernard Cornwell, *Waterloo: The History of Four Days, Three Armies and Three Battles* (2014), cited via Wikipedia, "Jean-de-Dieu Soult," not opened directly; Bernard Cornwell, *Waterloo* (novel), identified as the source of the Gneisenau-delay dramatization via Wikipedia, "August von Gneisenau," not adopted as historical fact.

## Colville Chesney, *Waterloo Lectures* {#source-chesney}

**Standing: established by research, not opened directly by this canon.** Colville Chesney's 1868 military-historical monograph is cited, via Wikipedia's synthesis, for the commonly given 128,000-strong figure for the Armée du Nord (p. 51) and for a judgment that "neither Napoleon nor Marshal Grouchy understood" the situation before Wavre (p. 157); this canon has not opened it directly.

Sources: Colville Chesney, *Waterloo Lectures* (1868), pp. 51 and 157, cited via Wikipedia, "Waterloo campaign," not opened directly.

## David Gates, *The Napoleonic Wars 1803–1815* {#source-gates}

**Standing: established by research, not opened directly by this canon.** David Gates's 2003 monograph, p. 259, is cited twice via Wikipedia's synthesis — for an exchange between Ney and other officers on 4 April 1814, and for an account of the "the army will obey its chiefs" episode at Ney's 1815 return — in neither case independently opened by this canon.

Sources: David Gates, *The Napoleonic Wars 1803–1815* (2003), p. 259, cited via Wikipedia, "Michel Ney," not opened directly.

## The Waterloo order-of-battle historians: Pigeard, Siborne, Sokolov, Haythornthwaite, Hofschröer, and Barbero {#source-waterloo-oob-historians}

**Standing: established by research, none of the six independently opened by this canon.** A cluster of named historians whose unit-level Waterloo strength tables and interpretive judgments this canon draws on only through Wikipedia's own compiled synthesis, never independently verified against any of their texts directly: André Pigeard (2005), Herbert Siborne (1895) — the nineteenth-century compiler of the "Waterloo Letters," an archival-adjacent collection of veterans' first-person accounts rather than a narrative history in its own right — Oleg Sokolov (2003), Philip Haythornthwaite, Peter Hofschröer (1999, pp. 68–69), and Alessandro Barbero, whose *The Battle: A New History of Waterloo* (2005) is quoted, again only via Wikipedia's synthesis, for the judgment that "in this heterogeneous army the difference between British and foreign troops did not prove significant under fire." This canon separately notes that a "Denis Sokolov (2020)" is cited for 1812 casualty figures at `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-niemen-crossing` and `#russia-destruction`; this canon has not established whether that is the same person as Oleg Sokolov here, and treats the two citations as distinct pending clarification.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Order of battle of the Waterloo Campaign," and "Battle of Waterloo," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo, citing André Pigeard (2005), Herbert Siborne (1895), Oleg Sokolov (2003), Philip Haythornthwaite, Peter Hofschröer (1999), pp. 68–69, and Alessandro Barbero, *The Battle: A New History of Waterloo* (2005), none opened directly by this canon.

## Becke and the 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* on d'Erlon at Quatre Bras {#source-erlon-verdict-sources}

**Standing: established by research, neither source independently opened by this canon.** Two distinct sources are cited together, via Wikipedia's synthesis, for the historiographical verdict that Comte d'Erlon bears no blame for his I Corps's failure to engage at either Quatre Bras or Ligny on 16 June 1815 — that "if the I Corps had engaged in either battle the outcome of the campaign might have been different": the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed. (1911), and A. F. Becke (1911), p. 377. This canon located but did not independently reach the 1911 *Britannica*'s own d'Erlon entry via Wikisource in this research pass, and has not opened Becke directly; both verdicts are repeated only as Wikipedia reports them.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Jean-Baptiste Drouet, Comte d'Erlon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Drouet,_Comte_d%27Erlon, attributing the verdicts to the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed. (1911), and A. F. Becke (1911), p. 377, neither independently opened by this canon.
