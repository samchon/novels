# Primary Documents and Official Texts

This file gives its own addressable unit to every treaty, decree, constitutional act, official despatch, archival return, and comparable period document this canon cites for its own wording rather than only for the fact it records — so that a downstream unit can cite the document itself and inherit its standing, instead of restating what kind of text it is and how this canon obtained it. `014-sources-and-historiography.md` remains the template these units follow; its nine surviving source units (after the retirement recorded at `030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-chandler` and neighboring units) are unchanged, and this file adds to the same evidence layer rather than replacing any part of it. Per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#standing-conventions`, a source's standing here binds every other file's use of it.

## The Treaty of Campo Formio, 17 October 1797 {#source-treaty-campo-formio}

**Standing: established by research.** The treaty ending the War of the First Coalition between France and Austria, signed at Campo Formio (now Campoformido, near Udine) on 26 Vendémiaire Year VI. This canon cites the public and secret articles directly by number rather than paraphrasing the settlement, and records a discrepancy internal to its own locator: the English-language transcription this canon reads gives the header date "October 27, 1797," which contradicts the Republican date given in the same document's own body and every other source this canon has checked; this canon follows the Republican date (17 October, not 27) and does not repeat the header's contradicting figure.

Sources: De Clercq, *Recueil des traités de la France*, vol. I, pp. 335–343, public and secret articles transcribed at The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/diplomatic/c_campoformio1.html, read directly and cited by article.

## The Treaty of Saint Petersburg, 11 April 1805 {#source-treaty-third-coalition-alliance}

**Standing: established by research.** The Anglo-Russian treaty of alliance that formed the diplomatic core of the Third Coalition against France, committing Britain to subsidize Russian and allied forces in exchange for a coordinated campaign. This canon cites the treaty text itself for its dated formation of the coalition, not a paraphrase of it.

Sources: "Treaty of Alliance between Great Britain and Russia 1805," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/diplomatic/c_britishrussian.html, the treaty text, dated 11 April 1805, read directly.

## The Treaty of Pressburg, 26 December 1805 {#source-treaty-pressburg}

**Standing: established by research.** The peace treaty ending the War of the Third Coalition between France and Austria after Austerlitz, signed at Pressburg (Bratislava). This canon cites its territorial and financial articles directly by number — the Venetian and Tyrolean cessions, the recognition of the German mediatized states, and the forty-million-franc indemnity — rather than summarizing the settlement from a secondary account.

Sources: "Treaty of Pressburg 1805," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/diplomatic/c_pressburg.html, the treaty text, read directly and cited by article: Art. XI, Art. XX, Art. XXIII, Arts. XXIX and XXXII, and the separate article providing forty million francs "metallic value."

## The Treaty of Schönbrunn (Vienna), 14 October 1809 {#source-treaty-schonbrunn}

**Standing: established by research.** The peace treaty ending the War of the Fifth Coalition between France and Austria, signed at Schönbrunn and titled *Treaty of Vienna* by some hosting editions. This canon cites its territorial articles directly by section rather than by paraphrase.

Sources: Wikisource, *Treaty of Schönbrunn* (titled there *Treaty of Vienna, 1809*), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Sch%C3%B6nbrunn, the treaty text, read directly and cited by article: Art. III §1, §2, §4, §5.

## The Treaty of Fontainebleau, 11 April 1814 {#source-treaty-fontainebleau-1814}

**Standing: established by research, with one clause read only at search-snippet level.** The treaty fixing the terms of Napoleon's first abdication: his sovereignty over Elba, the pensions granted to him and his family, and the disposition of Marie-Louise and the King of Rome. This canon cites it by article for the signatories and the specific terms of Arts. III, V, VI, XVI, and XVII, but flags one clause specifically: the second paragraph of Art. III was recovered only at search-snippet level, since three separate fetches of the treaty text truncated the article at its first sentence, so that clause is read indirectly rather than from a clean article-level text and should not be treated with the same confidence as the rest of the document.

Sources: Wikisource, *Treaty of Fontainebleau (1814)*, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Fontainebleau_(1814); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Treaty of Fontainebleau, 11 April 1814 — official text," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/treaty-of-fontainebleau.php — the treaty text, cited by article, with the Art. III §2 access limitation noted above.

## Napoleon's first abdication act, 4 April 1814 {#source-first-abdication-act}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact date corrected against a hosting page's own mislabeling.** The conditional first-abdication declaration Napoleon signed at Fontainebleau, offering to descend from the throne in favor of his son under a regency rather than an unconditional surrender — distinct from the unconditional second abdication that followed six days later once the Allies refused the conditional terms. This canon corrects a dating error in one of its own hosting sources: the Napoleon Series' own reproduction of the text mislabels the act "April 2," a date this canon does not adopt, following instead the 4 April date independently confirmed by Charles-Éloi Vial's dedicated scholarly treatment of the three distinct April 1814 abdication acts.

Sources: The Napoleon Series, "Napoleon's Addresses," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/napoleon/speeches/c_speeches13.html, for the first-abdication text, read directly and quoted for "the Emperor Napoleon, faithful to his oath, declares that he is ready to descend from the throne..."; cross-verified against Charles-Éloi Vial, "4, 6 et 11 avril 1814: les trois actes d'abdication de Napoléon Ier," *Napoleonica. La Revue*, 19 (2014), for the correct 4 April date, which the Napoleon Series page itself mislabels "April 2."

## The Constitution of the Year VIII, 22 frimaire an VIII (13 December 1799) {#source-constitution-year-viii}

**Standing: established by research.** The constitutional text establishing the Consulate: the Sénat conservateur, the Tribunat, the Corps législatif, the Conseil d'État, and the three Consuls, with Napoleon as First Consul holding the preponderance of executive initiative. This canon cites its titles directly for the offices it created rather than summarizing the settlement from a secondary account.

Sources: *Constitution de la République française du 22 frimaire an VIII* (13 December 1799), official French text at Légifrance, https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000571354, Titles II–IV, read directly; English translation, The Napoleon Series, "Constitution of the Year VIII," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/legislation/c_constitution8.html.

## The Code civil des Français, 1804 {#source-code-civil}

**Standing: established by research.** The Napoleonic Code's original 1804 text, governing family, property, and contract law across the Empire and, through later reception, well beyond it. This canon quotes specific articles directly — Art. 213 on spousal obedience, Art. 214 on the wife's legal domicile, and Art. 1124 on a married woman's contractual incapacity — rather than characterizing the Code's treatment of women in general terms, per `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`.

Sources: *Code civil des Français* (1804), original text, Wikisource, https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Code_civil_des_Fran%C3%A7ais_1804/Texte_entier, read directly and cited by article.

## The Loi du 20 mai 1802 (30 floréal an X) {#source-slavery-decree-1802}

**Standing: established by research.** The law reinstating slavery and the slave trade in the French colonies where the Revolution had abolished them, reversing the 1794 general emancipation decree for Guadeloupe, Martinique, and the other colonies still under French control. This canon quotes its Articles 3 and 4 directly rather than describing the reversal only in general terms, and records the Tribunat and Corps législatif vote counts alongside the text.

Sources: *Loi du 20 mai 1802* (30 floréal an X), text and vote record, French Wikipedia, "Loi sur la traite des noirs et le régime des colonies du 20 mai 1802," https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loi_sur_la_traite_des_noirs_et_le_r%C3%A9gime_des_colonies_du_20_mai_1802, quoting Articles 3 and 4 verbatim.

## The Loi du 7 germinal an XI (28 mars 1803) {#source-franc-germinal-law}

**Standing: established by research.** The monetary law establishing the franc germinal — five grams of silver at nine-tenths fine as the unit of account — the currency standard this canon uses for every Napoleonic-era financial fact from 1803 onward, per `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#franc-germinal`. This canon quotes the law's operative clause directly rather than paraphrasing the standard it fixed.

Sources: *Loi du 7 germinal an XI* (28 mars 1803), quoted directly at Fondation Napoléon/napoleon.org, "La création du 'franc germinal'," read via the fetch helper — "Cinq grammes d'argent au titre de 9/10e de fin, constituent l'unité monétaire qui consacre le nom de franc."

## The Continental System decree texts: the Berlin and Milan Decrees {#source-continental-system-decrees}

**Standing: established by research, with the First Milan Decree's content known only at second hand.** Three decrees form the legal architecture of the Continental Blockade: the Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806, declaring the British Isles in a state of blockade and barring British goods and mail from Continental ports; the First Milan Decree of 23 November 1807, tightening enforcement against neutral shipping that had touched a British port; and the Second Milan Decree of 17 December 1807, declaring any vessel that had submitted to British search or paid a British duty denationalized and subject to seizure. This canon's access to the three texts is uneven and states that plainly: the Berlin Decree and the Second Milan Decree were both read in full French text via the browser-agent fetch helper, but the First Milan Decree's content is known to this canon only through Eli Heckscher's 1922 monograph (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-heckscher`) rather than from an opened primary text, and is not independently verified against a Bulletin des lois reproduction. English Wikisource's "Berlin Decree" page was checked and rejected as a citable source for exact wording: it is a paraphrase rather than a verbatim translation.

Sources: the Berlin Decree, full French text, *Bulletin des lois de l'Empire français*, 1806, Tome 5, 4e série, pp. 569–572, reproduced at napoleon.org and read directly via the browser-agent fetch helper, https://www.napoleon.org/FR/salle_lecture/articles/files/decretBlocus_nov1806.asp; the Second Milan Decree, full French text, reproduced at napoleon.org and read directly the same way, https://www.napoleon.org/FR/salle_lecture/articles/files/document_blocus_2emedecretmilan_17decembre1807.asp; the First Milan Decree's content per Eli F. Heckscher, *The Continental System: An Economic Interpretation* (1922), as above, not independently verified against a primary text.

## The sénatus-consulte of 16 December 1809 {#source-divorce-senatus-consulte}

**Standing: established by research.** The decree effecting the civil dissolution of Napoleon's marriage to Joséphine, passed by the Senate the day after the couple's own formal declarations of consent before the assembled imperial family. This canon quotes its four operative articles directly.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Document: Décret du 16 décembre 1809," full text read directly via the fetch helper, for the *sénatus-consulte*'s four articles.

## Wellington's despatch to Earl Bathurst, Waterloo, 19 June 1815 {#source-wellington-waterloo-despatch}

**Standing: established by research.** Wellington's own official after-action report on Waterloo, written the day after the battle and published in *The London Gazette*, no. 17028, 22 June 1815 — the primary document underlying nearly every later account of the battle's chronology from the Allied side. This canon reads it directly and cites it for its own wording on the Hougoumont attack's timing, the army's own conduct, and Bülow's and Blücher's arrival, rather than only for the facts a secondary account draws from it. A separate Wellington document — a private letter privately describing his own army as "an infamous army, very weak and ill-equipped, and a very inexperienced Staff" — is a distinct primary document from the official despatch and is known to this canon only through secondary discussion, not from an archival original; the two should not be conflated as the same text.

Sources: Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington, despatch to Earl Bathurst, Waterloo, 19 June 1815, published in *The London Gazette*, no. 17028, 22 June 1815, and reproduced in full at Wikisource, "A Sketch of the Battle of Waterloo/Despatch from field-marshal the duke of Wellington to earl Bathurst," https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Sketch_of_the_Battle_of_Waterloo/Despatch_from_field-marshal_the_duke_of_Wellington_to_earl_Bathurst, read directly. The "infamous army" letter is cited only as Wikipedia's own synthesis reports it, via "Battle of Waterloo," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo, citing Alessandro Barbero, *The Battle: A New History of Waterloo* (2005), not independently traced to an archival original by this canon.

## Russian archival military returns: RGVIA and the Lithuanian State Historical Archive {#source-russian-archival-returns}

**Standing: established by research.** Two distinct Russian-archival primary documents, each translated into English for The Napoleon Series by the historian Alexander Mikaberidze and read directly by this canon rather than taken from a secondary paraphrase. The first is the Russian army's own return of killed and missing at Austerlitz, filed 25 December 1805 / 6 January 1806, held at the Russian State Military Historical Archive (RGVIA), file 26, and printed in *M. I. Kutuzov: Sbornik dokumentov*, ed. L. G. Beskrovny (Moscow, 1951); it gives 19,371 killed and missing, 131 guns lost, and 4,579 men later returned to the army, and is the arithmetic this canon uses rather than a rounder, unsourced Austerlitz casualty figure. The second is Prince Bagration's own after-action report to Kutuzov following the 1805 campaign's rearguard actions, held at the Central State Archive of Lithuania (fond 378, opis 13, delo 213) and printed in the same *Sbornik dokumentov* series (Moscow, 1954, vol. II); it confirms his holding action at Proedlitz and an orderly staged withdrawal, in his own words rather than a later reconstruction.

Sources: Alexander Mikaberidze (trans.), "Report on Killed and Missing in Action at the Battle of Austerlitz," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles-campaigns/report-on-killed-and-missing-in-action-at-the-battle-of-austerlitz/, read directly; Alexander Mikaberidze (trans.), Bagration's after-action report to Kutuzov, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/russianarchives/austerlitz/c_bagration.html, read directly, from the Central State Archive of Lithuania, printed in *M. I. Kutuzov: sbornik dokumentov* (Moscow, 1954), vol. II.

## Archives nationales, AF IV 1310 {#source-af-iv-1310}

**Standing: established by research, with access only through a secondary quotation.** The French national-archives fund holding Napoleon's 14 June 1810 instruction on the imperial nobility's structure and Cambacérès's 30 June 1810 report responding to it, including the specific line "je ne veux d'autres ducs" attributed to Napoleon within it. This canon has not independently opened the archival file itself: both documents reach this canon only through the historian Natalie Petiteau's direct quotation of them (`settings/030-scholarship-and-archival-compilations.md#source-petiteau`), and a downstream unit should not treat the wording as independently archive-verified by this canon.

Sources: Napoleon's 14 June 1810 instruction and Cambacérès's 30 June 1810 report, Archives nationales, AF IV 1310, quoted directly by Natalie Petiteau, "Noblesse d'Empire et élites au XIXe siècle: une fusion réussie," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 411 (Feb. 1997), reproduced at napoleon.org and read in full via the fetch helper — the actual source of both quotations, this canon not having independently opened the archival file.

## La Police secrète du Premier Empire, vol. III {#source-police-secrete}

**Standing: established by research.** The published edition of Napoleon's internal police-surveillance bulletins for 1806–07, a primary documentary record of the regime's own domestic intelligence-gathering rather than a later historian's narrative. This canon cites bulletin no. 1174 directly for Fouché's 19 November 1807 report on Joséphine's household.

Sources: *La Police secrète du Premier Empire*, vol. III, 1806–07, bulletin no. 1174, pp. 436–37, quoted at napoleon.org, "1804-1810: les différentes étapes conduisant à la dissolution du mariage de Napoléon Ier et de l'impératrice Joséphine," read in full via the fetch helper.

## The bull *Quum memoranda*, 10 June 1809 {#source-quum-memoranda}

**Standing: established by research, with the bull's own Latin text known to this canon only through two secondary scholarly quotations.** Pope Pius VII's bull of excommunication against those responsible for the annexation of the Papal States and the seizure of Rome, prepared in two alternate advance-drafted versions and posted on the walls of St. Peter's, Santa Maria Maggiore, and St. John Lateran once ordinary publication channels were blocked, per `settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md#church-excommunication`. This canon has not opened a primary edition of the bull's full Latin text: its incipit ("Pius. P. P. VII, ad perpetuam memoriam... Quum memoranda"), its deliberately unspecific "auteurs et fauteurs" formula, and its omission of Napoleon's own name are each known to this canon only through two secondary scholarly treatments that quote the document rather than through an opened primary edition, and a downstream unit should not treat the bull's wording as independently verified by this canon beyond what those two treatments quote.

Sources: Peter Hicks, "Napoléon et le pape Pie VII: du Concordat signé en juillet 1801 à l'excommunication de l'empereur en juin 1809," napoleon.org, read in full via the fetch helper, citing Bernardine Melchior-Bonnet, *Napoléon et le Pape* (Paris: Amiot Dumont, 1958), p. 122, for the excommunication clause's actual wording and its omission of Napoleon's name; Othenin d'Haussonville, "L'Église romaine et les Négociations du Concordat (1800-1814)," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, 2e période, tome 56 (1865), chs. 11–12, read in full at fr.wikisource.org, for the two advance-drafted versions, the bull's incipit, and the posting on the three basilicas.
