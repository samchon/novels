# Institutions and Economy

This file records the governmental, legal, and economic facts of Consular and Imperial France that any scene of administration, law, money, or faith must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`.

## The Consulate and its offices {#consulate-offices}

**Standing: established by research.** Following the Coup of 18 Brumaire (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-brumaire`), the Constitution of the Year VIII — dated 22 Frimaire Year VIII (13 December 1799), publicly proclaimed 24 December 1799, and confirmed afterward by an unusually delayed plebiscite completed in early 1800, so "ratified" and "proclaimed" name two different moments this canon does not conflate, matching the same distinction it draws for the Concordat at `#concordat-1801` — installed Napoleon as First Consul, with Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès as Second Consul and Charles-François Lebrun as Third Consul. Cambacérès focused on legal and judicial affairs and drove the Civil Code drafting process (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#cambaceres`); Lebrun oversaw financial and administrative matters. The system was nominally collegial but the First Consul held decisively greater constitutional power — appointing and dismissing officials, initiating law, commanding the military, negotiating treaties — reducing the other two to advisory and administrative roles. The Year VIII constitution split legislative function deliberately across four bodies to prevent any one from becoming a rival power center: the Conseil d'État (Council of State), which drafted bills, formulated policy, and supervised ministries, staffed by jurists Napoleon appointed from across the old political spectrum and used as his personal legislative workshop; the Tribunate, which debated bills but could not vote on them (purged in 1802, abolished 1807); the Corps législatif, which voted on bills after reviewing the Tribunate's debate record but could not itself debate; and the Conservative Senate, which verified constitutionality and became, in practice, the compliant instrument that ratified Napoleon's successive expansions of power.

Sources: Constitution de la République française du 22 frimaire an VIII (13 December 1799), official French text at Légifrance, https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000571354 (Titles II–IV establishing the Sénat conservateur, Tribunat, Corps législatif, Conseil d'État, and the three Consuls); English translation, The Napoleon Series, "Constitution of the Year VIII," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/legislation/c_constitution8.html (Art. 39–47 for the three Consuls and the First Consul's decisive powers; Titles II–III for the four legislative bodies' distinct functions — the Senate verifying constitutionality, the Tribunate debating without voting, the Corps législatif voting without debating, the Conseil d'État drafting and administering); Wikipedia, "Constitution of the Year VIII," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_VIII, for the adoption/promulgation timeline. The exact date on which the confirming plebiscite's count was completed is not pinned to a source in this canon's research and is not asserted above.

## Transition to Empire and the plebiscites {#empire-transition}

**Standing: established by research, with the plebiscite results flagged as officially reported figures of disputed reliability.** The *sénatus-consulte* of 18 May 1804 established the French Empire and named Napoleon hereditary Emperor, reportedly passed with only three votes against and two abstentions. The subsequent nationwide plebiscite (late May–early August 1804) recorded official results of 3,521,675 "yes" against 2,579 "no" out of roughly 3.6 million eligible male voters — over 99% approval. Historians treat Napoleonic plebiscite numbers with strong skepticism: voting was open and public, with no secret ballot and no provision for blank or spoiled votes, itself suppressing dissent. Historian Claude Langlois's archival research demonstrated outright manipulation in the earlier Year VIII (1799–1800) plebiscite, precedent for the same skepticism applied here — not the invention of a specific fabricated bloc of "military votes," but a documented pattern of departmental totals rounded upward, roughly 20,000 "yes" votes added at the local level, and army units' assent recorded by unverifiable collective "acclamation" rather than individual ballot; against an official reported "yes" of 3,011,007, Langlois found only some 1.5 million individually verifiable votes. The 1804 plebiscite, while less thoroughly documented for manipulation specifically, followed the same public-voting apparatus and produced a suspiciously similar near-unanimous margin; mainstream historians treat the reported near-unanimity as a manufactured legitimation ritual rather than a genuine reading of opinion. Any downstream reference to a plebiscite figure must carry this qualification as "official/reported," never as a reliable measure of actual public sentiment.

Sources: [Wikipedia — 1804 French constitutional referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1804_French_constitutional_referendum); [Wikipedia — 1800 French constitutional referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1800_French_constitutional_referendum); Claude Langlois, "Le plébiscite de l'an VIII, ou le coup d'état du 18 pluviôse an VIII," *Annales historiques de la Révolution française*, no. 207–208 (1972), via Persée: [persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1972_num_207_1_4627](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1972_num_207_1_4627).

## The Napoleonic Code {#napoleonic-code}

**Standing: established by research.** The Code civil des Français was promulgated 21 March 1804 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-empire-proclaimed`), replacing several hundred local customary and Roman-derived law codes with one uniform civil law for France — estimates of the prior number vary (commonly cited figures run roughly 300–400) and this canon does not commit to a single count. Its modernizing principles: legal equality before the law abolishing feudal privilege, secularized civil marriage and limited civil divorce, protection of private property and freedom of contract, careers open to talent rather than birth, and religious toleration in civil matters. Its regressive and patriarchal aspects are equally established and must not be omitted or softened in any downstream use: Article 1124 listed "les mineurs, les interdits, les femmes mariées" — minors, persons under a formal legal-incapacity judgment, and married women — among those legally incapable of contracting, reviving the *femme couverte* ("covered woman") doctrine and requiring a wife's husband's permission to contract, work, or hold property in her own name; Article 213 required wives to obey their husbands, and the separate Article 214 required her to live with him and follow him wherever he chose to reside; the Code's incapacity provisions, reinforced by separate professional-admission rules rather than by one Code article naming the bar, kept women out of licensed professions including law and medicine; divorce, briefly liberalized by mutual consent during the Revolution, was restricted to narrow grounds with harsher standards applied to wives than husbands (a wife's single act of adultery was grounds for divorce, a husband's adultery counted only if he kept a mistress in the marital home); and a widow or divorcée could not remarry for ten months. This legal framework for women persisted substantially in French law until 1938–1965: the "incapable" contract-classification regime began reform in 1938, the adultery double standard was equalized in 1946, and a wife's right to work without her husband's permission came only in 1965, with full equalization completed only later.

Sources: Code civil des Français (1804), original text, Wikisource, https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Code_civil_des_Fran%C3%A7ais_1804/Texte_entier — Art. 213 ("Le mari doit protection à sa femme, la femme obéissance à son mari"), Art. 214 (the wife's obligation to live with and follow her husband "par-tout où il juge à propos de résider"), Art. 1124 (listing "les mineurs, les interdits, les femmes mariées" among those legally incapable of contracting); English excerpts, World History Commons (Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media, George Mason University), "The French Civil Code (1804)," https://worldhistorycommons.org/french-civil-code-1804; Britannica, "Napoleonic Code," https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code, for the modernizing principles; Thierry Lentz, "'Napoleon, the dark side' — Napoleon and women," napoleon.org (Fondation Napoléon), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/napoleon-the-dark-side-napoleon-and-women-4-min-read/, for Article 1124's role, Napoleon's personal role in the Conseil d'État debate, and the specific end dates of these provisions (1938, 1946, 1965); Ute Gerhard, "Civil Law, a Tool of Masculine Domination?," EHNE (Encyclopédie d'histoire numérique de l'Europe, Sorbonne), https://ehne.fr/en/encyclopedia/themes/gender-and-europe/civil-law-a-tool-masculine-domination/civil-law-a-tool-masculine-domination, for comparative scholarly context.

## Reinstatement of slavery {#slavery-reinstatement}

**Standing: established by research, with the campaign's Guadeloupe death toll disputed between a weakly-sourced popular figure and vaguer scholarly language.** The Law of 20 May 1802, signed by First Consul Bonaparte, reinstated and continued slavery in colonies where the 1794 abolition decree had never actually been implemented — Martinique, Tobago, and Saint Lucia, returned to France under the Treaty of Amiens. The law's text is evasive, never stating "slavery" outright: Article 3 authorizes "the slave trade and importation... in accordance with existing laws," and Article 4 effectively suspends the 1794 abolition decree in these territories for ten years; the Tribunat passed it 54–27 and the Corps législatif 211–63. In Guadeloupe, where abolition had actually been implemented since 1794, the 20 May 1802 law did not formally apply, but Napoleon sent General Antoine Richepance with roughly 3,600 troops, landing 6 May 1802, to militarily re-impose slavery; resistance leader Louis Delgrès, declaring "resistance to oppression is a natural right," was crushed after fighting from 10 to 28 May, and roughly 300 resisters chose mass suicide at Matouba (28 May 1802) rather than surrender, with some 250 further death sentences passed by military commission and Richepance's own force taking roughly 40% losses; he himself died of yellow fever 3 September 1802. This canon withdraws a figure it previously carried without a traceable source — "roughly 10% of Guadeloupe's population, some 10,000 people" — which could not be located in any specialist or scholarly account in this research pass; the specialist literature instead describes "several thousand" Guadeloupe deaths without a settled total, and a separate, non-Guadeloupe-specific figure of roughly 100,000 deaths (about 70% Black victims) is given for the whole 1802–1804 West Indies theater combined. A downstream use should describe the toll as severe and in the thousands rather than repeat the withdrawn 10,000/10% figure. A consular decree of 16 July 1802 retroactively legalized this by restoring the pre-1789 colonial legal regime. In Saint-Domingue (Haiti), the Leclerc expedition (departed France December 1801, arrived February 1802, commanded by Napoleon's brother-in-law General Charles Leclerc, husband of Pauline Bonaparte, `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-pauline`) carried secret instructions dated 14 June 1802 to reinstate pre-1789 slavery once the island was pacified; word of the Guadeloupe reinstatement reached Toussaint Louverture's former officers and convinced them of Napoleon's true intent, reigniting the war that culminated in Haitian independence, declared 1 January 1804. This canon requires this fact be available to any scene touching colonial policy, the Leclerc expedition, or Napoleon's legal reforms; it may not be omitted as inconvenient background, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#viewpoint-conventions`.

the Tribunat and Corps législatif vote counts); Fondation pour la mémoire de l'esclavage — France's specialist national institution for the history of slavery, established by law — "Étude du décret-loi autorisant l'esclavage dans les colonies – 20 mai 1802," https://memoire-esclavage.org/espace-enseignant/activite/etude-du-decret-loi-autorisant-lesclavage-dans-les-colonies-20-mai-1802, and "Décret du 20 mai 1802, le rétablissement de l'esclavage par Napoléon Bonaparte," https://memoire-esclavage.org/decret-du-20-mai-1802-le-retablissement-de-lesclavage-par-napoleon-bonaparte; ## Legion of Honor {#legion-of-honor}

**Standing: established by research.** Founded 19 May 1802 by First Consul Bonaparte as a merit-based order of honor explicitly distinct from a revival of aristocratic chivalric orders — awarded for civil and military merit "without distinction of birth," open to men of any social origin provided the recipient swore to uphold liberty and equality, and deliberately cutting across former royalist and revolutionary factional lines. Napoleon's own justification: France needed "a start towards the organisation of the Nation" through recognition of merit rather than a new nobility — a founding rhetoric this canon notes sits in real tension with his later creation of an imperial nobility of titles in 1808. Originally four ranks (knight/légionnaire, officer, commandant, grand-officer); as Emperor he added a fifth, top rank, grand-aigle. Awards were made by the Grand Chancellor's council on nomination, typically for a specific act of military valor or notable civil service, and carried a modest pension.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9gion_d%27honneur); New World Encyclopedia, "Légion d'honneur" (founding date, four original ranks, grand-aigle added by decree of 10 Pluviôse Year XIII / 30 January 1805). The "a start towards the organisation of the Nation" justification is widely repeated in tertiary summaries but this canon could not trace it to a primary or archival locator in this pass — it is retained as widely-attributed rhetoric, not a confirmed verbatim quotation, and should not be treated as more certain than that downstream.

## Currency: the franc germinal {#franc-germinal}

**Standing: established by research, with the assignat depreciation figure resting on a traditional, widely-cited estimate rather than a single modern-peer-reviewed count.** Established by the law of 7 Germinal Year XI (28 March 1803), the franc germinal defined the franc as 5 grams of silver at 0.900 fine, on a bimetallic standard with the silver-to-gold ratio fixed at 15.5:1 (a gold franc equivalent to roughly 290.32 mg fine gold). Its purpose was to end the catastrophic hyperinflation of the Revolutionary assignat: the commonly cited figures — a roughly 99% loss of purchasing power by 1795–96, with circulation growing from roughly 1.5 billion livres in late 1791 to nearly 20 billion by November 1795 — trace to nineteenth-century monetary-history writing rather than a single modern archival count, and other reputable secondary accounts give a somewhat less extreme depreciation (estimates in the literature range roughly 92–99% depending on measurement date and method); this canon retains the commonly cited 99%/1.5-to-20-billion figures as its working reference but flags them as traditional rather than freshly peer-reviewed. The franc germinal gave France a stable, metal-backed currency that held its value into the twentieth century, commonly cited as stable until the First World War; this canon uses it as the fixed reference for all Napoleonic-era financial facts from 1803 onward, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#measurement-conventions`.

Sources: Loi du 7 germinal an XI (28 mars 1803), quoted directly at Fondation Napoléon/napoleon.org, "La création du 'franc germinal' (Loi du 7 germinal an XI / 28 mars 1803)," https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/dossiers-thematiques/la-creation-du-franc-germinal-loi-du-7-germinal-an-xi-28-mars-1803/ (quoting the law's operative clause, "Cinq grammes d'argent au titre de 9/10e de fin, constituent l'unité monétaire qui consacre le nom de franc," the 1 kg gold = 15.5 kg silver ratio, and the coin denominations minted); Guy Thuillier, *La réforme monétaire de l'an XI, la création du franc germinal*, reviewed with a documentary edition of 75 primary sources in *Annales historiques de la Révolution française*, no. 295 (1994), via Persée, https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1994_num_295_1_1608_t1_0136_0000_2, corroborating the same figures; for the assignat collapse, Andrew Dickson White, *Fiat Money Inflation in France* (1876/1896), full text at Project Gutenberg, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6949/6949-h/6949-h.htm — the monograph itself, superseding this canon's prior citation to a Wikipedia article about the book rather than the book; Richard M. ## Pay and price benchmarks {#pay-price-benchmarks}

**Standing: established by research for the officer rates, which rest on a published military history; not yet decided for the enlisted rates, the elite-income comparator, and all common-goods prices, none of which this canon can settle from the sources it has read.** French army pay circa 1803, used here as order-of-magnitude reference only: private (fantassin) roughly 0.62 francs/day; corporal roughly 0.96 francs/day; sergeant roughly 1.23 francs/day; sub-lieutenant roughly 125 francs/month; lieutenant roughly 141.6 francs/month plus a daily allowance; captain roughly 233 francs/month plus allowance; chef de bataillon roughly 408 francs/month plus allowance; colonel roughly 562.5 francs/month plus allowance; général de brigade roughly 833 francs/month plus allowance; général de division roughly 1,250 francs/month plus allowance; Marshal of the Empire roughly 3,333 francs/month. For relative wealth scale: a grand officer of the Légion d'honneur who was also a senator drew an annual income of roughly 20,000 francs, two orders of magnitude above a private soldier's roughly 225 francs a year, giving a workable relative scale of elite versus common income even though a single authoritative period source for common-goods prices such as bread was not located in this canon's research. Any specific bread or common-goods price used in a downstream scene must be independently verified against a period administrative *mercuriale* record or an economic history such as Braudel before being treated as established rather than invented; until then it is not yet decided.

Sources: for the officer figures, The Napoleon Series, "French Officer Pay during the Napoleonic Wars," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/abstract/military/army/france/pay/c_frenchpay.html, which reproduces the same monthly rates and credits them to Georges Blond, *La Grande Armée* (London: Arms and Armour Press, 1995), p. 512 — a published military history, and a materially better warrant than the uncredited hobbyist table this canon previously relied on. The enlisted daily rates are weaker and are not upgraded: they appear only in that uncredited compilation, and at least one competing secondary figure gives roughly half these amounts (fusilier 30 centimes, corporal 45, sergeant 62 a day), so the private's rate and the roughly 225 francs a year derived from it remain approximate and contested. The "roughly 20,000 francs" elite comparator is likewise a composite rather than an attested single figure: a Grand Officer's Legion pension was 5,000 francs a year, while senatorial income reached roughly 20,000–25,000 only for those holding a *sénatorerie* endowment. Rafe Blaufarb, *The French Army, 1750–1820: Careers, Talent, Merit* (Manchester University Press, 2002), is named as the standing scholarly reference for a future verification pass; this canon has not consulted it and cites it for no figure above.

## The Concordat of 1801 {#concordat-1801}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact staging and Pius VII's degree of foreknowledge at the coronation flagged as disputed among historians.** The Concordat was signed between Bonaparte's government and Pope Pius VII on 15 July 1801 and ratified by both parties that same year — Pius VII on 15 August, Bonaparte in the first days of September — while what belongs to 1802 is its promulgation in France on 8 April, bundled with the Organic Articles that Bonaparte added unilaterally and Rome never accepted. This canon states the three stages separately because the compressed formula "ratified in 1802" conflates the ratification with the promulgation and hides the Organic Articles entirely. The agreement recognized Catholicism as "the religion of the great majority of French citizens" without re-establishing it as the state religion, preserving formal religious liberty; the state gained the right to nominate bishops while the Pope retained canonical investiture, clergy swore an oath of allegiance to the government, and the state took over paying clergy salaries in exchange for the Church renouncing claims to confiscated Revolutionary-era Church lands. Its political purpose was to reconcile Catholic France, especially the countryside and the Vendée, with the post-Revolutionary state and to end the schismatic "Constitutional Church" chaos of the dechristianization period, bringing the Church under firm state control rather than papal or independent authority — a pragmatic instrument of political consolidation, not an act of personal piety. At the coronation, 2 December 1804, Notre-Dame (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-coronation`), Pius VII performed the religious anointing, the historically essential legitimating element of French royal coronations, but as he reached to take up the imperial crown, Napoleon seized it himself and placed it on his own head, then crowned Joséphine himself — a deliberate, premeditated symbolic assertion that his authority derived from his own merit and the will of the people rather than the Church, breaking sharply with the traditional Reims ritual where the archbishop crowned the king. David's famous 1807 painting of the coronation actually depicts Napoleon crowning the kneeling Joséphine, a different and more flattering moment than the self-crowning, which is why the painting is popularly misremembered as showing the self-coronation itself. Historians dispute whether Pius VII was forewarned of the self-crowning gesture or was blindsided by it; the more common scholarly reading treats it as a calculated snub of papal authority that Pius VII participated in only under considerable political pressure, having gained comparatively little from the arrangement.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Concordat of 1801," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordat_of_1801, for the 15 July 1801 signature, the 1801 ratifications, the "religion of the great majority of French citizens" wording, the bishop-nomination and clergy-salary terms, and the 8 April 1802 promulgation together with the Organic Articles; Wikipedia, "Coronation of Napoleon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_Napoleon, and "The Coronation of Napoleon" (David, 1807), for the painting depicting the crowning of the kneeling Joséphine rather than the self-crowning; Fondation Napoléon, "The Coronation," https://www.fondationnapoleon.org.
