# Geography and Distances

This file records the geographic reference facts — distances, march rates, and communication speeds — that let any scene of travel, campaign movement, or dispatch be checked for arithmetic consistency, per `principles/settings.md#internal-coherence`. Battlefield-scale terrain for Austerlitz specifically is at `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-terrain`.

## Corsica and Ajaccio {#geography-corsica}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon's birthplace, Ajaccio, sits on Corsica's western coast; the island lies roughly 170 km southeast of the French mainland (Toulon/Nice coast) and roughly 90 km west of the Italian (Tuscan) coast, closer to Italy than to France in raw distance despite its French sovereignty since 1768 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-corsica-annexation`) — a geographic fact this canon treats as reinforcing rather than resolving Napoleon's ambivalent Corsican/French identity (`settings/003-napoleon.md#napoleon-origins`).

Sources: Encyclopædia Britannica, "Corsica," https://www.britannica.com/place/Corsica, which gives the island as lying 105 miles (170 km) from southern France and 56 miles (90 km) from northwestern Italy, and places Ajaccio on the western coast — the direct authority for both figures used above. Note that these are island-to-mainland distances, not Ajaccio-specific ones: Ajaccio itself sits on the far side of the island from Italy, roughly 232 km from Nice and 266 km from Toulon by straight line, so a scene computing a sailing time from Ajaccio must use the larger figures, not the 170 km crossing.

## Paris and the seats of government {#geography-paris}

**Standing: established by research.** The Tuileries Palace was Napoleon's principal Paris residence and seat of government through the Consulate and Empire; Saint-Cloud and Malmaison (the latter Joséphine's own property, retained by her after the divorce, `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`) served as the principal suburban residences. Notre-Dame de Paris, site of the 1804 coronation (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-coronation`), and the Louvre (the Musée Napoléon, directed by Denon from November 1802, `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#denon`) are the other Paris sites this canon fixes as available without further invention.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, "Napoleon's lost palaces: the Tuileries, Saint-Cloud, Meudon," https://www.fondationnapoleon.org, on the Tuileries as principal seat of government 1800–1814 and Saint-Cloud as the preferred suburban residence from spring 1802; `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-coronation` for Notre-Dame; `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#denon` for the Louvre/Musée Napoléon under Denon from November 1802; `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine` for Malmaison as Joséphine's retained property.

## The Channel coast and the invasion camps {#geography-boulogne}

**Standing: established by research.** Paris to Boulogne-sur-Mer is roughly 256–259 km by road. The invasion camps of 1803–1805 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-boulogne`, `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-boulogne-camp`) were sited at Boulogne, Bruges, and Montreuil along this stretch of Channel coast, within reach of the invasion flotilla built in these ports.

Sources: Paris–Boulogne-sur-Mer road distance ≈256–259 km, per [travelmath.com](https://www.travelmath.com/drive-distance/from/Paris,+France/to/Boulogne-sur-Mer,+France); cross-reference `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-boulogne-camp` for the camp's siting at Boulogne, Bruges, and Montreuil.

## The road east: Rhine to the Danube {#geography-rhine-danube}

**Standing: established by research, distances given as modern road/straight-line approximations pending a period itinerary source.** Boulogne-sur-Mer to Strasbourg is roughly 590–628 km by road (route calculators vary; 624 km is commonly cited but sits at the high end of the observed range, not a settled figure). Strasbourg to Ulm is roughly 231–245 km by road (roughly 167 km straight-line). By 6 October 1805 the Grande Armée had wheeled south from its Rhine crossing (conventionally dated 26 September 1805) and stood along the Danube from Ulm to Ingolstadt — roughly six to ten days from the Rhine crossing to the Danube line over the intervening 250–300 km, consistent with the sustained march rate given at `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching`. Individual corps fanned out on parallel roads in the classic *bataillon carré* formation (Davout via Mergentheim–Ingelfingen–Crailsheim–Dinkelsbühl–Fremdingen–Oettingen–Harburg–Donauwörth; Soult via Öhringen–Hall–Gaildorf–Abtsgmünd–Aalen–Nördlingen; Ney via Esslingen–Göppingen–Heidenheim–Nördlingen), letting corps concentrate rapidly on whichever point Mack chose to stand.

Sources: Boulogne–Strasbourg road distance ≈590–628 km (route calculators vary; 624–628 km at the high end) and Strasbourg–Ulm road distance ≈228–231 km (≈167 km straight-line), per [distancefromto.net](https://www.distancefromto.net/distance-from-ulm-de-to-strasbourg-fr) and corroborating route calculators; [World History Encyclopedia — "Ulm Campaign"](https://www.worldhistory.org) for the Rhine-crossing date and the *bataillon carré* wheel toward the Danube. The specific corps-by-corps march routes (Davout via Mergentheim–Ingelfingen–Crailsheim…) remain sourced only to this canon's prior general campaign reading rather than an opened copy of David Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon*, which this canon does not cite: the routing is retained as plausible and consistent with the *bataillon carré* formation but flagged as not independently re-verified against a primary itinerary in this pass.

## Vienna and the approach to Moravia {#geography-vienna-moravia}

**Standing: established by research.** Ulm to Vienna is roughly 473 km straight-line (roughly 544–594 km by modern road depending on route; 544 km is the shortest-route low end, not a single settled figure; the period route would differ in detail). Vienna to Austerlitz (Slavkov u Brna) is roughly 122–135 km. Paris to Vienna, straight-line reference, is roughly 1,034 km. Davout's III Corps covered the Vienna-to-Austerlitz distance in the forced march detailed at `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`.

Sources: Ulm–Vienna straight-line distance ≈472–473 km, per [distancebetween2.com](https://distancebetween2.com/ulm/vienna); Vienna–Austerlitz (Slavkov u Brna) road distance ≈134 km / straight-line ≈122–135 km per rome2rio route data (this canon retains the narrower 122–135 km range as its working figure, per `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-distances-table`); Paris–Vienna straight-line distance ≈1,034 km per [distancebetween2.com](https://distancebetween2.com/paris/vienna).

## Distance and march-rate reference table {#geography-distances-table}

**Standing: established by research, consolidating the figures above for arithmetic checking.** Paris–Boulogne ~256–259 km; Boulogne–Strasbourg ~590–628 km (commonly cited ~624 km); Strasbourg–Ulm ~231–245 km road (~167 km straight-line); Ulm–Vienna ~473 km straight-line (~544–594 km road); Vienna–Austerlitz ~122–135 km; Paris–Vienna ~1,034 km straight-line. Sustained infantry march rate: ~20–32 km/day. Forced-march rate: up to ~56 km/day in exceptional, short-duration cases (`settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching`). Any downstream scene computing a travel time must use these figures or, where a more specific period itinerary is required, flag the more specific figure as not yet independently verified rather than inventing false precision.

Sources: consolidation of the entries above (`#geography-boulogne`, `#geography-rhine-danube`, `#geography-vienna-moravia`), each established by research with locators there; `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching` for the march-rate figures.

## Courier and communication speed {#geography-courier-speed}

**Standing: established by research for the general pre-modern benchmark; not yet decided for Napoleon's own dispatch-rider network specifically.** General period relay-horse courier systems, changing mounts roughly every 15 miles (24 km), could sustain roughly 100 km per day, substantially faster than infantry march rates, with a single rider on a fresh horse capable of considerably more over a short burst. This is a general historical benchmark for courier relay systems of the era, not a verified figure for Napoleon's own *estafette* dispatch-rider network specifically; no dedicated source on Berthier's staff courier system's actual sustained speed was located in this canon's research, so any downstream claim asserting a specific rate for Napoleon's own couriers is not yet decided and must either cite a dedicated source at that time or use the general benchmark explicitly flagged as general rather than Napoleon-specific.

Sources: general pre-modern relay-post benchmarks (the *poste aux chevaux* system, changing mounts roughly every 15 miles), cross-checked against period courier-system literature; this research pass again searched specifically for a dedicated source on Napoleon's own *estafette* dispatch-rider network's sustained speed and again did not locate one, so the standing remains unchanged: "not yet decided" for Napoleon's own network specifically, established only for the general period benchmark.
