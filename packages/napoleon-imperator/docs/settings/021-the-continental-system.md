# The Continental System

This file records the economic blockade of Britain — its legal architecture, its administration, the licensing regime that contradicted its own absolute language, the smuggling it never suppressed, and its effects on French, Continental, and British commerce — that any scene touching trade, customs, finance, or the empire's relations with its satellites between 1806 and 1813 must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Legal architecture: the Berlin and Milan Decrees {#continental-legal-architecture}

**Standing: established by research.** The Berlin Decree, signed by Napoleon at Berlin on 21 November 1806, a month after Jena-Auerstedt (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-jena-tilsit`), declared the British Isles "in a state of blockade" (Art. 1); forbade all trade and correspondence with Britain, including the carriage of English-addressed or English-language mail (Art. 2); ordered any British subject found in French or allied-occupied territory made a prisoner of war (Art. 3); declared all British-owned goods and property lawful prize (Art. 4) and all trade in English goods forbidden, whatever their ownership (Art. 5); barred any vessel that had called at an English port from being received in any Continental port (Art. 7); and routed prize disputes to the Prize Court of Paris or, for the Kingdom of Italy, Milan (Art. 9). Its eight-clause preamble frames the measure as reciprocity rather than aggression: it accuses Britain of extending the laws of blockade — properly restricted to fortified places actually invested by sufficient force — to open commercial towns and whole coastlines never invested by a single warship, and of treating private merchant crews and traveling merchants as prisoners of war, and asserts the "natural right to oppose to the enemy the arms which he uses himself." Two further decrees followed from Milan: a first, dated 23 November 1807, extended the regime empire-wide with certificate-of-origin requirements and confiscation for non-compliant vessels — this canon knows its content only through Eli Heckscher's 1922 monograph rather than an opened primary text, and does not claim to have read it directly. The second, dated 17 December 1807 and read here in full, was explicit retaliation for Britain's Orders in Council of 11 November 1807, which had required neutral shipping to submit to search and pay a duty in a British port before proceeding; it created the "denationalization" doctrine this canon treats as the System's most legally radical instrument: "Any vessel, of whatever nation, which shall have submitted to a search by an English vessel, or to a voyage to England, or shall have paid any tax whatsoever to the English government, is by that fact alone declared denationalized, has lost the guarantee of its flag, and has become English property" (Art. 1), and such vessels became lawful prize wherever a French warship, privateer, or allied port could reach them (Art. 2-3). The decree's own preamble calls the British measures "a just reciprocity for the barbarous system... which assimilates its legislation to that of Algiers."

## Administration and enforcement {#continental-enforcement}

**Standing: established by research.** Enforcement ran through a network of French customs officers operating with extraterritorial reach into nominally sovereign allied states: permanent posts were installed at Hamburg and along the Elbe estuary from shortly after the Berlin decree, and a new "external customs" line was built across the Grand Duchy of Berg in July 1809, running from Rees on the Rhine through the annexed principality of Salm. The Treaties of Tilsit (July 1807, `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-jena-tilsit`) formally brought Russia and Prussia into the blockade; Scandinavian states were drawn in from September 1807 (Sweden formally from 6 January 1810); Portugal was enrolled only by outright French invasion beginning 23 November 1807. A military cordon backed the customs courts: Napoleon's own letter to Marshal Davout of 1 January [1811] states the two divisions stationed on the German North Sea coast had "as their sole task the prevention of smuggling," and a plan to seize the British-held smuggling entrepôt of Heligoland by military force was seriously weighed and abandoned in late 1810 in favor of coastal fortification. Davout — the historian Sorel's "archi-douanier," chief customs officer, of the Empire — was the linchpin of North Sea enforcement, and enforcement collapsed within weeks of his March 1812 departure for the front, months before the Russian invasion itself began. Special customs courts, the *cours prévôtales des douanes*, carried steep penalties under the Fontainebleau decree of 18 October 1810: ten years' penal servitude and branding for a smuggling ringleader, five to ten years' police supervision on top of a lesser correctional sentence for a mere porter. Enforcement was nonetheless chronically leaky by structural necessity, not incompetence: Napoleon's own finance minister Mollien described roughly 20,000 customs officials, whose posts were known to everyone, attempting to guard a frontier against more than 100,000 smugglers with good Parisian connections and the sympathy of local populations. Corruption reached the top of the administration itself — Napoleon's own ambassador at Hamburg, Bourrienne (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bourrienne`), presided over what one specialist study calls "toutes les corruptions possibles," and Bourrienne's own estimate, recorded in Mollien's memoirs, put the number of smugglers in Hamburg alone at 6,000. Even loyal allied administrations could not suppress the trade: the Grand Duchy of Baden protested its good intentions but, in a specialist study's own words, "had no more grip on the problem than any other government in Europe." In the first months after the Berlin decree Napoleon was campaigning in Poland and the German coast went largely unwatched, so the decree was, in Roger Dufraisse's phrase, largely "lettre morte" — a dead letter — in its own opening season.

## The licensing system {#continental-licensing}

**Standing: established by research.** The absolute prohibitions of the decrees were, from 1809, systematically undercut by a state-run licensing regime that this canon treats as evidence of the System's real character: a genuine attempt to weaponize trade for France's benefit, not a literal prohibition ever intended to stop all commerce. A secret confidential circular of 14 April 1809 first authorized, exceptionally, the export of brandy, fruit, vegetables, grain, or salt to England and the import of wood, hemp, iron, or quinine from England, against a licence fee of 600-800 francs. Decrees of 4 December 1809 and 14 February 1810 widened the goods list (oils, textiles, iron, cotton) and expanded issuance; roughly 350 such early licences permitted some 10 million francs of exports and 6 million francs of imports. The regime tightened and formalized further through 1810: a decree of 3 July reserved licences to French-flagged vessels only; a decree of 25 July created a wholly new system requiring the Emperor's own personal authorization for every ship entering or leaving an imperial port, mandating that every import be balanced by an export of at least equal value (a third of which had to be silk goods), and restricting imports strictly to foodstuffs and raw materials, never manufactured goods. Before this Trianon-era tightening, total trade conducted under French licences had reached only about 20 million francs, a marginal fraction of prewar commerce; Bordeaux's merchants, by comparison, used only 171 licences plus 390 American "permis" for 561 total ship departures across the entire 1809-1814 period — roughly a fifth of one prewar year's activity spread across five. Britain ran a parallel, far larger licensing system of its own, over 18,000 licences in 1810 alone covering, per Heckscher's sources, "the greater part... of the maritime trade of the whole world" — a fact this canon records as evidence the self-blockade logic was compromised on both belligerents' sides, not only the French one. Napoleon's own rationale survives in his correspondence: to his stepson Eugène de Beauharnais, viceroy of Italy, on 23 August 1810, "Vous ne devez jamais perdre de vue que, si le commerce anglais triomphe sur mer, c'est parce que les Anglais y sont les plus forts; il est donc convenable, puisque la France est plus forte sur terre, qu'elle y fasse aussi triompher son commerce" (you must never lose sight of the fact that if English commerce triumphs at sea, it is because the English are strongest there; it is fitting, since France is stronger on land, that she make her own commerce triumph there too); and to his finance minister Gaudin, 29 May 1810, on wanting to "favour the export of foodstuffs from France and the import of money from abroad," adding he would rather let smugglers in only at Dunkirk "unless current practice required that they should also be received at Flushing" — a deliberately mercantilist, specie-extraction logic, not a pretense that the trade did not exist.

## Smuggling {#continental-smuggling}

**Standing: established by research.** The blockade was evaded on a scale that made it, in practice, a heavily taxed and dangerous trade rather than a sealed frontier. Heligoland, occupied by Britain from September 1807, became the chief entrepôt for contraband into north Germany: by 1808 Britain had spent some £500,000 building a fortified port and warehouse complex on an island of roughly 150 acres, hosted some 200 resident British merchants, and at times warehoused goods worth about £8 million — nearly a sixth of Britain's entire 1808 export trade. Tönning on the Danish Eider fed the same goods onward into Hamburg; Gothenburg and Malta served as comparable depots for north German and Dalmatian traffic once French enforcement slackened during the 1809 Austrian campaign; Frankfurt was, per Dufraisse, "one of the great turntables of smuggling," with the Rothschild banking house there documented profiting from cross-border contraband while brushing with French police five times between 1807 and 1811 and escaping consequence through the protection of the prince-primate Dalberg; and Cologne's merchant fortunes swelled visibly on contraband profits — before the French arrival only six Cologne merchants held fortunes over 150,000 francs, but by 1812, with licit trade sharply down, houses worth over 350,000 francs were common and some exceeded 2 million. Routes shifted continually with the frontier of enforcement, running at various points from Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Cologne to Bremen-Hamburg-Frankfurt to Stettin-Danzig-Leipzig, and, after the 1812 rupture with Russia, via Constantinople and Salonika through Lemberg to supply the Vienna market — the same network Heckscher records running "from Gothenburg in the northwest around all the coasts of Europe to Saloniki in the south[east]." Coastal mechanics were correspondingly inventive: on the North Sea, Bremen — called a "smuggling metropolis" by the contemporary historian Max Schäfer — saw goods run across the tidal mudflats, coffee disguised as horse-beans and sugar as starch, and British goods counterfeited down to their wrappers and forged French manufacturers' marks; on the Channel, goods were reportedly packed into hermetically sealed chests, thrown overboard chained to buoys off Cowes, and hauled ashore on the French coast like a fisherman's net. The scale is independently confirmed by French customs receipts themselves, which collapsed as trade fled the taxed, licit channel for contraband: 51.2 million francs in 1806 and 60.6 million in 1807 fell to 18.6 million in 1808 and a mere 11.6 million in 1809, before rebounding to 105.9 million for the period from the Trianon tariff through the end of 1811 as the state recaptured and taxed trade that had previously flowed entirely to smugglers. Smuggling margins ran high but not prohibitive — costs of 40-50% of a good's value in the worst periods, typically around 30% by 1809, and as little as 6-8% across the newer, more porous Rees-Bremen customs line — and specialized insurance existed specifically to cover smugglers' seizure risk, at premiums of at least 2.5% of the goods' value. Napoleon himself estimated that smugglers had "pocketed 20,000,000 francs" in Holland before he closed the Dutch frontier to colonial goods by decree on 16 September 1808, and confiscated contraband, publicly burned under the Fontainebleau decree's *auto-da-fé* provisions, totaled some 4.5 million francs in the Hanse Towns alone on incomplete contemporary accounting.

## Economic effects and the System's collapse {#continental-economic-effects}

**Standing: established by research, with the British-side historiographical verdict flagged as not independently verified in this pass.** Effects on French industry were genuinely mixed rather than uniformly protective. Cotton manufacturers Richard-Lenoir and Oberkampf saw output surge — Oberkampf's Jouy factory printed 1.7 million *aunes* of cotton cloth in 1805 against barely 800,000 two years before — and English goods were practically absent from the French market from 1807 to 1810, with textile imports falling from 65 million francs (1805) to 11 million (1807) and staying below 20 million even in 1816, after the Empire's fall, evidence of a durable import-substitution effect; French exports to the German states rose 37% (104 to over 143 million francs, 1804-1811). But overall French export totals stagnated below their 1806 peak of 408 million francs throughout 1807-1811, chiefly from the collapse of neutral, especially American, trade, and raw-material shortage bit hard: Oberkampf's 1806 order of 4,000 bales of American cotton saw only 360 bales clear customs a year later, and 40% of Parisian cotton spinners and weavers lost work within months in 1807; the Trianon tariff of 5 August 1810 then imposed duties of 400-800 francs per 100 kg of cotton by origin, against a prewar rate of just 1 franc. Wool substitution succeeded better — French flocks roughly doubled between 1797 and 1813 (2 to 4 million head), and woolen-cloth output nearly quadrupled — while the state-promoted beet-sugar industry, for which Napoleon personally decorated the refiner Delessert with the Legion of Honor, badly missed its own cultivation targets: a decree of 25 March 1809 mandating 32,000 hectares under beet, raised to 100,000 hectares by decree of 15 January 1812, had achieved only 6,785 planted hectares by the end of 1812. The domestic credit and unemployment crisis this produced by early 1811 — 1,400 of Paris's 1,700 textile firms halted and roughly 40% of textile workers idle — is recorded at scene-relevant depth at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#economic-crisis-1810-1811` rather than repeated here. Port cities paid a heavier, more visible price: Marseille's ship traffic fell from roughly 200 vessels in 1805 to under 50 by 1808 and to just 4 by 1812; Bordeaux's wartime shipping implies roughly a fifth of prewar activity, and the city held some 12,000 indigents out of 90,000 residents by the Empire's end, turning popular sentiment hostile enough that the city welcomed the comte d'Artois warmly in 1814; Hamburg's seized English-goods stocks were assessed by Napoleon at 17 million francs in 1810-11 (against 2 million for Lübeck and 9.15 million for Leipzig, while Bremen smuggled most of its stock away in time and accounted for only 377,000 francs), and Frankfurt's great trade fairs were, per the historian Darmstädter, "deserted by many merchants who did not return after 1815." On the British side, this canon's research could establish real, severe, but genuinely multi-causal damage rather than settle the long historiographical argument over how close the System came to working: British cotton-manufacture export values fell about 35.5% in 1811 (£18.4 million in 1809 to £11.5 million in 1811), a chain-reaction financial crisis followed the insolvency of a leading West Indian produce dealer and his bankers, coffee prices in London collapsed by roughly two-thirds between January 1810 and March 1811, and some 600 British vessels were left stranded in the Baltic unable to land cargo after the 1810 tightening — but Eli Heckscher, the standard earlier monograph (read here directly), attributes much of this crisis independently to British over-shipment into newly opened South American markets and to the disruptive effect of American non-intercourse laws, not to the blockade alone, and records British recovery well underway by early 1812. This canon could not independently verify François Crouzet's own conclusions in *L'économie britannique et le blocus continental* (1958) — the standard later monograph, which frames its inquiry as "could the Continental Blockade compel England to yield?" — having reached only one page of a contemporary review of it rather than the book itself, and a downstream unit should not attribute a specific verdict on that question to Crouzet without a fuller reading. The System's own unraveling is well dated regardless of that dispute: the Trianon and Fontainebleau turn to fiscal licensing (August-October 1810) already marked a retreat from absolute prohibition; Tsar Alexander I's refusal in autumn 1810 to enforce the blockade against neutral shipping in the Baltic, followed by a new, more permissive Russian tariff, opened the rupture that led to 1812 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`); Davout's March 1812 departure from the North Sea coast — months before the invasion itself — collapsed enforcement there; and by January 1813, with Russian troops advancing along the Baltic, crowds in Hamburg and the Grand Duchy of Berg tore down French customs barriers by force, one prefect reporting smuggling "raising its head all along the line." Heckscher's own term for this trajectory, "the self-destruction of the Continental System," is this canon's preferred framing: internal fiscal and enforcement contradictions were already dissolving the System before the military collapse of 1812-1814 delivered its formal end, revoked with Napoleon's abdication in April 1814.
