# Waterloo

This file records the terrain, order of battle, chronology, and aftermath of the Battle of Waterloo (18 June 1815) at the standard `settings/012-austerlitz.md` and `settings/016-borodino.md` set, so that a storyline reaching 1815 needs to invent nothing of the battle's setting or mechanics, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`. The Belgian campaign's shape, Ligny, and Quatre-Bras are at `settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md`; figure-level detail for commanders is at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md` and `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md`. Fixed headline figures for troop strength and single-battle casualties are already established at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-waterloo`; this file supplies the granular detail beneath those headline figures rather than restating them.

## Terrain and the mud {#waterloo-terrain}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact time fighting began genuinely disputed among named sources.** Wellington's position ran along a long, gently sloping ridge running east–west, bisected by the main road to Brussels, with the sunken Ohain road (the *chemin creux*) running along its crest; a large elm tree near the crossroads, roughly at the center of the position, served as Wellington's command post for much of the day. He deployed his infantry just behind the ridge's crest along the Ohain road, using the reverse slope — as he had done many times before in the Peninsula — to conceal his strength from the French except for his skirmishers and artillery. The front was relatively short, roughly four kilometers, allowing real depth. Three fortified outposts anchored the position. Hougoumont, on the French right, was held by roughly 1,800–2,000 troops (the 2nd Nassau Regiment's 1st Battalion, light companies of the Coldstream and Scots Guards, two light companies of the Grenadier Guards, and Hanoverian jägers) under Lieutenant-Colonel James Macdonell; Wellington himself judged that "the success of the battle turned upon the closing of the gates at Hougoumont," and the château absorbed roughly thirty-three French battalions (some 14,000 men) against roughly twenty-one Allied battalions (12,000) feeding into its defense across the day — one of the most lopsided diversions of the battle. La Haye Sainte, in the center, was held by some 400 King's German Legion troops under Major Georg Baring, who fortified it the night before; a British officer present, Captain James Shaw, judged its eventual loss "a very dangerous incident," since it "uncovered the very centre of the Anglo-allied army" and let French skirmishers and horse artillery work within sixty yards of Wellington's line. Papelotte, La Haie, and Frichermont, on the left, were held by two Orange-Nassau regiments and covered the approach of the Prussian I Corps later in the day.

A violent overnight storm soaked the ground, and Napoleon delayed his attack to let it firm enough for artillery and cavalry to maneuver; his own health may also have affected the delay, since he was reportedly unwell that morning (hemorrhoids or dysuria, the exact ailment not established) in a way contemporaries and later historians connect to his uncharacteristic lack of urgency. Even the basic question of when the battle began is unsettled between sources. Wellington's own official despatch to Earl Bathurst, dated 19 June 1815 and read directly by this canon, states plainly: "at about ten o'clock he commenced a furious attack upon our post at Hougoumont" — the source of the ten o'clock figure this canon adopts. Other secondary accounts give roughly 11:30 instead, and the grand battery's bombardment of the center is separately dated to between noon and 13:30 depending on the source; a modern historian is quoted, via Wikipedia's synthesis rather than this canon's own direct reading of his book, calling this "a curious fact about the Battle of Waterloo that no one is absolutely certain when it actually began." This canon states the range at each of these three points rather than adopting a single clock time. The same overnight rain delayed the Prussian approach march as badly as it delayed the French attack, since Bülow's men had to pass through the congested, partly fire-blocked streets of Wavre.

Sources: Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington, despatch to Earl Bathurst, Waterloo, 19 June 1815, published in *The London Gazette*, no. 17028, 22 June 1815, and reproduced in full at Wikisource, "A Sketch of the Battle of Waterloo/Despatch from field-marshal the duke of Wellington to earl Bathurst," https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Sketch_of_the_Battle_of_Waterloo/Despatch_from_field-marshal_the_duke_of_Wellington_to_earl_Bathurst, read directly for the ten o'clock Hougoumont timing quoted above; Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo, citing Andrew Roberts, *Waterloo: Napoleon's Last Gamble* (2005) — this canon has not opened Roberts's book directly and cites the quotation only as Wikipedia's own synthesis reports it; "Hougoumont," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hougoumont; "La Haye Sainte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Haye_Sainte; "Papelotte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papelotte; Thierry Lentz, "Bullet Point #35 – How did Napoleon manage to lose the Battle of Waterloo?", napoleon.org, June 2020, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/bullet-point-35-how-did-napoleon-manage-to-lose-the-battle-of-waterloo/, read via `fetch.sh`, for the mud, the delay, and Napoleon's possible illness.

## Order of battle: French {#waterloo-oob-french}

**Standing: established by research, with total strength given as a range across named sources.** Napoleon held supreme command, with Marshal Soult as chief of staff in Berthier's absence (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-command-friction`) and Marshal Ney as effective field commander of the forces engaged in the main assault, excluding those fighting the Prussians at Plancenoit. I Corps, under Jean-Baptiste Drouet, Comte d'Erlon (19,357 men, forty-four guns), carried the great mid-afternoon infantry assault; among its named losses, the 105th Line Regiment's Eagle was captured by Sergeant Charles Ewart of the Scots Greys. II Corps, under Honoré Reille (22,731 men, thirty-two guns), attacked Hougoumont for most of the day, Jérôme Bonaparte commanding the division directly engaged there. VI Corps, under Georges Mouton, Comte de Lobau (9,295 men, thirty-two guns), screened and then fought the arriving Prussians at Plancenoit. Two heavy cavalry corps completed the field army: III Cavalry Corps under François Étienne de Kellermann (son of the Marengo Kellermann; 3,702 men, twelve guns) and IV Cavalry Corps under Édouard Milhaud (2,982 men, twelve guns). The Imperial Guard's nominal commander, Marshal Mortier, was absent on sick leave with sciatica, leaving Général de Division Antoine Drouot as effective second-in-command; its Grenadier Division under Louis Friant (4,055 men) took 1,396 casualties (34%), and its Chasseur Division under Charles-Étienne Morand (4,603 men) took 1,775 casualties (39%) — General Michel, the Chasseur Division's deputy commander, and Colonel Mallet of the 3rd Chasseurs were both killed in the final assault (`settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-chronology`). Total French strength is commonly given as 71,947–73,000 (Clodfelter, Bodart, and Hofschröer respectively) with 246–252 guns.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Order of battle of the Waterloo Campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_battle_of_the_Waterloo_Campaign, whose unit-level tables cite André Pigeard (2005), Herbert Siborne (1895), Oleg Sokolov (2003), and Philip Haythornthwaite — none of which this canon has opened directly, so the corps-level strengths above should be read as this canon's use of Wikipedia's own compiled table rather than independent verification against those named historians; "Battle of Waterloo," as above, infobox, whose total-strength range is drawn there from Gaston Bodart (1908), p. 487, Michael Clodfelter (2017), pp. 169–170, and Peter Hofschröer (1999), pp. 68–69, on the same basis.

## Order of battle: Anglo-Allied {#waterloo-oob-allied}

**Standing: established by research.** Wellington commanded a genuinely multinational force he privately called "an infamous army, very weak and ill-equipped, and a very inexperienced Staff" — British troops made up only 38% of it (27,985), against King's German Legion (10%, 7,686), Dutch-Belgian and Nassauer troops (28.3%, 21,035), Hanoverians (15.5%, 11,496), and Brunswickers (8.2%, 6,124); the modern historian Alessandro Barbero is quoted, via Wikipedia's own synthesis rather than this canon's direct reading of his book, noting that "in this heterogeneous army the difference between British and foreign troops did not prove significant under fire" — a reading Wellington's own despatch, read directly, corroborates in substance without the same analytic framing: "the army never, upon any occasion, conducted itself better." This canon's fixed headline figure at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-waterloo`, sourced to Britannica, gives Wellington's engaged strength as roughly 68,000; a more granular unit-roll source this file adds rather than substitutes gives present strength at Waterloo as 71,257 (3,866 officers, 65,919 other ranks) — this canon treats the difference as ordinary variance between a rounded headline figure and a detailed roll rather than a contradiction, and does not revise the fixed figure. A further 17,000 were held in reserve at Halle, thirteen kilometers west, against a possible flanking move and never engaged. I Corps under the Prince of Orange (25,403 men, fifty-two guns) included the same Lieutenant-Colonel James Macdonell who commanded Hougoumont's garrison; II Corps under Lord Hill (24,385 men, forty guns) included Adam's Brigade; the Cavalry Corps under the Earl of Uxbridge (15,395 men, thirty-five guns) included the Household Brigade under Lord Edward Somerset. Total guns across the army are commonly given at roughly 156–157. Lieutenant-General Sir Thomas Picton, commanding the 5th Division, was killed leading a bayonet counter-attack against d'Erlon's assault at roughly half past one in the afternoon, shot through the temple; he had concealed a serious hip wound taken two days earlier at Quatre-Bras, bandaging it himself and telling no surgeon, a fact discovered only at his post-mortem examination — Wellington called him the most senior officer to die at Waterloo. Wellington's Quartermaster-General, Colonel Sir William Howe De Lancey, was also mortally wounded. Colonel Christian von Ompteda of the King's German Legion was killed leading a single battalion in a doomed attempt to retake La Haye Sainte after it fell, his flank exposed to French cuirassiers who destroyed the battalion and took its colour; the order to attempt the recapture is attributed by one source to the Prince of Orange directly and by the fuller narrative to General Alten acting in the crisis, and this canon does not resolve which account is more authoritative. In the earlier cavalry fighting, the Union Brigade's initial charge broke d'Erlon's infantry columns and captured the Eagle of the 45th Line, but overextended into the French gun line and was caught by a counter-charge from Milhaud's cuirassiers and Jaquinot's lancers: the Royal Scots Greys alone lost 104 dead and ninety-seven wounded, with 228 of their 416 horses killed, reduced from three squadrons to two; the brigade commander, Major-General Sir William Ponsonby, was captured and then killed by the lancer who had taken him, who used the same lance afterward to kill three Scots Greys attempting his rescue.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," as above, citing Alessandro Barbero, *The Battle: A New History of Waterloo* (2005), a book this canon has not opened directly; "Order of battle of the Waterloo Campaign," as above; "Thomas Picton," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Picton; "Christian Friedrich Wilhelm von Ompteda," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Friedrich_Wilhelm_von_Ompteda; "Royal Scots Greys," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Scots_Greys; Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington, despatch to Earl Bathurst, as above, read directly, for "the army never, upon any occasion, conducted itself better."

## Order of battle: Prussian {#waterloo-oob-prussian}

**Standing: established by research, with the extent of documented tension between Gneisenau and Wellington narrower than one popular framing suggests.** Field Marshal Blücher held command, with August von Gneisenau as chief of staff, Karl von Grolman as Quartermaster-General, and Blücher's own aide-de-camp Nostitz, who had helped him after his fall at Ligny. Two and a half of the four Prussian corps engaged at Waterloo itself, some 48,000 men in total (a slightly higher, more granular figure than the round 45,000 this canon's chronology file cites): I Corps under Hans von Zieten (32,500 men, ninety-six guns), II Corps under Georg von Pirch (33,000 men, eighty-five guns, engaging only partially), and IV Corps under Friedrich von Bülow (32,000 men, 132 guns), which opened the Prussian attack on Lobau's screening force at Plancenoit around 16:30; Thielmann's III Corps was engaged separately, against Grouchy at Wavre (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-grouchy-detachment`), and is not counted among the Waterloo forces here. This canon does not adopt a specific narrative of Gneisenau's reluctance to march or personal distrust of Wellington beyond what is directly attested: Gneisenau's own standard characterization is of harmonious cooperation with Blücher, and this canon has found the stronger claim of a documented Gneisenau-authored delay traceable only to a historical novel rather than to a primary or secondary historical account it could verify — a downstream unit should not present a dramatized reluctance to march as established fact.

Zieten's approach produced the single most consequential near-miss of the day. Late in the afternoon his I Corps, arriving north of La Haie, grew concerned at the sight of retreating stragglers from Nassau units on Wellington's left and from the Prussian 15th Brigade, and, fearing his own troops would be caught in what looked like a general retreat, began moving away from Wellington's flank toward the main Prussian body near Plancenoit — a movement reinforced by a direct order from Blücher to support Bülow instead. General Karl von Müffling, the Prussian liaison officer at Wellington's own headquarters, rode to intercept Zieten and warned him: "The battle is lost if the corps does not keep on the move and immediately support the English army." Zieten reversed course and resumed his march to Wellington's flank, and his arrival let Wellington shift cavalry from his left to reinforce his crumbling center.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Order of battle of the Waterloo Campaign," as above; "Battle of Waterloo," as above, section "Zieten's flank march"; "August von Gneisenau," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_von_Gneisenau, which characterizes the Blücher–Gneisenau relationship as furnishing "one of the best examples of harmonious co-operation between a commander and his chief of staff" and identifies a scripted "deliberate delay" as belonging to Bernard Cornwell's novel *Waterloo* rather than to the historical record.

## Battle chronology {#waterloo-chronology}

**Standing: established by research for the sequence of events; several clock times are disputed between sources by thirty minutes to an hour, per `settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-terrain`.** Fighting opened at Hougoumont between roughly 10:00 and 11:30. Around 11:00, Napoleon issued his general order for Reille's and d'Erlon's corps to attack the village of Mont-Saint-Jean, based on a mistaken belief that Wellington's line stood in the village rather than on the ridge above it. The grand battery of I, II, and VI Corps reserve artillery opened on the Allied center between roughly noon and 13:30. A little after 13:00, d'Erlon's I Corps advanced in large infantry columns; British Household and Union Brigade cavalry counter-charged and broke them, the Scots Greys capturing the Eagle of the 45th Line before overextending into the disastrous counter-charge recorded above. At roughly 13:15, Napoleon spotted the first Prussian columns near Lasne-Chapelle-Saint-Lambert, some six to eight kilometers from his right flank — about a three-hour march for an army — and Soult sent word to Grouchy to turn and engage them, an order that reached Grouchy too late and too far from the field to matter (`settings/018-the-hundred-days-and-the-belgian-campaign.md#hundred-days-grouchy-detachment`). From roughly 16:00 to 18:30, Ney launched a series of massed, largely unsupported French cavalry charges against the Allied infantry squares; the charges could not break formed squares, though French cavalry repeatedly overran and were then driven off the Allied gun line as it reformed between waves. Bülow's Prussian IV Corps began its attack on Plancenoit at 16:30; Napoleon fed Young Guard battalions into its defense, and the village changed hands five times before the battle's end. La Haye Sainte fell to the French in the early evening as its King's German Legion garrison exhausted its ammunition, and Ompteda's death in the failed counter-attack to retake it followed almost immediately. Zieten's I Corps and part of Pirch's II Corps engaged at roughly 18:00, after the near-turn-back recorded at `settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-oob-prussian`; by 19:30 the French position had bent into a rough horseshoe anchored on Hougoumont, Plancenoit, and La Haie.

Around 19:30, Napoleon committed the Middle Guard — five or six battalions of Grenadiers and Chasseurs — against Wellington's center-right in the battle's final assault. Dutch General David Hendrik Chassé, on his own initiative, advanced a fresh division and opened with horse artillery into the Grenadiers' flank, later recording, "I saw the Garde Impériale advancing while the English troops were leaving the plateau en masse and moving in the direction of Waterloo; the battle seemed lost..."; his subordinate Detmers's brigade then broke the Grenadiers with a bayonet charge. Simultaneously, some 1,500 British Foot Guards under Peregrine Maitland, lying prone to shelter from artillery, rose and fired a point-blank volley into the advancing Chasseurs — "some 300 fell from the first volley, including Colonel Mallet and General Michel" — followed by a bayonet charge that broke the square; Colborne's 52nd Light Infantry then wheeled onto the flank of the last Guard battalion and broke it after a sharp firefight that cost the 52nd some 150 casualties of its own. As the news spread through the French army that the Guard itself was retreating, panic followed: "La Garde recule. Sauve qui peut!" The Guard's remaining reserve battalions made a last stand south of La Haye Sainte before being broken by Adam's Brigade and Vivian's and Vandeleur's cavalry. The famous exchange sometimes placed here — "La Garde meurt, elle ne se rend pas!" ("The Guard dies, it does not surrender!"), attributed to General Cambronne — is explicitly fictitious: Victor Hugo himself later noted Cambronne never said it and that the actual reply was a single obscenity, and some accounts hold Cambronne was already a prisoner when the exchange is supposed to have happened, meaning the retort, if made at all, may belong to General Michel rather than to Cambronne. As the Guard collapsed at Plancenoit as well, a Prussian regimental history records a different cry there, without panic: "Sauvons nos aigles!" ("Let's save our eagles!"); the French Young Guard division suffered 96% casualties in the fighting, and two-thirds of Lobau's corps ceased to exist. General Guillaume Philibert Duhesme, commanding the Young Guard at Plancenoit, was seriously wounded in the head during the position's fifth and final change of hands, captured, and died two days later, 20 June, at Genappe, reportedly with Blücher present at his death.

The last two cohesive Old Guard battalions, positioned at La Belle Alliance to protect Napoleon, were broken by Adam's Brigade on one side and the Prussians on the other before Napoleon left the field, persuaded the battle was lost. Ney's own later account explicitly disputes the "sauve qui peut" characterization of the collapse: "There remained to us still four squares of the Old Guard to protect the retreat. These brave grenadiers... yielded ground foot by foot, till, overwhelmed by numbers, they were almost entirely annihilated. From that moment, a retrograde movement was declared, and the army formed nothing but a confused mass. There was not, however, a total rout, nor the cry of sauve qui peut, as has been calumniously stated in the bulletin." Wellington and Blücher met near La Belle Alliance around 21:00, consistent with this canon's existing dating at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-waterloo`; Gneisenau's own account records: "it was there that, by happy chance, Field Marshal Blücher and Lord Wellington met in the dark, and mutually saluted each other as victors." Coalition cavalry pursuit continued until roughly 23:00, Gneisenau pursuing as far as Genappe, where Napoleon's abandoned carriage — containing an annotated copy of Machiavelli's *The Prince* and diamonds later added to the Prussian crown jewels — was captured.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," as above, for all quoted passages including Ney's own account of the final collapse, which this canon has not traced to Ney's original memoir or a scholarly edition of it and cites only as Wikipedia's own quotation of it; "Guillaume Philibert Duhesme," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillaume_Philibert_Duhesme; Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington, despatch to Earl Bathurst, as above, read directly, for Bülow's flanking movement and Blücher's personal arrival, corroborating the Wikipedia-sourced narrative independently: "the march of general Bulow's corps by Frischemont upon Plancenois and La Belle Alliance had begun to take effect," and "marshal prince Blucher had joined in person, with a corps of his army to the left of our line by Ohain."

## Napoleon's order of the day {#waterloo-proclamation}

**Standing: not yet decided that a battle-day proclamation exists — this canon has searched the collection it already relies on for the Austerlitz and Borodino proclamations and found none dated 18 June 1815.** No address specific to the day of Waterloo is attested in the sources this canon could reach. The Wikisource "Napoleon's Addresses" collection's last entry before the battle is dated 14 June 1815 — a Proclamation on the Anniversary of the Battles of Marengo and Friedland, issued at the campaign's opening, before the army crossed into Belgium — followed by an address "to the Belgians and the inhabitants on the left bank of the Rhine" dated 17 June, and then directly by the second-abdication proclamation of 22 June; there is no entry between the 17th and the 22nd. The 14 June proclamation's full text, for reference as the closest documented equivalent to a campaign-opening address, though it is not a Waterloo-day document: "Soldiers: This day is the anniversary of Marengo and Friedland, which twice decided the destiny of Europe... Soldiers: We have forced marches before us, battles to fight, and dangers to encounter; but firm in resolution, victory must be ours. The honor and happiness of our country are at stake! and, in short, Frenchmen, the moment is arrived when we must conquer or die!" A downstream unit dramatizing the eve or morning of Waterloo should not invent or repurpose a battle-day proclamation as documented speech; the 14 June address, four days earlier, is what the record actually offers.

Sources: Wikisource, "Napoleon's Addresses/Part V," https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_Addresses/Part_V, read directly in full to confirm the absence of any entry between 17 and 22 June 1815.

## Casualties {#waterloo-casualties}

**Standing: established by research for the headline figures already fixed at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-waterloo`; granular unit-level and named-officer figures established by research below, with one alternate historian's aggregate figure flagged as a genuinely distinct framing.** This file does not restate or revise the fixed headline totals — French 26,000–27,000, Wellington's forces 17,000, Blücher's forces 6,604–7,000 — but records the finer detail beneath them. Wikipedia's own narrative aftermath text gives a marginally different formulation from its infobox, worth noting as a real internal inconsistency reflecting genuine archival dispute rather than a single settled figure: "Napoleon's losses were 24,000 to 26,000 killed or wounded, including 6,000 to 7,000 captured, with an additional 15,000 deserting subsequent to the battle and over the following days." Thierry Lentz, director of the Fondation Napoléon, gives a distinctly different aggregate: "By 10 o'clock in the evening, it was all over; there were 10,800 dead (6,800 of which were French soldiers) and around 35,000 injured (of which two thirds were French), strewn across the 'bleak plain.'" This is a named historian's own tally, not a re-derivation of the more commonly cited figures, and this canon records it as a genuine alternate framing rather than reconciling it to the others. A separate unit-roll tally for Wellington's army gives 16,084 total casualties (3,024 killed, 10,222 wounded, 2,838 missing, a 24.6% loss rate) against the round headline figure of 17,000 — again presented as a more granular figure from the same broad sourcing tradition, not a contradiction.

Named officers killed or mortally wounded are recorded above at `settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-oob-allied` and `#waterloo-oob-prussian` (Picton, Ompteda, Ponsonby, Duhesme) and `#waterloo-chronology` (Michel, Mallet). Unit-level losses of particular note: the Scots Greys' 104 dead and ninety-seven wounded against 228 of 416 horses (`settings/019-waterloo.md#waterloo-oob-allied`); the Imperial Guard's Grenadier Division at 34% casualties and Chasseur Division at 39%; the Young Guard's 96% casualties at Plancenoit; and the Prussian 18th Regiment, which fought at both Frichermont and Plancenoit, alone recording 810 casualties out of Blücher's roughly 7,000 total losses and winning thirty-three Iron Crosses in the fighting.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," as above, infobox and "Aftermath" section — the infobox figures are drawn there from Gaston Bodart, *Militär-historisches Kriegs-Lexikon (1618–1905)* (1908), and Michael Clodfelter, *Warfare and Armed Conflicts* (2017), neither of which this canon has opened directly, and this entry's own figures should be read as Wikipedia's synthesis of those works rather than as this canon's independent verification of either; "Order of battle of the Waterloo Campaign," as above; Thierry Lentz, "Bullet Point #35," napoleon.org, read via `fetch.sh`, as above — the one figure in this entry this canon can attribute to a named historian it has actually read in full.

## Immediate aftermath {#waterloo-aftermath}

**Standing: established by research.** As dusk fell, the last two Old Guard squares withdrew in relatively good order even as the rest of the French army disintegrated around them, "surrounded by thousands of fleeing, broken French troops." Coalition cavalry harried the retreat until roughly 23:00, with Gneisenau pursuing as far as Genappe before halting; by this point seventy-eight guns and 2,000 prisoners, including several generals, had been taken. The two commanders agreed on the field that the less exhausted Prussian army would continue the pursuit toward Avesnes and Laon via Charleroi, while Wellington's army rested overnight before advancing separately toward Péronne — an unusually sustained, continued-after-dark pursuit that made the defeat far more destructive than an ordinary retreat, denying the French any chance to rally on Belgian or French soil and costing them most of their remaining artillery.

Napoleon's own escape route is precisely documented: fugitives reached the Sambre at Charleroi, Marchienne-au-Pont, and Châtelet by daybreak on 19 June, were disturbed there by Prussian cavalry pushed forward from Gosselies, and fled on toward Beaumont and Philippeville. From Philippeville, hoping to communicate more easily with the still-intact Grouchy, Napoleon spent four hours dispatching orders to Generals Rapp, Lecourbe, and Lamarque to force-march their corps to Paris and to fortress commandants to defend to the last extremity, before starting for Laon by post-horse at two in the afternoon on the 19th; he reached Paris at half past five in the morning on 21 June. Soult's main army retreated on Laon in confusion; Grouchy's still-cohesive force, cut off from the wreck of the main army, was compelled onto the road to Rethel and then Rheims to force a junction with Soult. The political collapse, distinct from and following the military one, crystallized in Paris over the following three days rather than at any single battlefield moment: Napoleon still hoped, writing to Joseph, to raise a fresh army and hold off invasion until Grouchy's force could reinforce him, and Lucien Bonaparte and Marshal Davout urged him to dissolve the Chamber of Deputies and rule by military dictatorship; instead the Chamber, on Lafayette's urging, voted itself permanent on 21 June, preempting any such move. On 22 June, under pressure from the Chamber of Representatives, Napoleon signed his second abdication in favor of his son, which the provisional government and the Coalition rejected — the act already fixed at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-waterloo`, which this file does not reopen. A final skirmish of the wars followed on 3 July, when General Dominique Vandamme was defeated by Blücher at Issy. A British officer present at Waterloo four days after the battle, Major W. E. Frye, recorded the field's state directly: "This morning I went to visit the field of battle... the sight was too horrible to behold. I felt sick in the stomach and was obliged to return. The multitude of carcasses, the heaps of wounded men with mangled limbs unable to move, and perishing from not having their wounds dressed or from hunger... formed a spectacle I shall never forget."

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," as above; "Waterloo campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign, "Invasion of France" section.
