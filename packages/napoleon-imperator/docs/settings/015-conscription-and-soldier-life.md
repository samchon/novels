**Status:** Source-supported for mass mobilization and conscription; local exemptions and resistance require departmental records.

The Jourdan-Delbrel law was voted on 5 September 1798 (19 Fructidor an VI) after a 12 January report, and the recruitment history describes its practical stages: tables, contingent designation, medical revision, allocation to corps, and final lists. Later measures created reserve and replacement channels, recruitment councils, and the 30 December 1803 return of drawing lots. An army is therefore made from registers, age classes, medical inspection, exemptions, substitutes, transport, and pay. “The French” hides departmental, linguistic, colonial, and allied differences. A recruit arrives with village, family, trade, and legal status; numbering and training do not erase those ties.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “La conscription sous le Premier Empire,” Jourdan law, recruitment stages, replacement, exemptions, https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/la-conscription-sous-le-premier-empire/; Fondation Napoléon, “Du village à la caserne,” departmental list and recruitment path, https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/du-village-a-la-caserne-les-etapes-de-la-conscription-sous-le-consulat-et-lempire/.

**Status:** Work canon. Drill creates usable formations but consumes time, instructors, shoes, ammunition, and bodies.

Discipline is enforced through officers, inspections, punishment, comradeship, pay, food, equipment, and shared identity. Davout's training account describes repeated manoeuvres, long marches in all weather, healthy food, camp comfort, and complete equipment as deliberate preparation rather than natural courage; his provision of shoes and sabots also shows the material cost of readiness. A battalion that marches well may still be inexperienced under artillery fire. The Grande Armée equipment account emphasizes shoes, a heavy pack, weapons, cartridges, food, and requisition. The manuscript must show fatigue, fear, desertion risk, and the work required to keep ranks closed.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Conscription converts age, residence, health, family status, exemption, and substitution into administrative records.

The recruitment history describes the municipal list, departmental distribution by prefect and sub-prefect, medical revision, recruitment council, and assignment to a regiment. The law's exemptions included maritime workers, some married men, first-levy western departments, and medical incapacity; a replacement system allowed those with money to pay another man, while the critical history records protection affecting later careers. The register is therefore a route from village to regiment, not a neutral national mirror. **Status:** Work canon. Foreign contingents have distinct oaths, pay, law, religion, language, and political loyalty.

The Grande Armée included foreign regiments—Polish, Dutch, Italian, Spanish, Bavarian, and Württemberg formations—alongside French forces. An allied regiment can fight beside France without becoming French: oath, pay, legal status, religion, language, officers, and political purpose differ. Orders may require interpreters or bilingual officers; food and pay disputes can become discipline problems; a retreat can expose obligations to a king, a treaty, or a homeland rather than to Napoleon alone. The novel gives these troops names, institutions, and agency rather than treating them as anonymous numbers, while campaign owners specify the legal relation for each contingent.
