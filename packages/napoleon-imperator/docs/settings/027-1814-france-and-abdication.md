**Status:** Source-supported. Napoleon's rapid local concentration operated inside a state short of trained men, secure roads, and political time.

The 1814 chronology records Napoleon leaving Paris on 25 January, the 1 February La Rothière defeat against a much larger allied force, and the February sequence at Champaubert, Montmirail, and Vauchamps after the coalition armies separated. It later records Arcis-sur-Aube on 20–21 March, where 27,000 French faced about 100,000 allies, and notes that the coalition then chose to march on Paris rather than chase every imperial movement. These entries support a material rule: a mobile army may win a local action while losing the road network, recruits, bridges, depots, and political tempo required to protect the state. The setting may show maneuver, snow, fatigue, ammunition, and courier work, but a tactical victory never restores 1812 resources by declaration.

**Status:** Source-critical. An advancing coalition changes authority through roads, supplies, and visible administration before every legal question is settled.

The chronology records Beresford entering Bordeaux on 12 March and being welcomed by a population already favorable to the Bourbons, while Austrian forces entered Lyon on 22 March after Augereau's retreat. Paris faced a different military and political emergency: Moncey and volunteers defended against a far larger coalition force on 30 March, and the capitulation followed at 2 a.m. on 31 March. These are not one “French civilian reaction.” A town may welcome a column, bargain over billets, flee, or keep officials working under pressure; a temporary passage differs from a garrison and both differ from a new government. Downstream scenes must identify who controls the gate, who signs the requisition, which language is used, and how food, horses, records, and wounded cross the occupied road.

**Status:** Source-supported and source-critical. Diplomacy narrows through military pressure but does not speak with one French voice.

The coalition's 4 December Declaration of Frankfurt stated that its war was against Napoleon rather than France, while the February Châtillon negotiations began under a different practical balance. The chronology says the Allies demanded a return to France's 1792 frontiers rather than the earlier “natural frontiers,” that Caulaincourt held negotiating authority, and that talks were interrupted amid coalition disagreement and battlefield change. The 1–9 March Treaty of Chaumont then bound Austria, Prussia, Russia, and Britain to continue war and avoid separate peace. These documents and meetings have different audiences and force. A minister's report, Senate act, emperor's instruction, and public charter cannot be merged into “France decided.” Later scenes must identify the sender, recipient, legal authority, and route by which a term becomes known.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. The army's local obedience, Paris's legal action, and the dynasty's survival diverged before the abdication document resolved any of them.

On 28 March the regency council met at the Tuileries around Marie-Louise and Joseph; on 30 March Paris was defended by Moncey and volunteers against the coalition; the capitulation was signed at 2 a.m. on 31 March. The chronology says Napoleon learned of the capital's fall roughly twenty kilometres away, turned toward Fontainebleau, and that the Senate moved to depose him on 1 April. **Status:** Primary-text anchored and source-critical. Abdication is a sequence of drafts, transmission, treaty, and ratification rather than battlefield surrender alone.

The official act page identifies the instrument as done at Fontainebleau on 11 April and states that Napoleon declares readiness to descend from the throne for the fatherland while mentioning his son's rights and the empress's regency. The campaign chronology describes a short text written in the night of 5–6 April, delivered to Caulaincourt, and withheld pending a treaty establishing the rules of abdication; the treaty was signed on 11 April. Lentz's documentary outline also lists a conditional draft dated 4 April and a draft signed on 6 April but postdated to 11 April. These layers support a legal and political rupture without requiring a single motive. The novel must distinguish draft, decision, public act, plenipotentiary signature, and ratification.

**Status:** Primary-text anchored work boundary. The son and empress appear in the legal instruments, but legal language does not by itself prove practical custody or political survival.

Napoleon's official act invokes the rights of his son and the regency of the Empress. The Fontainebleau treaty preserves Napoleon and Marie-Louise's titles in Article II, gives Marie-Louise Parma, Placentia, and Guastalla in Article V, and assigns those territories to her son and his direct descendants. Those clauses are not equivalent to a continuing French imperial succession: Article I renounces sovereignty for Napoleon, his successors, descendants, and family members. The setting therefore gives household agents distinct risks—access to the child, safe conduct, papers, servants, pensions, and foreign supervision—and hands actual post-abdication custody and restoration politics to later owners. A dynastic claim may remain in correspondence while losing the army and institutions able to enforce it.

**Status:** Primary-text anchored and source-critical. Regime change is material and documentary; later public memory can reorganize, but not replace, those records.

The Fontainebleau treaty reserves family revenues in Article VI, protects private family property, reduces Josephine's income in Article VII, returns Napoleon's estates to the crown in Article IX, and addresses jewels, civil-list effects, household debts, and Milanese obligations in Articles X–XIII. These clauses create concrete choices for officials and families: swear, wait, conceal papers, claim private ownership, or seek a commissioner. They do not tell us how every person reacted. The Fondation's account of the 20 April farewell explicitly distinguishes the historical event from later Vernet and Épinal representations, whose composition, color, and symbolism shaped public memory. Settings therefore date each oath, seizure, payment, and image and leave retrospective legend to its own evidence owner.
