## Defending France with a shrinking state {#defending-france-with-a-shrinking-state}

**Status:** Source-supported. Napoleon's military skill operates amid exhausted resources, coalition invasion, conscription fatigue, and threatened administration.

Local defense, fortresses, roads, Parisian politics, and army movement are coupled. A battlefield victory can fail to restore the national position if the capital, allies, or supplies are lost. The novel must show scale and scarcity together: a small force may move quickly while a larger one is tied to depots, wounded, guns, and political instructions.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Allied invasion and civilian administration {#allied-invasion-and-civilian-administration}

**Status:** Source-required domain. Coalition advance changes roads, requisition, property, courts, and local authority before a final peace exists.

An occupying column needs food, guides, billets, interpreters, guards, and records. Civilians may negotiate, flee, profit, resist, or simply try to keep a household alive. The setting must identify which army controls a town, which official signs a demand, and whether a report describes military occupation or a temporary passage.

Sources: Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/; Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/.

## Diplomacy at the edge of Paris {#diplomacy-at-the-edge-of-paris}

**Status:** Work canon. Negotiation, military movement, public opinion, and the Senate interact; no single chamber speaks for France.

Officials receive contradictory reports and weigh survival, legitimacy, property, and foreign terms. The emperor's orders may be militarily sound but politically impossible. Scenes must identify the audience and coercive power behind every declaration, and must keep a private fear, a ministerial memorandum, and a public address as different evidence.

Sources: Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/; Fondation Napoléon correspondence overview, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.

## Army command and political fracture {#army-command-and-political-fracture}

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. A field army's obedience, a capital's legality, and a dynasty's survival can diverge before any document settles the conflict.

Couriers and ministers carry incompatible time scales. A commander may win a local action while lacking food or reinforcements; a politician may declare continuity while negotiating a succession. The settings layer must not give Napoleon knowledge of Parisian decisions before the route and receipt of information are established.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Fondation Napoléon correspondence overview, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.

## Abdication as document and rupture {#abdication-as-document-and-rupture}

**Status:** Source-supported. Abdication ends one legal claim while leaving family, army, property, and succession disputes unresolved.

The act's wording, date, audience, and constitutional interpretation matter. It is not the same as battlefield surrender or exile. The settings graph must connect it to the provisional government and allied occupation, while preserving disagreement over whether a document is a voluntary act, a forced concession, or both under different legal readings.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/.

## Family succession and the king of Rome {#family-succession-and-the-king-of-rome}

**Status:** Work boundary. Family security, succession claims, property, and the status of Napoleon's son require dedicated political and household owners.

Abdication changes the risks faced by the empress, child, relatives, officers, and servants. A dynastic claim can survive in correspondence while losing institutional protection. Later narrative must identify which government, household, or foreign court controls access and papers rather than treating family separation as a private mood alone.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon biography, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/; Fondation Napoléon correspondence overview, https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.

## Property legitimacy and public memory {#property-legitimacy-and-public-memory}

**Status:** Source-required domain. Political transition is material: offices, pensions, titles, confiscations, and oaths determine who can remain secure.

Officials and families must decide whether to swear, wait, conceal papers, or change allegiance. A later regime's legal settlement may call an earlier act treason, service, or necessity. Settings must preserve the date and authority of each oath or confiscation and hand retrospective memory to its own setting rather than using it as contemporary knowledge.

Sources: Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Boundary to Elba {#boundary-to-elba}

**Status:** Work boundary. Treaty arrangements, travel, guards, and island administration belong to the Elba owner.

Abdication creates the legal and political condition for exile but does not determine the journey's exact escort, communications, or local reception. Those facts must be sourced separately. The transition carries forward custody, family, property, and legitimacy questions without pretending that a signature resolves them.

Sources: Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte/; Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/.
