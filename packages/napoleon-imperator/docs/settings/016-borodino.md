# Borodino

This file records the terrain, order of battle, chronology, and aftermath of the Battle of Borodino (7 September 1812) at the standard `settings/012-austerlitz.md` sets, so that a storyline reaching 1812 needs to invent nothing of the battle's setting or mechanics, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`. Campaign lead-up (the advance, Smolensk, the push to Moscow) is at `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md`; the occupation, fire, and retreat that follow are at the same file. Figure-level detail for commanders is at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md` and `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md`.

## Terrain {#borodino-terrain}

**Standing: established by research, with the distance from Moscow disputed between sources and the day's weather not yet decided for the reason stated below.** The battlefield lies near the village of Borodino on the old Smolensk road, roughly 110–125 km west of Moscow — Britannica gives about 70 miles (110 km), French-language sources give 125 km, and this canon states the range rather than a single figure; Mikaberidze's own account separately places Kutuzov's headquarters near Mozhaisk, just east of the field, at "thirteen versts" (roughly 14 km) from the fighting. The Russian position ran along high ground south of the new Smolensk highway, anchored on the Moskva river at the right flank and using the Kolocha river's steep banks as a natural obstacle across the center-right; the ground was, in Ségur's own description of the approach to the Great Redoubt, "steep heights, encompassed by deep and muddy ravines." The center and left offered too little natural cover, which is why the Russians built the field's two defining artificial works. The Bagration flèches were three arrow-shaped, open-backed earthworks south of the village of Semyonovskaya, initially held by the 11th and 32nd Russian Battery Companies with a battalion of the 2nd Combined Grenadier Division at each work. The Raevsky Redoubt (the Great Redoubt) was a similarly open-backed earthwork on high ground center-right, mounting eighteen to nineteen twelve-pounder guns. The southernmost anchor, at Utitsa and the Utitsa Mound, sat on the old Smolensk road — a distinct road from the new highway further north — and was held by Tuchkov's 3rd Corps against Poniatowski's Polish V Corps. Two days before the main battle, on 5 September, the Shevardino Redoubt — a pentagonal earthwork meant to delay the French advance and screen the Russian left while the main position was fortified — was stormed by Murat's cavalry (Nansouty's and Montbrun's corps) and Compans's division of Davout's I Corps, with Poniatowski's infantry attacking from the south, against Prince Andrei Gorchakov's troops of Bagration's army; French losses ran roughly 4,000 against Russian losses of roughly 6,000 across the two days' fighting, with three Russian guns captured. The fall of Shevardino threw the Russian left into disarray and forced a hastily improvised position near Utitsa — the vulnerability Poniatowski's flanking attack would exploit two days later. This canon has not been able to source the day's weather from any academic account it has read: no rain is reported anywhere, and "clear, cold dawn" is the characterization that recurs in popular narrative (including Tolstoy's literary treatment), but this canon treats it as **not yet decided**, the record it has consulted not settling the matter, rather than adopting the popular characterization as fact.

## Order of battle: French {#borodino-oob-french}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon held supreme command. I Corps under Marshal Louis-Nicolas Davout (wounded in the fighting), roughly 35,000–37,000 with 156 guns, anchored the assault on the flèches together with Ney's III Corps (roughly 12,000–15,000, reduced by earlier losses; 75 guns). IV Corps under Prince Eugène de Beauharnais, Viceroy of Italy (roughly 24,000, 88 guns), and V Corps under Prince Józef Poniatowski (roughly 10,000, 50 guns) held the center-left and the Utitsa flank respectively; VIII Corps under Andoche Junot (roughly 8,900, 30 guns) and Murat's Cavalry Reserve — subordinate commanders Nansouty, Montbrun (killed in the action, see below), Grouchy (wounded), and Latour-Maubourg — completed the field army. The Imperial Guard (18,500–19,000, 109 guns, under Mortier, Lefebvre, and Bessières for the Guard cavalry) was never committed to the main assault, the decision recorded at `settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-guard-controversy`. Total French strength is commonly given at roughly 130,000–135,000 with about 587 guns — Mikaberidze's own figure is "some 135,000 men with 587 guns"; Britannica gives 130,000 with over 500 guns; a French-language figure of 124,000 excludes the Guard's 18,500 and nets to the same total once added back. At the flèches, Davout's divisions under Compans (wounded) and Dessaix (wounded), together with Friant's division, bore the fighting; Napoleon's aide-de-camp Jean Rapp, replacing the wounded Compans, took his twenty-second career wound leading a bayonet charge (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#general-rapp`). At the Great Redoubt, Broussier's, Morand's, and Gérard's infantry divisions and the Italian Guard under Eugène pressed the assault, supported by Chastel's light cavalry and the II Reserve Cavalry Corps; General Bonnamy led an earlier, unsupported assault by roughly 1,800 men of the 30th Line around ten in the morning that briefly penetrated the redoubt before being thrown back for lack of support, Bonnamy himself wounded twenty times and captured.

Sources: Alexander Mikaberidze, "French Order of Battle at Borodino," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles/1812/Russia/Borodino/Mikaberidze/OrdersofBattle/c_MikaberidzeOOB2.html, read directly; Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow*, as above, read directly ("Arriving at Borodino on 5 September, Napoleon fielded some 135,000 men with 587 guns"); Britannica, "Battle of Borodino," as above; Ségur, vol. 1, Book VII, chs. VIII–XI, as above, read directly, for the flèches and Great Redoubt fighting and Rapp's, Bonnamy's, and Compans's and Dessaix's wounds.

## Order of battle: Russian {#borodino-oob-russian}

**Standing: established by research, with total strength disputed between sources for a stated reason.** General Mikhail Kutuzov held supreme command, with General Levin Bennigsen as acting chief of staff. Barclay de Tolly's First Western Army (82,400 excluding militia, 438 guns) held the center and right, its corps commanded by Baggovut, Tuchkov I (mortally wounded, see below), Osterman-Tolstoy (wounded), and Dokhturov, who assumed command of the shattered center after Bagration's wound; Nikolay Raevsky commanded the Great Redoubt's defense, and Alexander Kutaisov, chief of artillery, was killed, his death contributing to a large reserve of unused guns on the day. Pyotr Bagration's Second Western Army (41,000 excluding militia, 186 guns) defended the flèches directly and Bagration himself was mortally wounded there (see below). Matvei Platov's Cossacks and Fyodor Uvarov's 1st Cavalry Corps conducted a raid on the French rear (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-chronology`). Total strength genuinely diverges across sources this canon does not silently reconcile: the regulars-only figures above sum to 123,400 men and 624 guns, directly confirming the commonly cited 624-gun total; Mikaberidze's own narrative total in a different work gives "some 155,000 troops, of whom 115,000 were regulars, supported by 636 guns," implying roughly 40,000 additional militia and Cossacks; Wikipedia's synthesis gives 125,000–160,000, with an engaged strength of roughly 122,200 and 43,000 irregulars (10,000 Cossacks, 33,000 militia) largely uncommitted, and 637 guns total with 300 held in reserve; Britannica gives a rounder 120,000 with over 600 guns. This canon states the range: roughly 120,000–155,000 depending on whether Cossacks and militia are counted, with core regular field strength consistently 115,000–123,000 and guns consistently 624–637.

Around 7:30 in the morning, Cossacks discovered a ford across the Kolocha on the extreme Russian right; Uvarov's and Platov's cavalry (roughly 8,000 with twelve guns) crossed and struck the rear of Eugène's IV Corps near its baggage train, alarming Napoleon's own headquarters. The raid forced Eugène to cancel a renewed assault on the Great Redoubt and pull back for roughly two hours — a disruption Wikipedia's synthesis credits with contributing to Napoleon's later refusal to commit the Guard, even though Kutuzov's own staff judged the raid a tactical failure at the time; Ségur's independent, contemporaneous account confirms the disruption from the French side, describing "Ouwarof, with two regiments of cavalry, and some thousand cossacks," throwing Eugène's reserve into disorder and forcing him to personally intervene before resuming the assault.

Sources: Alexander Mikaberidze, "Russian Order of Battle at Borodino," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles/1812/Russia/Borodino/Mikaberidze/OrdersofBattle/c_MikaberidzeOOB1.html, read directly; Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow*, as above, read directly; Wikipedia, "Battle of Borodino," as above; Britannica, "Battle of Borodino," as above; Ségur, vol. 1, as above, read directly.

## Battle chronology {#borodino-chronology}

**Standing: established by research for the sequence of events; the clock times are a working reconstruction disputed between sources by thirty minutes to an hour at several points.** Around 5:30 a.m., Napoleon waited near the captured Shevardino Redoubt for first light and Poniatowski's opening fire. At roughly 6:00 the sun rose and Napoleon reportedly exclaimed, "Behold the sun of Austerlitz!" — but Ségur, present, immediately notes the irony rather than the omen this canon already records for Austerlitz itself (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-weather`): "it was opposite to us. It rose on the Russian side, made us conspicuous to their fire, and dazzled us." The roughly 102-gun French grand battery opened on the Russian center at the same hour. Around 7:00, Eugène's 106th Regiment seized the village of Borodino and its bridge on the French left, then was thrown back with heavy loss after overextending across the Kolocha. From roughly 7:00–7:30, Davout's corps attacked the southernmost flèche, both Compans and Dessaix wounded and Rapp wounded replacing Compans; this is also the setting for the first of the Guard-controversy exchanges recorded below. The Platov/Uvarov raid began around 7:30. Through 8:00–9:00 the flèches were repeatedly stormed — a widely repeated figure holds they changed hands as many as eight times, though this canon has not independently verified that count against the underlying scholarship it cites at second hand and flags it as uncertain rather than settled. Around 10:00, per Ségur's explicit "It was yet only ten o'clock," Bonnamy's unsupported assault briefly took the Great Redoubt before being thrown back by a Russian counterattack under Kutaisov and Yermolov. Around 11:00, Bagration was struck by shell or cannonball splinters in the leg — this canon's sources disagree on whether this fell closer to eleven or closer to noon — and the French right finally broke the Second Army's line at the flèches; command of the shattered center passed to Dokhturov. By midday the flèches had fallen for the last time and the Platov/Uvarov raid was called off. Around 14:00, a renewed massed assault (Broussier's, Morand's, and Gérard's divisions with Chastel's cavalry and the II Reserve Cavalry Corps) struck the Great Redoubt again.

At "about half-past three o'clock," per Ségur's direct account, the Great Redoubt finally fell to a combined cavalry assault. Auguste-Jean-Gabriel de Caulaincourt — the younger brother of Armand de Caulaincourt, this canon's sustained secondary-viewpoint figure from 1812 onward (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#focal-figures`; `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#caulaincourt`) — had just taken command of the II Reserve Cavalry Corps after its commander, Louis-Pierre Montbrun, was killed; Ségur records him telling Montbrun's grieving aides, "Follow me, weep not for him, but come and avenge his death!" Ordered by Murat to break through and wheel his cuirassiers into the redoubt's open rear, Auguste reportedly answered, "You shall see me there presently, alive or dead." He led the assault, broke through, wheeled into the rear of the position, and was killed entering it — Ségur's wording is "struck dead by a musket-ball. His conquest was his tomb," while a French-language account gives cannon fire rather than a musket ball as the specific cause, a minor discrepancy this canon states rather than resolves. Thielmann's Saxon and Polish cuirassier squadrons are separately credited with forcing the redoubt's embrasures in the same assault; its Russian defender, General Likhachyov, was captured. When news reached headquarters, Napoleon asked Armand de Caulaincourt — present at his side — whether he wished to retire; per Ségur, Armand "made no reply; he did not retire; he only half uncovered himself to thank the emperor, and to refuse." Napoleon is separately quoted, via the family's own account, saying of Auguste: "il est mort comme un brave, en décidant la bataille" ("he died as a brave man, deciding the battle").

Between roughly 15:00 and 16:00, Russian Guard cavalry counter-charged the captured redoubt and was repelled; Grouchy was wounded in the fighting that secured the position. From 16:00 to 18:00, Russian Guard infantry squares held under sustained artillery fire, suffering heavy losses without breaking, and the fighting gradually died down as ammunition ran low on both sides; "It was getting late, our ammunition was exhausted, and the battle ended," per Ségur. Napoleon withheld the Guard throughout, and no French pursuit followed; the Russian army withdrew largely intact in the night. Bagration was evacuated to the village of Sima in Vladimir Governorate and died there on 24 September 1812 — one account records that on learning the army was abandoning Moscow he "rapidly stood up, totally forgetting about his grave wound," worsening his condition and hastening his death. Tuchkov I, mortally wounded leading the Pavlovsky Grenadiers in a counterattack at Utitsa, died considerably later, on 30 October 1812 — a different interval from Bagration's roughly two and a half weeks, which this canon does not conflate with it.

## The Guard controversy {#borodino-guard-controversy}

**Standing: disputed between sources as to the exact wording of each exchange, and the historians' judgment on whether withholding the Guard was a blunder or a justified caution is itself unresolved in the scholarship this canon could reach.** Napoleon refused, twice, to commit the Imperial Guard. The first refusal came in the morning, when Rapp's aide, asked what the flèches fighting required, answered that it would need the Guard to finish; Napoleon's reply, per Ségur: "No!... I shall take good care of that; I have no wish to see it destroyed; I shall gain the battle without it." The second, and better attested, came in the afternoon after the flèches had fallen, when General Belliard (Murat's chief of staff) twice rode to Napoleon urging the Young Guard forward, reporting the Russian rear visible in "a confused crowd of flying and wounded soldiers, and carriages retreating." Bessières, sent to check, reported the opposite — the Russians "far from being in disorder... had retreated to a second position... preparing for a fresh attack" — and Wikipedia's synthesis independently confirms Bessières as "one of the very few senior generals to strongly advise against the intervention of the Guard." Napoleon told Belliard that "nothing was yet sufficiently unravelled" and that he wanted "to see more clearly upon his chess-board" before committing his reserve. Ney, furious, reportedly said: "Are we then come so far, to be satisfied with a field of battle?... Since he will no longer make war himself, since he is no longer the general, as he wishes to be the emperor every where, let him return to the Tuilleries, and leave us to be generals for him!" When Daru, urged by Dumas and Berthier, told Napoleon in a low voice that "from all sides it was the cry that the moment for sending the guard was now come," Napoleon answered with the line this canon treats as the clearest statement of his actual reasoning: "And if there should be another battle to-morrow, where is my army?" Historians' judgment on the decision is genuinely split, and this canon flags a specific gap rather than inventing a resolution: the withholding is "widely criticized... as a large blunder, as it allowed the Russian army to retreat into territory increasingly hostile to the French," but this canon has not located, in the sources it could reach in this research pass, a named modern historian's explicit defense of the decision as correctly cautious given the distance from any possible reinforcement — the strongest available defense remains Napoleon's own reported reasoning above, not a scholarly verdict.

Sources: Ségur, vol. 1, Book VII, chs. IX–XI, as above, read directly; Wikipedia, "Battle of Borodino," as above. ## Casualties {#borodino-casualties}

**Standing: disputed between sources across a wide range, with Napoleon's own official figure treated as a documented Bulletin-scale undercount rather than a credible floor.** This was the single bloodiest day of the Napoleonic Wars, and this canon states the range rather than one number. Mikaberidze's own considered figure, from *The Burning of Moscow*: "the Grande Armée lost some 35,000 men, including forty-nine generals, while Russian losses exceeded 45,000 men, including twenty-nine generals." Wikipedia's wider synthesis gives French losses of 30,000–42,000 (with fifty generals and marshals killed or wounded) and Russian losses of 40,000–53,000 (with twenty-nine generals killed or wounded, matching Mikaberidze's own figure exactly and suggesting shared underlying sourcing); Britannica gives the more traditional, lower French ~30,000 and Russian ~45,000. Combined totals across these figures run from roughly 70,000 at the low end to roughly 95,000 at the high end; this canon has found no source supporting a combined figure above that range and treats any claim of 100,000 or more as unconfirmed. Napoleon's own 18th Bulletin gave a far lower French figure — roughly 6,500 killed and 21,500 wounded, some 28,000 total — while "proudly proclaim[ing] the French triumph on the field of Borodino" and stating "the victory was never uncertain," per Mikaberidze's direct account; this canon treats that figure as a documented instance of the same Bulletin-propaganda pattern already established at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`, not as a credible floor for the true total. Named officers killed or mortally wounded: on the Russian side, Bagration (died of wounds 24 September), Tuchkov I (died 30 October), and Kutaisov (chief of artillery, body never recovered); Likhachyov was captured, not killed. On the French side, Montbrun (II Reserve Cavalry Corps commander) and Auguste de Caulaincourt (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-chronology`) were both killed at the Great Redoubt, one of thirteen French generals killed or mortally wounded in the battle; Bonnamy was wounded twenty times and captured at the same position. Davout, Compans, Dessaix, Rapp, and Grouchy were all wounded.

## Napoleon's proclamation {#borodino-proclamation}

**Standing: established by research, wording treated as a directly sourced primary text rather than paraphrase.** Napoleon deliberately withheld his proclamation to the army overnight, per Ségur, ordering that it "should not be read to his troops until the next morning, and then only in case of the certainty of a battle" — it was therefore read at dawn on 7 September itself, not the evening before. Its text, from the same public-domain 1897 collection this canon already cites for the Austerlitz proclamation (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-proclamation`): "Soldiers: This is the battle you have so much desired. The victory depends upon you! It is now necessary to us. It will give us abundance of good winter quarters, and a prompt return to our country. Behave as at Austerlitz, at Friedland, at Witepsk, at Smolensk, and let the latest posterity recount with pride your conduct on this day; let them say of you, 'He was at the battle under the walls of Moscow.'" This canon has not independently re-verified the French original wording against a live source in this research pass and treats the English text above as the reliably sourced form available for direct use, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#language-conventions`.

Sources: Ida M. Tarbell (ed.), *Napoleon's Addresses: Selections from the Proclamations, Speeches and Correspondence of Napoleon Bonaparte* (1897), archive.org id `napoleonsaddress00napo`, p. 122, downloaded and read directly; Ségur, vol. 1, as above, read directly, for the deliberate overnight withholding.

## Immediate aftermath {#borodino-aftermath}

**Standing: established by research.** Kutuzov drafted a dispatch claiming victory before the fighting had even fully ended. When his report reached St. Petersburg on the night of 10–11 September, Alexander — per Mikaberidze's direct account — "certainly saw through Kutuzov's claims of victory, since he ordered the report to be edited and any reference to the Russian withdrawal excluded" before its public release, a documented instance of top-down editing of a battle report for public consumption that this canon treats as structurally parallel to the propaganda pattern already recorded for the French Bulletins (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-satschan-pond`; `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`). The edited report produced genuine public celebration on both a false and an ambiguous basis: the courier reached St. Petersburg on the Tsar's saint's day, the imperial family learned of it at mass, and contemporary eyewitnesses (Joseph de Maistre, the American envoy John Quincy Adams, the English traveler Ker Porter) record an illuminated capital and a sung Te Deum, with rumor snowballing into further embellishment — one version claimed the French had run entirely out of ammunition and were reduced to throwing stones. Alexander promoted Kutuzov to field marshal general, granted five roubles to every participating soldier, and distributed promotions and decorations to dozens of generals — all before the truth of the withdrawal, and Moscow's coming fall, became undeniable. The actual retreat order, given the night of 7–8 September despite the victory framing, provoked "a torrent of the most bitter invective" from Barclay de Tolly on being woken with it, who blamed Bennigsen as its principal author; the army marched in two columns on 8 September, the left wing retracing the old Smolensk road past Utitsa.

On the French side, Mikaberidze's direct account records that "Davout and Ney's corps were devastated by losses, while the cavalry had suffered tremendously as well," and with over 90,000 artillery rounds and roughly a million cartridges fired, Napoleon was anxious to replenish his ammunition before pressing on. Despite the tactical win, "a gloomy silence reigned" in the French bivouacs; Georges Chambray, quoted via Mikaberidze, records that "few battles won had produced such an extraordinary effect on the winners; they seemed to be stupefied." Napoleon himself was ill — a sore throat, a dry cough, and what his physicians treated as a probable early cold or bladder ailment, per Ségur's separate account — and remained idle at the battlefield until noon on 8 September before beginning the pursuit, a further delay layered onto the Guard's non-commitment (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-guard-controversy`). By 12–13 September, a new defensive position near Poklonnaya Gora, examined by Bennigsen, Toll, Michaud, and Crossard, was judged unsuitable, and the council of war at Fili on 13 September split roughly five to four in favor of giving battle for the city; Kutuzov overrode the majority and ordered Moscow abandoned without further fighting, reasoning, per his own words as rendered by Mikaberidze: "if we manage to hold our ground, we would suffer losses similar to those at Borodino and thus would be unable to defend a city as extensive as Moscow... having preserved our army, the hopes of our Fatherland would persevere, and the war, our only means to salvation, would be continued on better terms." The road to Moscow lay open; the occupation that followed is recorded at `settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-moscow-occupation`.

Sources: Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow*, as above, read directly, for all quoted passages in this entry except the Ségur material; Ségur, vol. 1, as above, read directly, for Napoleon's illness.
