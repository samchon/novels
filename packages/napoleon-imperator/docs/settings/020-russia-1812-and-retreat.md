**Status:** Source-supported. The 1812 army contains French and allied contingents with different languages, supplies, loyalties, and commanders.

Numbers conceal unequal readiness. A regiment may be present but understrength; a cavalry division may lack remounts; an allied contingent may interpret orders through its own government. Every strength claim needs a date and definition. A nominal order of battle is not the same as the men who can march, fight, feed themselves, and answer a French staff officer on a given day.

**Status:** Work canon. Russian roads, distances, heat, autumn rain, winter cold, forage failure, and disease transform operational strength.

An army can win a battle and lose the campaign through hunger, exhaustion, sickness, and horse death. Abandoned baggage and guns are consequences of transport failure as much as enemy action. The manuscript must show villages, wells, grain, clothing, shelter, and the road surface, while distinguishing what a commander knows from what later historians infer.

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Occupying Moscow does not compel Russian negotiation; the emperor's expectation and Russian policy must be separated.

The city, fire, evacuation, supplies, and diplomatic silence produce an information crisis. Napoleon may infer that symbolic possession will lead to talks, while Russian authorities choose endurance. Scenes must preserve the absence of the expected reply and must not convert a later memoir's confident explanation into knowledge available at the time.

**Status:** Source-required domain. The invasion's multinational scale makes command a problem of translation, liaison, precedence, and incompatible political aims.

An order passing from imperial headquarters to a French corps, a Polish cavalry commander, an Austrian contingent, or a Prussian auxiliary is not a single act. It is written, carried, interpreted, acknowledged, and converted into marches. Settings must name the sender, recipient, language, route, and receipt state where known. A map can show a common axis while units have different food, remount, or national reporting systems. The army's nominal total therefore cannot stand in for a synchronized instrument.

**Status:** Work canon. Operational distance is governed by roads, bridges, wagons, wells, grain, and horse condition rather than by map scale alone.

Columns consume the country along their route, and the country may not contain enough to replace what is taken. A damaged bridge delays guns and ambulances differently from infantry; a flooded track converts baggage into a blockage; a lost horse removes mobility and draught power. Later scenes must identify the road surface, crossing method, convoy order, repair labor, and the local population's choices. “The army advanced” is incomplete unless the setting states what reached the next halt and what was abandoned.

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Moscow's occupation, evacuation, fire, and diplomatic silence must remain separate facts whose causes and meanings are contested.

The city is not a bargaining token that automatically produces a treaty. Napoleon's expectation, Russian governmental policy, the city's physical damage, and the army's need for quarters and food belong to different evidence chains. A courier's absence is an event in the command system, not proof of an enemy intention. The settings must preserve uncertainty over responsibility for fires where sources disagree and must never turn a later memoir's neat explanation into an omniscient scene fact.

**Status:** Work boundary. Retreat is a sequence of changing capacities, not one weather tableau or one date.

Each stage alters the ratio of fighting troops to stragglers, the number of serviceable horses, the availability of shelter, the condition of bridges, and the reach of commanders. Rear guards buy time with ammunition and fatigue; engineers decide which crossings can carry guns; medical parties choose whom they can move; civilians face requisition, flight, hunger, and violence from several armed groups. Settings must distinguish battle loss, disease, exposure, desertion, capture, and abandonment, and must identify which report counted which category.
