# Russia 1812 and Retreat

This file owns the campaign-level constraints of the Russian invasion and retreat.

## Coalition army and multinational scale {#coalition-army-and-multinational-scale}

**Status:** Source-supported. The Grande Armée was a coalition instrument whose national contingents had different languages, supplies, loyalties, and commanders.

The Fondation timeline gives an initial invasion force of about 455,000 men spread across three main groups over roughly 650 kilometres, while a frontline Russian force is given as about 280,000. Those are dated estimates with a stated definition, not a promise that 455,000 men could march together, draw equal rations, or answer one staff language. French, Polish, German, Italian, Austrian, Prussian, and other contingents had different governments and reporting habits. A regiment could be present on paper yet understrength; a cavalry division could have men but not remounts; an allied column could receive the same objective but not the same food or route security. Every downstream strength claim therefore states date, category, and source, separating order-of-battle presence from men able to march, fight, feed themselves, and communicate.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “Napoleon's Russian campaign: the march to the Niemen,” initial force and spread, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-russian-campaign-the-march-to-the-niemen/; Fondation Napoléon, “Napoleon's Grande Armée (1),” coalition organization, https://www.napoleon.org/en/young-historians/napodoc/napoleons-grande-armee-1/.

**Status:** Work canon grounded in primary report limits. Heat, distance, roads, forage, disease, autumn rain, and frost transform operational strength through material losses, not a single “winter” cause.

The 29th Bulletin says the weather was favorable through 6 November, cold began on the 7th, and from 14–16 November the thermometer reached 16–18 degrees below freezing; it also reports horses dying by the hundreds and then thousands, especially French and German animals. This is an official French retrospective written during the disaster and therefore evidence of reported conditions and explanatory framing, not a complete causal ledger. A gun team needs draught horses, harness, road space, and repair; infantry needs food, shoes, clothing, shelter, and water; a sick man needs transport or abandonment. A victory can leave no forage, and a frozen road can help one load while breaking another. Later scenes must identify what the commander knew at the time and what historians infer from the surviving report.

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Occupying Moscow did not compel Russian negotiation; Napoleon's expectation, the city's condition, and Russian policy are separate evidence chains.

The retreat timeline states that Napoleon entered Moscow on 15 September, expecting a delegation and peace discussions, but found the city almost empty; it reports that fires began the same day and drove him from the Kremlin the next day. The timeline cautiously attributes supervision to Governor Rostopchin while noting the contemporary Russian circulation of a French-incendiary explanation. Napoleon's 16 October letter to Maret records a different decision system: two or three thousand wounded and sick had been sent toward Smolensk, Moscow was “a heap of ruins” with no inhabitants, and he considered Kaluga, Tula, Briansk, or a return toward Smolensk depending on enemy cover and weather. The letter is a primary statement of intention, not proof that every subordinate understood it. The setting preserves the missing reply as an operational fact.

**Status:** Work boundary. The retreat is a sequence of changing capacities and route decisions, not one event or one weather tableau.

Moscow departure, the attempt toward Kaluga, the turn back to the devastated Smolensk road, the November crossings, and the final movement toward Vilna alter the army differently. A rear guard buys time with ammunition and fatigue; engineers decide which crossings can carry guns; medical parties choose whom they can move; mounted forces lose the ability to screen; stragglers and prisoners change the crowd at every halt. The official retreat timeline records the 12 November advance guard leaving Smolensk for the Dnieper and the 22 November arrival of Chichagov's headquarters at Borisov, while the 29th Bulletin describes the later cold and road condition from the French reporting position. These entries do not provide one omniscient timetable. Every downstream scene must specify route, road head, command reach, and what capacity has disappeared.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Multinational command turns an order into a chain of translation, liaison, precedence, receipt, and political interpretation.

An order from imperial headquarters to a French corps, Polish cavalry commander, Austrian contingent, or Prussian auxiliary is not one act. It is drafted, carried, translated or explained, acknowledged or misunderstood, and converted into a march under that contingent's food and remount system. The Russian timeline records Alexander and Barclay receiving news of the Niemen crossing late on 24 June and issuing withdrawal orders shortly afterward; it also records the order spreading to corps commanders between 26 and 27 June. That is a usable model for information delay without inventing a dispatch. A common axis on a map does not prove synchronized arrival. Later settings identify sender, recipient, language, route, receipt state, and the political authority that can alter execution.

**Status:** Work canon. Operational distance is governed by roads, bridges, wagons, wells, grain, and horse condition rather than map scale.

The Russian campaign timeline notes that retreating forces tried to destroy Vyazma, while French troops extinguished fires and saved food stores; elsewhere it describes Russian forces leaving Smolensk in ruins and taking poor roads to avoid French artillery. These are campaign reports, not a universal rule that every village was burned or every store saved. A bridge delays guns and ambulances differently from infantry; a flooded track turns baggage into a blockage; a dead horse removes both cavalry mobility and draught power. Columns consume the country along their route, but local officials, peasants, and retreating armies choose what is hidden, destroyed, sold, or surrendered. “The army advanced” is incomplete until the setting states what reached the next halt and what was abandoned.

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Moscow's evacuation, fire, damage, quarters, and diplomatic silence remain separate facts whose causes and meanings are contested.

The official French Bulletin tradition blamed Rostopchin and described pumps and arsenals; the later retreat timeline cautiously presents supervision while recording the competing explanation circulated in Russia. Napoleon's 16 October letter reports that the city was no longer a military position and that he had evacuated wounded and sick toward Smolensk, but it is a staff communication, not a neutral city survey. A missing courier cannot tell the army whether the Tsar refuses peace, has not received the message, or is using delay. **Status:** Work boundary. Retreat mechanics include civilian, medical, animal, and reporting systems, each changing at a different rate.

At one halt the rear guard may still hold a road while hospitals cannot move; at another the bridge can carry infantry but not guns, wagons, or the wounded. Civilians face requisition, flight, hunger, and violence from several armed groups, while their choices affect guides and food. Reports distinguish battle loss, disease, exposure, desertion, capture, and abandonment only when the recorder has a reason and means to count them. The 29th Bulletin's horse and frost account is a French command report; the retreat timeline's dates and Russian movements provide a different operational frame. This file does not convert either into a final total. Later scenes must show who can still march, who guards the crossing, who treats the wounded, and which report excludes the people left behind.
