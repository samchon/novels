# Maps, Communications, and Time

This file owns the information infrastructure that lets a continental state act while remaining uncertain. A map is an object, a message is a journey, a clock is a convention, and a decision is made from what a person has actually received.

<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance This host treats maps as selective objects and does not grant them omniscience. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked the engineers-geographers account on instruments, templates, and map dispatch. -->

**Status:** Source-supported material rule. The Fondation Napoléon account of the engineers-geographers describes fragile instruments, mobile survey work, pre-traced templates, and maps sent from the Dépôt to field engineers; during Russia, maps circulated from Paris through couriers and were requested by officers at many levels. A map therefore records a survey, scale, symbols, date, and purpose. It does not replace a guide who knows a ford, a rider who sees a broken bridge, or reconnaissance that finds a ravine hidden by contour and vegetation. A later unit must identify whose map is present, who made it, what it omits, and what the observer can see at the decision point.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “Les ingénieurs géographes. La science au service de l'Empire,” https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/les-ingenieurs-geographes-la-science-au-service-de-lempire/ (map circulation and instruments section).

**Status:** Postal and technical evidence. The 1812 routes-and-posts account records a special Saint-Cloud-Paris link created in 1802 with five horse departures daily and stated delivery targets; it describes the 1805 estafette as a locked leather portfolio transferred between postilions, with station arrival and departure times recorded. Those controlled routes do not make a campaign courier instantaneous. The telecommunications study describes human dispatch riders, postilions, military post, and the dependence of field orders on a rider reaching the right receiver. Dispatch, route, station, weather, interception, arrival, and response remain separate.

**Status:** Historical method. “Morning,” “near noon,” a calendar date, and an editor's converted hour may describe observation, order, arrival, or publication. A field report may use local apparent time; a staff officer may estimate from march stages; a later memoir may regularize sequence. The novel must identify whether a time is local, astronomical, military, estimated, or converted. Contradictory clocks may describe two observers or two stages. If evidence cannot decide, retain the range and give the downstream scene physical cues—light, distance, fatigue, and report delay—instead of a fabricated minute.

**Status:** Technical military history. Quennevat describes verbal orders, drums, trumpets, estafettes, cannon alarms, and coded vocabulary as Grande Armée media, with the rider as the ordinary human link; the study records a pre-Austerlitz cannon signal ordering a division to move. A signal can travel farther than sight yet be masked by terrain, smoke, distance, battle noise, or another unit's sound; hearing it does not guarantee knowing its code or intended receiver. The setting names sender, medium, code, route, receiver, confirmation, and the consequences of a missed or mistaken signal. No later scene may treat a drumbeat or cannon as unambiguously understood without support.

**Status:** Source-critical. The Fondation account of the Grande Armée bulletins says they were dictated by Napoleon, reshaped by staff officers, circulated through public and military institutions, and used to amplify achievement while minimizing failure and loss. The correspondence project distinguishes published texts, original drafts, later discoveries, annotations, and editorial apparatus. A printed date may be composition, dispatch, receipt, or publication; an edited quotation may omit an enclosure or normalize spelling. Preserve document identity, edition, audience, and intervention before assigning exact time or voice. Official confidence is evidence about public memory, not proof that every participant experienced the event as announced.

**Status:** Work canon grounded in physical media. The reader may know the later result, but the acting person may not. This prevents omniscient command and makes delay, misreading, and correction material.
