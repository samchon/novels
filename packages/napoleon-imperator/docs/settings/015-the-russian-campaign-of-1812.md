# The Russian Campaign of 1812

This file records the diplomatic collapse, the advance, the occupation of Moscow, the retreat, and the destruction of the Grande Armée in 1812, at the same standard of scene-level sufficiency `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md` set for 1805, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#intended-scale`. Borodino, the campaign's single battle, is given its own file at `settings/016-borodino.md`; this file supplies everything around it — the march in, the occupation, the fire, the retreat, and the Berezina — so that a storyline reaching 1812 invents nothing of terrain, chronology, distance, or casualty arithmetic. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## The crossing of the Niemen {#russia-niemen-crossing}

**Standing: established by research, with the Polish-uhlan-disguise and startled-hare embellishment flagged as unverified popular tradition.** The Grande Armée crossed the Niemen at Kovno (Kaunas) beginning 24 June 1812 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`), three bridges thrown across the river near the village of Poniemen; three hundred voltigeurs crossed first, by boat, to secure the far bank while engineers built the pontoons, and Napoleon's own tent stood three hundred yards from the river on the most elevated height overlooking the operation. Roughly 220,000 men crossed in this initial wave, organized in three columns. Napoleon personally reconnoitered the crossing point at night before the main body moved; his horse stumbled and threw him on the sand, and someone in his party exclaimed, "This is a bad omen; a Roman would recoil!" — Ségur records the stumble and the remark but this canon has found no corroboration, in Ségur or elsewhere in this research pass, for the widely repeated embellishment that a hare startled the horse or that Napoleon was reconnoitering in a Polish uhlan's disguise; that detail is popular tradition this canon does not adopt as documented. His proclamation to the army read, in part: "Soldiers, the second Polish war is begun... Let us advance, then; let us pass the Niemen, and carry the war into her territory!" Within days a violent thunderstorm struck the advancing columns — Ségur's chapter heading calls it "a dreadful storm and its fatal effects" — and an estimated ten thousand horses died in the storm and the bivouacs that followed it, from cold rain and from eating unripe rye in stripped country; this is the single most precisely dated attrition figure this canon has for the campaign's opening days. Total invasion strength is treated per the existing range fixed at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812` and `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-russia-1812` — roughly 450,000 for the narrowly counted first-wave crossing force, 600,000–685,000 across the full multinational theater — and this file does not reopen that range.

Sources: Philippe-Paul de Ségur, *History of the Expedition to Russia*, vol. 1 (London: Treuttel and Würtz, 1827), archive.org id `historyofexpedit01sgur`, read directly, pp. 141, 147–154, 174, for the bridging, the voltigeurs, Napoleon's tent, the night reconnaissance and horse-stumble, the proclamation, and the storm and horse losses, read with the reliability qualification at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-segur`; Britannica, "French invasion of Russia," https://www.britannica.com/event/French-invasion-of-Russia; Wikipedia, "French invasion of Russia," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia, whose infobox attributes the 685,000/440,000–449,000 range to Alexander Mikaberidze, *The Napoleonic Wars: A Global History* (2020), p. 539, and to Riehn (1990) and Sokolov (2020) — this canon has not opened that specific Mikaberidze volume (as distinct from *The Burning of Moscow*, which it has read directly elsewhere in this file) or either of the other two directly, and repeats the range as Wikipedia's own synthesis, consistent with this canon's existing figures at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`.

## The advance and the escape of the Russian armies {#russia-advance-vitebsk}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon's plan depended on defeating Barclay de Tolly's First Western Army (~159,800 per a single-source figure this canon flags as needing corroboration) and Bagration's Second Western Army (~45,000–49,423, deployed along a fifty-mile front near Volkovysk and Białystok) separately before they could unite; a third force, Tormasov's Third Reserve Army of Observation (40,000), held the southern flank against Reynier's Saxons and Schwarzenberg's Austrian Auxiliary Corps (~30,000, of which 7,000 were killed in battle and 4,000 died of disease and exposure over the campaign). Davout's I Corps (over 70,000 at full campaign strength) left Vilnius 1 July, defeated Raevsky at Mogilev/Saltanovka on 23 July, occupied Minsk, and by Napoleon's secret order took over the pincer command from Jérôme Bonaparte, whose right wing had been slowed by his insistence on traveling in state with a full court and train; Wikipedia's synthesis attributes "tens of thousands" of eventual casualties to the botched encirclement, though this canon has not traced that figure to a named historian and repeats it only as an unattributed secondary claim. Macdonald's left wing (X Corps), with Yorck's Prussian auxiliary corps attached, failed to take Riga against General Steingel. The escape from encirclement unfolded in a documented sequence: at Mir (9–10 July) Polish cavalry screening Bagration's approach lost 808–908 men to Platov's Cossacks in the campaign's first Russian victory; at Saltanovka/Mogilev (23 July) Raevsky's attack on Davout's position, though a tactical French win (Russian losses ~2,548 against French ~1,200), let Bagration reroute south of the Dnieper rather than link with Barclay at Vitebsk as planned; at Vitebsk (26–27 July) Napoleon's forces struck Barclay's rearguard (French losses 3,400 killed/wounded plus 300 captured, Russian 2,700 plus 1,100 captured), but Napoleon halted operations around 11:00 on the 27th and the Russians withdrew that evening under Cossack screen. Bagration's army had already begun concentrating with Barclay's at Smolensk by roughly 3 August, and by the time the French arrived in mid-August the two Russian armies stood united — foiling Napoleon's divide-and-destroy strategy before a single decisive battle was fought.

Sources: Wikipedia, "First Western Army," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Western_Army (159,800 figure, sourced to Jean-Claude Damamme, *Les soldats de la grande armée*, 2002, p. 401, and flagged there as needing corroboration); "Second Western Army," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Western_Army; "Alexander Tormasov," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Tormasov; "Karl Philipp, Prince of Schwarzenberg," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Philipp,_Prince_of_Schwarzenberg; "Louis-Nicolas Davout," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Nicolas_Davout; "Jérôme Bonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A9r%C3%B4me_Bonaparte; "Jacques MacDonald," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_MacDonald; "Battle of Mir (1812)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mir_(1812); "Battle of Saltanovka," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Saltanovka; "Battle of Vitebsk (1812)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vitebsk_(1812); "Pyotr Bagration," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyotr_Bagration; Britannica, "French invasion of Russia" (3 August junction date), as above.

## Vilnius and the refused restoration of Poland {#russia-vilnius}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon reached Vilnius 28 June 1812, four days after the Niemen crossing (~23 km/day, within this canon's normal marching band), and remained roughly eighteen days, departing 16 July at half-past eleven at night; Ségur's own text calls the halt "twenty days" in one place, a minor internal discrepancy this canon notes rather than silently resolves. He installed Hugues-Bernard Maret, Duke of Bassano, as governor and ordered a levy of eleven Lithuanian regiments. On 1 July 1812 Napoleon's order established the Lithuanian Provisional Governing Commission, a short-lived unrecognized client state controlling four governorates (Vilnius, Grodno, Minsk, Białystok), tasked chiefly with raising Lithuanian forces and provisioning the army; it dissolved 30 April 1813 after Russian forces retook Lithuania. Separately, on 28 June the Warsaw Sejm proclaimed the General Confederation of the Kingdom of Poland, hoping for a restored, reunified Polish-Lithuanian state; a Polish delegation under Józef Wybicki traveled to Vilnius on 11 July specifically to press Napoleon for full restoration and was turned down — he deliberately refused any permanent commitment that would tie his hands before an anticipated peace settlement with Russia, and had separately promised Austria he would not disturb "the peaceable possession of what remains to him of the Polish provinces." Three days later, on 14 July, the Lithuanian Commission formally joined the Confederation, a nominal union that changed nothing about Napoleon's refusal to commit Lithuanian units to Polish command. The refusal is a fact this canon records without staging it: it constrains any scene of Polish or Lithuanian hope and disappointment in 1812, but this file does not decide when or how that disappointment lands.

Sources: Ségur, vol. 1, pp. 162, 191, as above; Wikipedia, "Lithuanian Provisional Governing Commission," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Provisional_Governing_Commission; "Duchy of Warsaw," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Warsaw; Britannica, "French invasion of Russia," for the 28 June Vilnius arrival date.

## The halt at Vitebsk and the push onward {#russia-vitebsk-halt}

**Standing: established by research, wording treated as reliably attested via Ségur's memoir rather than an independent primary transcript.** Reaching Vitebsk 28 July 1812, Napoleon at least initially planned to winter the campaign there, reportedly throwing his sword down on his maps and declaring: "Here I stop! here I must look round me; rally; refresh my army, and organize Poland. The campaign of 1812 is finished." He was already hedging the same moment with a fallback: "if peace should not come to seek us in our winter quarters, we will go and conquer it at Moscow." What broke the halt, per Ségur, was a cavalry check at Inkowo, where Sebastiani was surprised and defeated by an estimated 10,000 Russian cavalry — "it was the check at Inkowo which decided Napoleon" to abandon Vitebsk and pursue toward Smolensk; he departed 13 August. The three days from Vitebsk to the approach to Smolensk (16 August) covered roughly 126 km straight-line, close to forty-two kilometers a day — near this canon's forced-march ceiling of roughly fifty-six kilometers a day (`settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching`) and explicitly framed in the sources as a deliberate rapid maneuver, using four pontoon bridges General Jean-Baptiste Eblé's engineers built at Rossasna on 13–14 August to move some 175,000 troops south of the Dnieper in an attempt to outflank Barclay and cut the Moscow road. The attempted encirclement failed, but the pace itself is a genuine forced-march episode a downstream scene may use as such.

Sources: Ségur, vol. 1, pp. 215, 245, as above; Wikipedia, "Battle of Smolensk (1812)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Smolensk_(1812), which attributes the Rossasna bridges and the flanking maneuver to George Nafziger, *Napoleon's Invasion of Russia* (1988), pp. 185–186 — this canon has not opened Nafziger's book directly and repeats the detail as Wikipedia reports it; `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching`, established by research, for the marching-rate ceiling this entry checks against.

## Smolensk {#russia-smolensk}

**Standing: established by research, with the wall's tower count genuinely disputed three ways between sources.** Fought 16–18 August 1812 (Britannica extends the fighting to the 19th), Smolensk was defended by an old medieval wall whose exact scale this canon does not silently resolve: Ségur's contemporaneous account gives a wall "twenty-five feet high, eighteen thick, three thousand fathoms in length, and defended by twenty-nine massive towers," historyofwar.org gives "a four mile long medieval town wall, protected by 32 towers," and Wikipedia (citing Riehn) gives "38 bastion towers." Before the main battle, Barclay's rearguard under Neverovsky was struck at Krasnoi on 14 August by some 20,000 French under Murat and Ney — a preliminary action distinct from the much larger battles fought at the same place in November during the retreat (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-retreat`) — costing the Russians 1,500–2,300 casualties and seven guns against roughly 500 French losses before Neverovsky fell back into the city; Raevsky's VII Corps arrived 15 August to hold the southern Dnieper bank. On 16 August a French probing attack took two suburbs but failed to draw the Russians into open battle; Napoleon ordered a general assault by three corps and roughly two hundred guns, the bombardment set the city alight, and French troops lacking scaling ladders were checked at the walls while most of Smolensk burned by nightfall. On 17 August Polish forces breached the walls at dawn and French troops entered within hours, fighting through to the citadel; one unnamed unit lost two-thirds of its men in a single assault wave against the citadel gate. Barclay evacuated overnight, destroying ammunition and bridges and holding the far riverbank until the night of the 18th to block a French crossing. Casualties are disputed across named historians and this canon states the range rather than a single figure: Russian losses run from Barclay's own claim of 4,000 through Bogdanovich's and Bodart's 6,000 to Chandler's and Mikaberidze's 10,000–14,000, with Dokhturov's VI Corps alone reportedly reduced from 16,800 to 6,000 combat-capable; French losses run from Napoleon's own official claim of 700 killed and 3,100–3,200 wounded (a Bulletin-scale undercount, per the pattern already documented at `settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`) through Chandler's, Mikaberidze's, and Bodart's converging figure of roughly 10,000 to Britannica's notably higher "nearly 15,000 killed and wounded," while contemporary Russian claims ran as high as 20,000. Of 2,250 buildings, 84% were destroyed, leaving only 350 standing; of roughly 15,000 pre-battle inhabitants, only about 1,000 remained in the ruins. Napoleon captured the city but failed his real objective — the Russian army escaped intact, and Tsar Alexander replaced Barclay with Kutuzov on 29 August at Tsaryovo-Zaymishche (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#kutuzov`).

Sources: Ségur, vol. 1, pp. 258–259, 264, 269, 286, as above (wall description, citadel assault, and cross-reference to Gudin's death at Valutino below); historyofwar.org, "Battle of Smolensk, 1812," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_smolensk_1812.html, read directly for the 32-tower figure and the 12,000–14,000/10,000–12,000 casualty range; Wikipedia, "Battle of Smolensk (1812)," as above, whose 38-tower figure and disputed casualty range are attributed there to Richard K. Riehn, *1812: Napoleon's Russian Campaign* (1990), David Chandler, and Alexander Mikaberidze — this canon has not opened any of the three directly and repeats their figures only as Wikipedia's synthesis reports them; Britannica, "French invasion of Russia," for the higher French-loss figure and the 16–19 August date range; Jakob Walter, *The Diary of a Napoleonic Foot Soldier* (1938 English publication), quoted at second hand via Wikipedia's own quotation of it, not from this canon's own reading of the diary, for the eyewitness account of the assault.

## Valutino and the road to Moscow {#russia-valutino}

**Standing: established by research.** On 19 August 1812, near Valutino and Lubino roughly twenty kilometers east of Smolensk, Ney's corps (~35,000) attacked Barclay's rearguard under Tuchkov and Yermolov, holding marshy ground along the Stragan river; Murat's cavalry bogged down in the marsh, and Junot's force notably declined to engage despite Murat's urging — an acknowledged missed opportunity for a decisive envelopment. General Charles Gudin led a final assault, was struck by cannon fire, lost a leg, and died of the wound roughly three days later; Ségur records that "Gudin was conveyed to Smolensk, and there received the unavailing attentions of the emperor; but he soon expired. His remains were interred in the citadel of the city." French casualties ran 7,000–9,000 against Russian losses of roughly 6,000. Despite fierce resistance and Russian reinforcement, the French finally took the crest, but Barclay had never intended to hold the position and continued his orderly, scorched-earth withdrawal — a second missed chance, after Krasnoi and Saltanovka, to trap and destroy the Russian main body before it reached Moscow.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Valutino," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Valutino; Ségur, vol. 1, p. 286, as above.

## The push to Moscow {#russia-push-to-moscow}

**Standing: established by research, with the exchange's exact placement in the chronology noted rather than smoothed over.** By his own account, given to Sebastiani on the road out of Smolensk, Napoleon's reasoning for pressing on rather than wintering near the city was explicit: "It is dreadful, I know... from Wilna, half of it consisted of stragglers; now they form two-thirds; there is, therefore, no time to be lost: we must extort peace; it is at Moscow. Besides, this army cannot now stop: with its composition, and in its disorganization, motion alone keeps it together. One may advance at the head of it, but not stop or go back. It is an army of attack, not of defence; an army of operation, not of position." This is Napoleon's own self-reported figure for the army's collapse into stragglers — roughly half the force at Vilnius in early July, roughly two-thirds by late August — and this canon treats it as the clearest single articulation of both the campaign's underlying attrition and his stated reason for continuing rather than halting. Wikipedia's synthesis independently confirms the broader pattern without a matching precise fraction: "The main body of Napoleon's Grande Armée diminished by a third in just the first eight weeks of the campaign, before any major battle was fought," from extreme weather, disease (diphtheria, dysentery, typhus), desertion, straggling, and the peeling-off of garrison and supply-depot troops along the route — consistent with, though not identical in framing to, Napoleon's own "half, then two-thirds" figure for stragglers specifically. This canon could not locate, in this research pass, any source stating a specific absolute number (such as "150,000") for losses in the Niemen-to-Smolensk phase; that figure, if a downstream unit wants one, should be treated as a plausible but unverified derivation from the "roughly a third" fraction rather than as an established count. Smolensk to Moscow is roughly 400 km by the commonly cited figure, consistent with a straight-line distance of roughly 369 km plus normal road indirection; the actual march, delayed by Borodino (`settings/016-borodino.md`) and the general slowing of the army, covered it at roughly fourteen kilometers a day — markedly slower than the Vitebsk–Smolensk dash and well inside this canon's normal marching band.

Sources: Ségur, vol. 1, p. 280, as above; Wikipedia, "French invasion of Russia," as above (the "diminished by a third" claim); `settings/008-grande-armee.md#logistics-marching` and `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-distances-table`, each established by research, for the marching-rate cross-check.

## Moscow occupied {#russia-moscow-occupation}

**Standing: established by research, with one napoleon.org timeline page's "15 September" entry date noted as an outlier this canon does not adopt.** Napoleon occupied Moscow 14 September 1812 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`), Murat's vanguard reaching the Dorogomilovo district at two in the afternoon and Napoleon himself entering later that day after waiting a quarter-hour at the city walls for a delegation with the keys that never came. Governor Fyodor Rostopchin had evacuated the fire brigade — some 2,000 men and roughly 100 water pumps — the night before, along with civil servants and elites; of a pre-war population cited variously at 200,000–270,000, only some 6,200–15,000 remained, plus 10,000–15,000 wounded Russian soldiers left behind. Napoleon took the Kremlin as headquarters; discipline collapsed within days, with looting by soldiers and locals alike — "attempts by French commanders to maintain discipline failed and soldiers would openly disobey the orders of their officers," including elements of the Imperial Guard — and Napoleon made at least three documented, unanswered peace overtures: a message to Alexander via the Moscow Orphanage's Ivan Tutolmin around 18 September; a letter carried through French lines by Ivan Yakovlev around 22 September; and, most formally, General Jacques Lauriston's mission to Kutuzov's headquarters at Tarutino on 4–5 October, carrying a personal letter from Napoleon and securing only a stalling secret meeting rather than any real negotiation, since Alexander had explicitly ordered no negotiation while a single French soldier remained on Russian soil. The occupation lasted five weeks, 14 September to 19 October. On departure Marshal Mortier laid demolition charges under the Kremlin on Napoleon's orders; rain and Muscovite intervention limited the damage to the Vodovzvodnaya Tower (destroyed) and the Nikolskaya, First Bezymyannaya, and Petrovskaya towers, the wall, and part of the arsenal (damaged), with the Ivan the Great Bell Tower surviving intact.

Sources: Wikipedia, "French occupation of Moscow," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_occupation_of_Moscow, which attributes the population figures and the discipline collapse there to Richard K. Riehn (1990) and Vladimir N. Zemtsov, "Russia and the Napoleonic Wars: 200 Years Later," *Quaestio Rossica* (2017) — this canon has not opened either directly and repeats the figures as Wikipedia's own synthesis; "French invasion of Russia," as above, for the Alexander Herzen family connection to the Yakovlev mission, sourced there to the 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica*'s "Herzen, Alexander" entry — the 1911 *Britannica* is itself freely available via Wikisource and this canon has not separately opened that entry to confirm the detail beyond Wikipedia's own quotation of it; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Russian Campaign: The Retreat," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-russian-campaign-the-retreat/, for the Lauriston mission and Napoleon's own letter of 4 October (*Correspondance Générale* no. 31792) — this canon notes the same page dates Napoleon's entry to Moscow to "the 15th," contradicted by every other source it has read, including napoleon.org's own companion timeline, and does not adopt that date.

## The fire of Moscow {#russia-fire-moscow}

**Standing: disputed between sources both on the fire's extent and its cause, the latter contested even by this canon's own privileged modern historian.** Fires began the night of 14–15 September, worsened by strong winds starting the night of 15–16 September, and burned through 18 September; flames were reportedly visible from 215 km away. Extent is genuinely disputed: Wikipedia's dedicated fire article gives roughly 71% of residential buildings destroyed (6,496 of 9,151), while a separate Wikipedia passage citing Riehn gives "three quarters" of the city, and napoleon.org's own retreat timeline gives the lower figure of "a third of Moscow's houses"; this canon states the range — roughly a third to three-quarters, depending on source and metric — rather than adopting one figure silently. Attribution is likewise contested and not merely by amateur tradition: Wikipedia calls deliberate arson on Rostopchin's order "the most widely accepted theory," but cites for that judgment both a popular history and a dedicated chapter by Alexander Mikaberidze — this canon's own privileged modern historian for the Russian side (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-modern-scholarship`) — titled "By Accident or Malice? Who Burned Moscow," confirming the question is a genuine, still-argued historiographical problem rather than a settled fact even within the scholarship this canon trusts most. The countervailing reading holds that an undisciplined occupying army in a mostly wooden, deserted city with no functioning fire service made a catastrophic fire near-inevitable without any single order; Rostopchin himself denied responsibility in an 1823 Paris pamphlet before later admitting his role. The fire destroyed the winter quarters and much of the food and forage Napoleon had counted on, and this canon treats that material loss — not any deliberate reveal of the arsonist — as the fire's decisive strategic fact.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Fire of Moscow (1812)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_of_Moscow_(1812), which attributes the "most widely accepted theory" judgment to Paul Britten Austin, *1812: Napoleon in Moscow* (2012), ch. 1, and to a chapter of Alexander Mikaberidze's *The Burning of Moscow: Napoleon's Trial by Fire, 1812* (Pen & Sword, 2014) specifically titled "By Accident or Malice? Who Burned Moscow" (ch. 8, pp. 145–165) — this canon has read other portions of the same Mikaberidze book directly (`settings/016-borodino.md#borodino-aftermath`) but has not itself opened chapter 8, and repeats the chapter's existence and title only as Wikipedia reports them, not as this canon's own reading of its argument; Britten Austin's book this canon has not opened at all; "French occupation of Moscow," as above; "Fyodor Rostopchin," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Rostopchin, for the 1823 pamphlet and later admission; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Russian Campaign: The Retreat," as above.

## The decision to retreat and Maloyaroslavets {#russia-retreat-decision}

**Standing: established by research.** By 18 October, with Bennigsen's defeat of Murat at Tarutino/Vinkovo and a French reverse at the Second Battle of Polotsk the same day, Napoleon accepted that no peace was coming. He left Moscow 19 October by the old, southern Kaluga road, deliberately avoiding the devastated Smolensk road he had marched in on. At Maloyaroslavets, 24 October, Eugène de Beauharnais's Franco-Italian IV Corps (~24,000, including Delzons's division and Pino's Italian Royal Guard) fought Dokhturov's corps, reinforced during the day by Raevsky and then by Kutuzov's main army; casualties ran roughly 6,000–8,000 on each side. The French held the town tactically, but Kutuzov's army fell back to a blocking position just south of it, sealing the Kaluga road and forcing Napoleon back onto the Mozhaisk–Smolensk route — the same devastated road he had tried to avoid. On 26 October Napoleon informed Berthier of the decision to retreat via Vyazma.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Maloyaroslavets," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maloyaroslavets, whose casualty figures are attributed there to Richard K. Riehn (1990), the contemporary British liaison officer Robert Wilson, and Gaston Bodart — none opened directly by this canon, and repeated only as Wikipedia's synthesis; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Russian Campaign: The Retreat," as above, read via `fetch.sh`.

## The retreat: Vyazma, Krasnoi, and the cold {#russia-retreat}

**Standing: established by research.** At Vyazma, 3 November, roughly 25,000 of the ~37,500 French troops present (drawn from Davout's, Eugène's, Ney's, and Poniatowski's corps) fought some 27,000 Russians under Miloradovich; French losses ran 4,000–7,000 against Russian losses of 1,800–2,500, and the action marked a turning point from organized retreat toward increasingly chaotic withdrawal. Napoleon reached the ruins of Smolensk on 9 November, found supplies insufficient, and departed 14 November, with Ney's III Corps leaving last at 2 a.m. on the 17th. At Krasnoi, 15–18 November — the much larger and more consequential battles fought at the same crossroads where the preliminary action of 14 August had occurred (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-smolensk`) — Ney's corps was nearly encircled; of roughly 4,000 remaining men, he led perhaps 800–1,000 survivors across the frozen Dnieper in a night escape to rejoin the main army, an episode that earns him the epithet recorded at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-ney`. Overall French losses at Krasnoi ran 5,000–13,000 killed, wounded, or drowned, plus 26,500 prisoners (mostly stragglers) and 228 guns, against Russian losses of 2,000–5,000. The cold itself is precisely documented in places: Gaspard Gourgaud's 1825 critical history records a night temperature drop to roughly minus 35°C in late November that cost Loison's division 12,000 of its 15,000 men in three days without a battle, and Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 data chart records temperatures reaching the equivalent of roughly minus 38°C; napoleon.org's retreat timeline records minus 30°C at Kovno on 7 December. Starvation produced widely attested cannibalism of horseflesh — Caulaincourt, cited via Wikipedia's page-level citations since this canon could not access his memoir's full text directly in this research pass (see the note below), describes men cutting up horses that had slipped and fallen even before the animal was dead, and other accounts describe eating the flesh of horses "too cold to react in pain." The cavalry arm was effectively destroyed: 108,000 men left Moscow on 19 October, but with horses exhausted or dead, cavalrymen were redirected into infantry and the army lost over 150,000 horses across the campaign, leaving it undefended against constant Cossack harassment.

This canon notes explicitly that Caulaincourt's own memoir, *With Napoleon in Russia* (1935 English translation), could not be read directly in this research pass: the archive.org copies are marked access-restricted (controlled digital lending) and returned HTTP 401/403 to every access attempt, and Gallica's OCR endpoint for the French original returned only a bot-detection page. Every Caulaincourt quotation and page citation in this file and `settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#relation-napoleon-caulaincourt` therefore rests on secondary page-level citation (chiefly Wikipedia's own sfn references to the 1935 edition) rather than on this canon's own direct reading of the primary text, and this file flags that limitation rather than presenting the citations as equivalent to the direct archival reading this canon gives Ségur.

## The Berezina {#russia-berezina}

**Standing: established by research.** Between 26 and 29 November 1812, three converging Russian forces — Wittgenstein (~30,000) from the north, Chichagov's Third Western Army (~35,000) from the west, and Kutuzov's main army (~39,000, roughly two marches behind) from the rear — threatened to trap the retreating Grande Armée at the Berezina river. Because Chichagov and the other Russian commanders expected Napoleon to continue toward Minsk, their forces concentrated wrongly; Napoleon instead struck for Studianka via the Zembin road toward Vilna, reaching it with the main army by 25 November. General Jean-Baptiste Eblé, having earlier disobeyed a direct order to destroy the army's bridging equipment, built two pontoon bridges at Studianka — the river there twenty to thirty meters wide, choked with ice, with swampy banks — the first completed by 1 p.m. on 26 November and the second, for artillery and wagons, later that afternoon after collapsing twice under load; his engineers worked in the freezing water, and Eblé himself died 31 December 1812 at Königsberg, likely from the effects of the exposure. Napoleon and the Guard crossed by midday on 27 November; 28 November saw coordinated Russian attacks from both banks; at roughly 8:30 a.m. on 29 November Eblé burned the bridges, leaving tens of thousands of stragglers and camp followers stranded on the east bank, many of whom drowned attempting to swim through the ice. Casualties are disputed and this canon states the range: historian David Chandler's figures, cited via Wikipedia, give 20,000–30,000 French combatant casualties over the three days plus a separate roughly 30,000 non-combatant deaths from exposure and starvation (many after capture), against Russian losses of at least 10,000 killed; napoleon.org's retreat timeline gives a broader "25,000 to 40,000 men" lost overall. Only twenty-five guns were reportedly abandoned. napoleon.org's own framing captures the paradox this canon adopts: "in many ways, the crossing of the Berezina was disastrous for Napoleon... However, it was one of his most brilliant victories, since the crossing actually took place" — the fighting core of the army and Napoleon himself escaped a converging trap that could have ended the campaign in total capture, even as the toll among stragglers and camp followers left behind when the bridges burned made it, in popular and historical memory, the campaign's single worst massacre.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Battle of Berezina," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Berezina, whose casualty figures are attributed there to David G. Chandler, *The Campaigns of Napoleon* (1966), and to Gaston Bodart (1908) — this canon has not opened either directly (the existing canon at `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french` records the same standing non-access to Chandler) and repeats the figures as Wikipedia's synthesis; "Jean-Baptiste Eblé," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Eblé; napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Russian Campaign: The Retreat," as above, read via `fetch.sh`.

## Destruction of the Grande Armée {#russia-destruction}

**Standing: established by research, with the total invasion force and the survivor count both explicitly disputed among named historians for the reasons stated below.** No single figure this canon has found settles either end of the campaign's arithmetic, and this canon states the range at every stage rather than collapsing it. On the initial force: Wikipedia's infobox gives 600,000–685,000 total (citing Mikaberidze 2020, Riehn 1990, and Bodart 1916), with the narrower first-wave Niemen crossing at 440,000–449,000 (Sokolov 2020; Riehn 1990) — consistent with this canon's existing figures at `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-russia-1812`. A named revisionist caveat exists and this canon records it without adopting it: François Lelouard, writing for The Napoleon Series, argues the widely cited totals are inflated by specific double-counting errors (a Bavarian infantry regiment counted twice, a Baden regiment counted three times, a 10,000-man Danish division under General Ewald that "never set foot in Russia" but appears in several tallies), surveys competing published totals as low as ~400,000 (Jacques-Olivier Boudon, 2012) and as high as 611,154 (the Fondation Napoléon's own *Correspondance générale*, tome XII), and argues that inflated French and minimized Russian numbers together serve an apologetic "overwhelming numbers, scorched-earth inevitability" narrative. This canon does not adopt Lelouard's specific downward revision as fact, but records it as a genuine, named methodological caveat on precision.

On losses, Wikipedia's infobox (citing Adam Zamoyski 2005, Michael Clodfelter 2008, and Bodart 1916) gives total casualties of 580,000–630,000: 300,000–350,000 dead (100,000 killed in combat, 200,000 dead of other causes, 50,000 dead in captivity), 180,000 wounded, 50,000 deserted, 50,000 captured. Survivors are given at roughly 120,000 excluding early deserters, broken down as 50,000 Austrian, Prussian, and other German troops, 20,000 Poles, and only 35,000 Frenchmen (Zamoyski). Britannica's own summary, quoted directly, gives the figures in a different but broadly consistent form: "It is estimated that of the 612,000 combatants who entered Russia only 112,000 returned to the frontier. Among the casualties, 100,000 are thought to have been killed in action, 200,000 to have died from other causes, 50,000 to have been left sick in hospitals, 50,000 to have deserted, and 100,000 to have been taken as prisoners of war." Alexander Mikaberidze's own figure for prisoners specifically, from a dedicated study, gives over 110,000 Russian-held prisoners across the six-month campaign, of whom roughly two-thirds died within weeks of capture; official Russian provincial returns recorded 65,503 prisoners dead by February 1813 and 39,645 still held, including fifty women and seven children.

A distinct and much-quoted figure comes from Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 flow-map, which this canon treats as measuring a narrower quantity than the survivor totals above rather than contradicting them: 422,000 crossing the Niemen with Napoleon, 22,000 detached on an early side expedition, 100,000 surviving the battles en route to Moscow and back, and ultimately only 10,000 organized, combat-fit troops recrossing the Niemen as a body under command. This canon states explicitly that Minard's roughly 10,000 and the roughly 110,000–120,000 survivor figures above are not in contradiction: the low figure counts only the organized fighting remnant crossing the river on the specific date the retreat's front reached it, while the higher figures include stragglers, the sick left in hospital (some 20,000 in Vilnius alone), garrison troops who never engaged, and individuals who made their way back over the following weeks by other routes. A downstream unit citing "how many came back" must specify which of these two very different quantities it means. Within days of Napoleon's own departure (`settings/015-the-russian-campaign-of-1812.md#russia-departure`), a further roughly 20,000 men died of louse-borne disease, per a 2006 genetic study of remains recovered in Vilnius.

## Napoleon's departure and the 29th Bulletin {#russia-departure}

**Standing: established by research, with the complete verbatim text of the 29th Bulletin not located in this research pass.** Napoleon left the army at Smorgon (Smarhon) on 5 December 1812, roughly nine in the evening, prompted in part by news reaching him on 6 November near Mikhailovka of General Claude-François de Malet's failed coup attempt in Paris on 23 October, which had briefly announced his death and collapsed within a day. He told Caulaincourt, "in the current state of affairs, I can command in Europe only from the Tuileries palace," and left the already-sick Murat in nominal command; Eugène de Beauharnais assumed effective command only in January 1813, after Murat abandoned the post to try to save his Neapolitan throne. The 29th Bulletin, issued from Molodetschno on 3 December — two days before Napoleon's own departure — reached Paris and *Le Moniteur Universel* on 17 December, per this canon's standing on the Bulletins as a documented propaganda instrument (`settings/014-sources-and-historiography.md#source-bulletins`); its closing line, "La santé de Sa Majesté n'a jamais été meilleure" ("His Majesty's health has never been better"), followed a candid catalogue of the army's collapse — men falling from hunger, cold, and discouragement, cavalry without horses, generals doing captains' work — making the line's implicit claim, that the Emperor himself remained untouched by the catastrophe he had just described in detail, the Bulletins' starkest instance yet of managing morale through a single, deliberately jarring assertion. This canon has not located a complete verbatim transcription of the Bulletin itself in this research pass; the text above is drawn from Jacques Bainville's 1931 secondary account, which paraphrases and quotes its most significant passages directly.

Caulaincourt accompanied Napoleon alone on the roughly 2,500-kilometer, thirteen-day sledge and carriage journey from Smorgon to Paris — the basis for the sustained secondary-viewpoint access this canon grants him from 1812 onward (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#focal-figures`) — traveling via Vilna, Kovno (crossing the frozen Niemen out of Russia at 5 a.m. on 7 December, in reported cold of minus 30°C), Warsaw (where Napoleon walked incognito to the Hôtel d'Angleterre and received Polish ministers), Dresden, and the Rhine crossing at Frankfurt, before arriving unannounced at the Tuileries in the small hours of 18–19 December 1812 — the same day the 29th Bulletin reached Verdun on their own route home. Their daily rhythm on the journey — coffee together each morning, dinner together each evening, and the extended private conversation Caulaincourt later published separately as *En traîneau avec l'Empereur* — is the documented basis for treating this journey as the origin point of the unusually candid access this canon's fourth focal figure has to Napoleon's unguarded thinking.
