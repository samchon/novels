**Status:** Source-supported political geography. The Italian Kingdom article stresses that the 1805 Kingdom of Italy was not the peninsula as a whole but principally Lombardy and Emilia-Romagna, while other states and territories retained distinct institutions. A French army can move through one jurisdiction, negotiate with another, and requisition from a third. Settings identify state, city, port, road, court, local official, language, season, and affected population before treating Italy as a single response.

**Status:** Critical political history. The Italian Kingdom account records the Cisalpine/Italian Republic becoming a kingdom on 17 March 1805, with Napoleon as king, while the Cambridge study emphasizes inherited ancien-régime institutions, municipal autonomy, and the difficulty of unified administration. Military victory becomes power through treaties, constitutions, taxes, offices, translations, and local cooperation. A battlefield result does not erase Venetian, Milanese, Papal, Neapolitan, or municipal interests. Later scenes distinguish French order, client-state policy, official compliance, and population reception.

**Status:** Source-critical. Conflict with the papacy is simultaneously diplomatic, territorial, religious, legal, and symbolic. The French 1801 Concordat account notes that Catholicism was described as the religion of the majority rather than the state religion, that Church property seized during the Revolution was not reclaimed, and that the French state controlled the Church of France through Gallican arrangements. The Cambridge account distinguishes the 1803 Italian Concordat, where Catholicism was treated as a state religion; French and Italian settlements therefore cannot be collapsed. These facts do not settle private belief. Settings identify treaty, envoy, territory, clergy, property, ceremony, and audience; ceremony and coercion can coexist.
