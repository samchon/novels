**Status:** Source-supported. Coalition members share an enemy without sharing war aims, territorial priorities, or tolerance for loss.

The 1805 correspondence introduction describes Britain, Russia, and Austria forming a new coalition against France, while the 1807 correspondence overview lists further alliances and arrangements involving Prussia, Saxony, Denmark, Spain, Persia, and the Confederation of the Rhine. An alliance is therefore a document, cabinet decision, military timetable, and public argument, not a single will. Austria, Russia, Prussia, Britain, and smaller states pursue different territory, commerce, religion, security, and tolerance for loss. A French victory can dissolve one coalition while creating another; a treaty can transfer local populations before their daily authority changes.

**Status:** Work canon. Armistice, treaty, demobilization, occupation, and durable peace are separate states.

The Treaty of Amiens was signed on 25 March 1802 after ten years of war and required Britain to return colonies while France withdrew from Naples and the Roman States; its public harmony clause did not make every military, colonial, or commercial question disappear. Tilsit's English text states that hostilities cease when official intelligence is received and that extraordinary couriers must carry the news to commanders, making receipt part of the peace mechanism. An armistice may leave troops facing one another, prisoners unresolved, and supplies scarce; peace negotiations can continue while armies prepare. A signature is not an instant emotional or material reset.
