# The Third Coalition and the 1805 Campaign

This file records the diplomatic and operational formation of the War of the Third Coalition and the campaign that carried the Grande Armée from the Channel coast to Moravia, so that the work's opening at Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md`) needs to invent nothing of its context, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality` — this file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule.

## Collapse of the Peace of Amiens {#coalition-amiens-collapse}

**Standing: established by research.** The Treaty of Amiens, signed 25/27 March 1802, gave Europe its only full peace of the Revolutionary/Napoleonic era, lasting roughly fourteen months. The immediate rupture cause was Malta: Britain had agreed to evacuate the island and hand it to the Knights of St. John, but refused, citing continued French interference in Italy, Switzerland, and the Levant; Napoleon treated the retention of Malta as a treaty violation. Underlying causes ran deeper — neither side had ever regarded Amiens as more than a truce, Napoleon refused a comprehensive trade treaty with Britain and continued aggressive consolidation of satellite territories (annexation of Piedmont, intervention in Switzerland via the Act of Mediation), and a notoriously undiplomatic dressing-down of the British ambassador Lord Whitworth in March 1803 hardened British resolve. Britain declared war on France on 18 May 1803, ending the peace (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-amiens`). Napoleon's retaliatory lever was the occupation of Hanover, the German electorate held in personal union by the British crown, by Marshal Mortier's roughly 20,000–30,000 troops in June 1803 — a lever later used repeatedly as a bargaining chip to try to entice Prussia into alliance.

Sources: Britannica, "Treaty of Amiens," https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Amiens-1802 (27 March 1802 signing; Britain's undertaking to evacuate Malta within three months); Britannica, "Napoleonic Wars: The Third and Fourth Coalitions, 1803–07," https://www.britannica.com/event/Napoleonic-Wars/The-Third-and-Fourth-Coalitions-1803-07 (Britain declaring war 18 May 1803; Malta as the technical casus belli); Wikipedia, "Invasion of Hanover (1803)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Hanover_(1803) (26 May – 5 June 1803, Mortier in command, French strength cited as 25,000 in the infobox and 13,000 for the initial force in the body text, both within this canon's stated 20,000–30,000 range). This canon has not independently sourced the Whitworth dressing-down or the Act of Mediation claims beyond what is already carried here.

## Formation of the Third Coalition {#coalition-formation}

**Standing: established by research, except the Convention of 6 November 1804's troop-commitment figures, which are not yet decided for the reason stated below.** Sweden allied with Britain in December 1804, financed almost entirely by British subsidy. Russia and Britain concluded the Treaty of St. Petersburg on 11 April 1805, a formal offensive alliance. Austria acceded secretly on 9 August 1805, the moment usually cited as completing the Third Coalition as an operative military alliance. Naples/Sicily joined 11 September 1805, while simultaneously double-dealing — signing a neutrality treaty with Talleyrand on 21 September (ratified 3 October) before reneging and admitting a 13,000-strong Anglo-Russian landing force on 20 November 1805. Tsar Alexander I's motivations (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#alexander-i`) included resentment at Napoleon's continuing territorial reorganization of Germany and Italy, particularly his assumption of the title King of Italy in Milan in May 1805; the execution of the Duc d'Enghien in March 1804 (a Bourbon prince seized from neutral Baden and shot after a summary trial) caused genuine personal outrage at the Russian court; and Napoleon's self-coronation reinforced Alexander's sense of permanent hegemonic ambition. Alexander's Polish-born adviser Adam Jerzy Czartoryski negotiated the Convention of 6 November 1804, an Austro-Russian military planning agreement that built the coalition's military skeleton before Britain and Austria formally joined; this canon can no longer locate a checkable source for the specific troop-commitment figures (Russia 115,000, Austria 235,000) it previously carried for that convention, and treats them as not yet decided pending a dedicated diplomatic-history source rather than repeating them on no located authority. Austria's own reluctance and eventual accession, and Prussia's continued neutrality through the Hardenberg–Haugwitz split at the Prussian court, are recorded at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#prussia-frederick-william`.

Sources: World History Encyclopedia, "War of the Third Coalition," https://www.worldhistory.org/War_of_the_Third_Coalition/, for the Sweden-Britain accession, Austria's accession, the Enghien execution, and the King of Italy title — this source does not cover the Convention of 6 November 1804 or its figures; Wikipedia, "Treaty of Saint Petersburg (1805)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Saint_Petersburg_(1805); The Napoleon Series, "Treaty of Alliance between Great Britain and Russia 1805," https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/diplomatic/c_britishrussian.html, the treaty text itself, dated 11 April 1805.

## Camp de Boulogne: purpose and scale {#coalition-boulogne-camp}

**Standing: established by research.** The Armée des côtes de l'Océan, the "Armée d'Angleterre," assembled at camps around Boulogne, Bruges, and Montreuil (`settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-boulogne`) from 1803 through 1805, intended for a cross-Channel invasion of Britain. Strength grew from roughly 80,000 in late 1803 to over 150,000 by June 1805, with 10,000–15,000 horses and 500 artillery pieces across the camps; peak figures for the full invasion order of battle, including depots, run to roughly 200,000. The invasion flotilla comprised roughly 2,000 craft — nearly half gunboats of various sizes, plus 765 armed transports and roughly 350 converted fishing boats and coasters.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Napoleon's planned invasion of the United Kingdom," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_planned_invasion_of_the_United_Kingdom ("a new army of 200,000 men... was gathered and trained at camps"); napoleon.org, "Bullet Point #32: Did Napoleon really intend to invade Britain?," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/bullet-point-32-did-napoleon-really-intend-to-invade-britain/ (confirming "more than 150,000 men, 10,000 to 15,000 horses and 500 artillery pieces" in the camps by 1805).

## The invasion flotilla and Villeneuve's fleet {#coalition-villeneuve}

**Standing: established by research, upgraded from Wikipedia/historyofwar.org alone to a named scholarly naval history for the fleet's movements and, directly, for Villeneuve's own stated reasoning at the turn to Cádiz; several day-level dates still vary by a few days between otherwise reliable sources.** Readers of this entry should treat its dates as accurate to within a few days rather than exact: the Toulon departure is given as 29 or 30 March 1805 and the Martinique arrival as 12, 13, or 14 May across sources this canon consulted. The invasion plan depended on a temporary, local command of the Channel: Villeneuve's Franco-Spanish fleet was to break out of the Mediterranean, draw the Royal Navy off toward the West Indies, then double back and appear in the Channel long enough to escort the invasion barges across. Villeneuve sailed from Toulon 29 March 1805, evaded Nelson, passed Gibraltar 8 April, picked up Spanish ships at Cádiz, and reached Martinique in mid-May 1805. Pursued back across the Atlantic, his fleet fought the inconclusive Battle of Cape Finisterre on 22 July 1805, was forced into Vigo on 26 July, and put out again 30 July for Ferrol; rather than pushing on for Brest or the Channel once ordered to do so, Villeneuve — low on supplies, uncertain of British dispositions, and fearing entrapment — reversed course from off Ferrol/A Coruña and re-entered Cádiz around 20-21 August 1805, effectively abandoning the Channel rendezvous. His own stated reasoning at the turn survives in his own words, quoted directly by Corbett from Desbrière's archival compilation of French naval documents: "Seeing that I had no confidence in the condition of my ships, in their sailing, and in their power of manoeuvring together, the concentration of the enemy and the knowledge they possess of all my movements since I reached the coast of Spain, leave me no hope of being able to fulfil the grand object for which the fleet was destined" — a primary-adjacent statement of exactly the combination this canon's characterization already gave: British numerical and qualitative pressure, incomplete allied fleet concentration (the Ferrol squadron never fully joined him in time), and a justified fear of being caught and destroyed piecemeal in open water. General Lauriston, present with the fleet, separately judged in a contemporaneous report that Villeneuve had "lost his nerve."

Sources: [Wikipedia — Battle of Cape Finisterre (1805)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cape_Finisterre_(1805)); historyofwar.org, "Finisterre, Calder's battle off, 22 July 1805"; Julian S. Corbett, *The Campaign of Trafalgar* (London: Longmans, Green, 1910), full text at https://archive.org/details/cu31924027923410, pp. 258–259 and 271 — read directly, the standard scholarly naval history of the campaign this canon already relies on at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-trafalgar`, here corroborating the Finisterre and Cádiz-turn dates (Corbett gives the Cádiz re-entry as "the 20th") and supplying Villeneuve's own quoted reasoning, sourced there to Desbrière, *Trafalgar* (Projets), v. 726n and 786, an archival French naval-history compilation this canon has not itself opened directly, and Lauriston's "lost his nerve" judgment.

## Abandonment of the invasion and the march east {#coalition-abandon-invasion}

**Standing: established by research for the camp-breaking date; the specific 23 August intelligence date is not yet decided, no checkable source having been located for it.** Napoleon received word of Villeneuve's retreat together with intelligence that Austria was mobilizing; a widely repeated but not yet independently sourced date puts this around 23 August 1805. He gave the order to strike camp on 26 August (8 Fructidor) and the order to march on 29 August, per napoleon.org's own campaign timeline, which this canon prefers to the "26–27 August" formulation it previously carried without a source for the second day. This canon states both dates rather than compressing them, since striking camp and ordering the march are recorded as two distinct moments three days apart; the redesignation as the Grande Armée follows (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-boulogne`). Napoleon had already begun quietly repositioning some units eastward before the invasion was formally cancelled, suggesting the decision was building over days rather than made in a single instant, and that he was hedging even before Villeneuve's failure was confirmed.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Bullet Point #32: Did Napoleon really intend to invade Britain?" (Thierry Lentz), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/bullet-point-32-did-napoleon-really-intend-to-invade-britain/ — read in full; this article gives general framing only and states no date for either the intelligence or the camp-breaking, so it does not by itself support the dates above; napoleon.org, "Austerlitz, 2 December, 1805: a timeline," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/austerlitz-2-december-1805-a-timeline/, the actual source for the 26 August strike-camp order and the 29 August march order. David Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon* is not cited: this canon has not opened it.

## The Grande Armée's march to the Danube {#coalition-march-danube}

**Standing: established by research.** Seven corps marched east under named commanders: I Corps (Bernadotte), II Corps (Marmont), III Corps (Davout), IV Corps (Soult), V Corps (Lannes), VI Corps (Ney), VII Corps (Augereau), plus the Cavalry Reserve (Murat) and the Imperial Guard — roughly 145,000 infantry and 38,000 cavalry in the seven corps and reserve, with 25,000 Bavarian allies added once the army reached southern Germany. Bernadotte's I Corps, stationed in Hanover, marched via Würzburg to collect the Bavarian contingent; the other six corps converged on the middle Rhine and crossed in the last days of September (conventionally dated 26 September 1805, though World History Encyclopedia gives 25 September for the arrival-and-crossing — a one-day variance this canon notes rather than silently resolves), then fanned out on parallel roads toward the Danube in the *bataillon carré* formation detailed at `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-rhine-danube`, letting the army concentrate rapidly on whichever point the Austrians chose to stand.

Sources: World History Encyclopedia, "Ulm Campaign" (Harrison W. Mark), https://www.worldhistory.org/article/2249/ulm-campaign/, for corps strength figures and the Rhine-crossing date; napoleon.org, "First Bulletin of the Grande Armée," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/first-bulletin-of-the-grande-armee/, for Bernadotte's march from Hanover via Würzburg and Napoleon's own Strasbourg departure; [Wikipedia — Ulm campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulm_campaign), for corps routing.

## The Ulm campaign {#coalition-ulm}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact surrendered troop count disputed between sources.** General Karl Mack von Leiberich, nominally under Archduke Ferdinand, concentrated the Austrian Swabian army at Ulm, assuming the French main thrust would come frontally through the Black Forest, and built his defense around sealing those passes while awaiting Kutuzov's slower Russian army. He badly misjudged Napoleon's actual maneuver: the bulk of the Grande Armée swung in a broad wheel north and east of Ulm, crossing the Danube downstream of Mack's position and closing on his rear and communications while a screening force held his attention to the front. By 30 September–early October 1805, roughly eleven days after crossing the Rhine, French corps were already positioned to strike from behind Mack's line; related actions include Wertingen (8 October), Günzburg (9 October), Haslach-Jungingen (11 October), and Elchingen (14 October, Ney's action), which sealed the last escape route. This canon corrects its own earlier description of Haslach-Jungingen as "an Austrian tactical win but strategically irrelevant": it was neither. Dupont, with some 4,000–6,000 men, attacked rather than withdrew before a far larger Austrian force, and the aggression convinced Mack he faced a much heavier concentration than he did. The action counts as a French victory and was strategically load-bearing — it closed one of Mack's few remaining escape routes and helped hold him inside the trap Napoleon was closing. Mack agreed to surrender terms 17 October, and the formal capitulation and march-out occurred 20 October 1805 (`settings/002-chronology.md#chron-ulm-austerlitz`); officers were paroled and 60 guns taken. Sources vary on the exact figure: roughly 23,000–27,000 Austrian troops marched out and surrendered at Ulm itself on 20 October, while total Austrian losses across the entire campaign (including preliminary actions and the escaping remnant under Archduke Ferdinand) ran to roughly 50,000 against a total theater order of battle of roughly 70,000–72,000. This canon uses the narrower 20 October figure (23,000–27,000) for the capitulation event itself and the wider figure (~50,000) for total campaign losses, and treats both as established-but-approximate rather than reconciling them to one number.

Sources: historyofwar.org, "Battle of Ulm, 20 October 1805," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_ulm.html (27,000 surrendered at Ulm itself; roughly 50,000 total Austrian losses across the campaign); Wikipedia, "Ulm campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulm_campaign (citing Clodfelter, *Warfare and Armed Conflicts*, 2017, p. 152; Fisher &amp; Fremont-Barnes, 2004, p. 41; Nafziger, 2002, p. 282), whose infobox gives 25,000 at the capitulation and which dates the formal march-out to 21 rather than 20 October — a one-day discrepancy this canon notes but does not adopt; Wikipedia, "Battle of Haslach-Jungingen," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Haslach-Jungingen, which records the action as a French victory under Dupont and credits it with helping prevent the Austrian army escaping Napoleon's trap — the basis for the correction above; World History Encyclopedia, "Ulm Campaign" (Harrison W. Mark), citing Robert Goetz, *1805: Austerlitz* (Greenhill, 2005), for the itemized preliminary-action losses.

## Trafalgar and its irrelevance to the land campaign {#coalition-trafalgar}

**Standing: established by research.** Fought 21 October 1805 off Cape Trafalgar, southwestern Spain — the day after Ulm's capitulation. Nelson attacked the combined Franco-Spanish fleet under Villeneuve (who had sailed from Cádiz on Napoleon's orders after growing impatient with his inaction, and in part because Villeneuve had learned he was about to be replaced). The result was a decisive British victory, the combined fleet effectively destroyed with no British ships lost; of the thirty-three that had left Cádiz only eleven returned. Nelson was struck at about a quarter past one by a ball fired from the mizzen-top of the *Redoutable* and died at half past four that afternoon — roughly three and a quarter hours later, not "that evening" as this canon previously had it. The popular notion that "Trafalgar saved Britain from invasion" is a retrospective myth: the invasion plan had already collapsed with Villeneuve's retreat to Cádiz in mid-August (`settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-villeneuve`), and Napoleon's order redirecting the army eastward predates Trafalgar by nearly two months. News of Trafalgar reached Napoleon only weeks later, at Znaym in Moravia on 18 November, and had essentially no effect on his operational decisions — a point this canon can now state from his own hand rather than by inference, since his reply to Decrès that same day acknowledges the action and adds that it "changes nothing in my cruising plans"; the battle's true significance was long-term and maritime — permanent British naval supremacy — not an intervention altering the unfolding land campaign. This canon requires any downstream scene invoking Trafalgar in connection with Austerlitz to preserve this "myth versus reality" distinction rather than conflate the two events causally.

Sources: Julian S. Corbett, *The Campaign of Trafalgar* (London: Longmans, Green, 1910), full text at https://archive.org/details/cu31924027923410 — ch. ## Vienna to Moravia {#coalition-vienna-moravia}

**Standing: established by research.** After Ulm, the Grande Armée pushed down the Danube toward Vienna, racing to prevent Kutuzov's Russian army from linking with the remaining Austrian forces or reinforcing Vienna's defense; Kutuzov conducted a skillful fighting retreat, notably at Dürnstein/Krems (11 November 1805), falling back toward Moravia to await reinforcement and Alexander I's arrival in person. Vienna itself was an open, undefended city — all Austrian troops had evacuated and the national guard was doing duty in the streets — while the government and court had withdrawn first to Brünn in Moravia, moving on to Olmütz only as the French closed up behind them; Napoleon himself entered Brünn on 20 November. The strategically important obstacle was the Danube bridges, particularly the Tabor bridge, which the Austrians had prepared for demolition. French troops entered the capital and carried that bridge on the same day, 13 November 1805; what completed on the 14th was not the occupation but the army's passage through the city — Soult's corps crossing at nine in the morning, Davout's behind it — and Napoleon's installation at Schönbrunn.

## The Tabor bridge {#coalition-tabor-bridge}

**Standing: established by research that the bridge was taken intact on 13 November 1805 by parley rather than assault, and at Napoleon's own written prompting; disputed between sources as to who was present and what was said.** The bridge over the Danube north of Vienna was carried at daybreak on 13 November 1805 without a fight, the Austrian fireworkers failing repeatedly to fire the demolition charges while French officers talked with Austrian generals on the span. Two things about it this canon now states as established. First, the deception was not the marshals' improvisation: Napoleon had already instructed Murat, in the negotiations with the magistrates of Vienna, to see whether it could be arranged that the Danube bridges be left standing — an instruction the French general staff's own campaign history identifies as leading directly to the coup de main of 13 November. Second, Napoleon treated the trick as a known fact three days later, when his rebuke to Murat over Hollabrunn drew the parallel explicitly: "the Austrians let themselves be tricked over the passage of the Vienna bridge, and you let yourself be tricked by an aide-de-camp of the Emperor" (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#relation-napoleon-murat`).

The choreography, however, is genuinely unsettled, and the three accounts this canon has read do not agree. The 23rd Bulletin, written the next day, has a cavalry column cross after parleys with Austrian generals, with Lannes and General Bertrand first over, and places Murat crossing Vienna itself at ten in the morning rather than at the bridge; it names no Austrian commander and mentions no armistice story. Savary has Lannes and Murat together at the Austrian hussar post repeating the current armistice rumours until the post commander, taking them for ordinary officers, walked onto the bridge with them, while Lannes's grenadiers came up through the Prater claiming to be posting a picket and then crossed at the double, throwing the incendiaries into the water — the Austrian gunners not daring to fire because they could see their own officers in conversation on the span. Marbot has Murat and Lannes with German-speaking officers, Oudinot's grenadiers concealed behind plantations, and Prince Auersperg himself coming up and withdrawing his troops; Marbot alone names Auersperg at the bridge, and his chapter is demonstrably misdated, placing the episode after Napoleon was installed at Schönbrunn, which happened the following day. Marbot is also the only one to moralise about it — "was the stratagem which they employed permissible? I think not." A downstream dramatization may therefore stage the ruse as fact but must not present any particular exchange of words, or Auersperg's presence, as documented. Marbot's further claim that Auersperg was condemned to death and dragged on a hurdle is not adopted: this canon could not confirm it from any reliable source.

## The diplomatic feint before Austerlitz {#coalition-diplomatic-feint}

**Standing: established by research, with the date on which the retreat was staged disputed between sources and one previously carried episode withdrawn as unsupported.** Having occupied Vienna, Napoleon pushed north into Moravia to bring Kutuzov's Russians and the retreating Austrians to battle before further reinforcement, or Prussian intervention under the Treaty of Potsdam (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#prussia-frederick-william`), could change the balance. Berthier ordered Soult to Austerlitz on 20 November, and on 21 November Napoleon rode the Pratzen Heights and the Goldbach villages in person, telling his suite to look well at those heights because they would be fighting there, and describing the scheme he in fact executed eleven days later: that if he refused his right and drew it back towards Brünn, and the Russians left the heights, they would be caught and lost however many they were. This canon withdraws its previous claim that Soult and Lannes staged a feigned confused withdrawal from the Pratzen around 21 November — no source it has read supports one on that date, and the reconnaissance above is what the record puts there instead.

The theatrical retreat belongs later, and its date is disputed. The 30th Bulletin places it on 28 November: after the Allies took Wischau and Savary returned, Napoleon ordered a retreat at once, withdrew by night "as if he had suffered a defeat," took a good position three leagues back, and had it fortified and batteries established "with much ostentation." Napoléon &amp; Empire agrees, dating the abandonment of the Pratzen to 28 November; historyofwar.org instead places a feigned panicked retreat from the heights on 1 December and attributes it to Murat.

The Savary mission needs stating precisely, because the letter he carried is not what this canon previously described. Napoleon wrote to Alexander from Brünn on 25 November 1805, and that letter (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md#savary`) offers only compliments on the Tsar's arrival with his army: it proposes neither a meeting nor an armistice. Napoleon's own later account is explicit that the request for a meeting came afterwards, prompted by Savary's favourable report of his reception, and the armistice offer was a third and separate act made on 27 November on receiving Stadion's and Gyulai's full powers. Savary went twice; Alexander, rather than meeting Napoleon himself, sent his young aide-de-camp Prince Pyotr Dolgorukov (`settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#dolgorukov`) back with him on 29 November. Napoleon met Dolgorukov at the outposts, riding out from a bivouac on straw among the infantry, dismounting and walking the high road alone with him. Dolgorukov reported the French position weak and beatable, reinforcing the Allied war party's push for immediate attack — precisely as intended, and this canon can now state that intent from Napoleon's own hand rather than by inference: writing to the Elector of Württemberg on 5 December, he says Dolgorukov "must have taken my extreme moderation for a mark of great terror, which is what I wanted from the military point of view, and which gave rise to the battle of Austerlitz." This diplomatic theater directly produced the Allied decision to attack at Austerlitz on 2 December 1805 rather than continue withdrawing to await reinforcements — arguably the single most consequential piece of stagecraft in the campaign, and the direct precondition for the battle recorded at `settings/012-austerlitz.md`.
