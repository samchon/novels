**Status:** Source-supported. Napoleon's army prepared for Britain before coalition action redirected it east; the change required secrecy, orders, roads, and naval intelligence.

The 1805 correspondence introduction identifies two declared projects—an invasion of England and creation of an Italian kingdom—before Britain fostered a coalition with Russia and Austria that redirected Napoleon toward the continent. The detailed chronology dates the decision to transfer the Boulogne army on 25 August, the renaming as the Grande Armée and seven-corps organization on 26 August, and the order of march on 29 August; 25,000 remained at Boulogne under Brune. An army cannot turn from invasion to continental war without reassigning corps, maps, magazines, horses, roads, and couriers. The Channel project leaves training and organizational habits that the march toward Austria repurposes, while secrecy limits what each unit knows.

**Status:** Work canon. The 1805 march depends on corps routes, river crossings, allied territory, intelligence, and the ability to move faster than Austrian concentration.

The First Bulletin dated 7 October records separate Rhine crossings and routes: Davout through Mannheim and Heidelberg, Soult over the Speyer bridge toward Heilbronn, Ney opposite Durlach toward Stuttgart, and Bernadotte from Hanover through Frankfurt and Würzburg. Each corps therefore has a road head and tail, and a reported crossing is not a complete arrival. Bridges, weather, local officials, food, train depth, and enemy observation determine whether a timetable survives. A headquarters may know that a corps is on the march while not knowing its exact brigade or baggage position.
