# Elba and Restoration

This file owns Napoleon's first exile and return from Elba, ending when the French mainland response becomes the separate Hundred Days setting.

**Status:** Treaty-grounded and source-supported. Elba is sovereign property by treaty, but its scale and resources impose a restricted government.

Article III of the Fontainebleau treaty makes Elba a separate principality held by Napoleon for life; Article XVII permits a personal guard of 400 volunteers, officers, and soldiers. Fondation's place account describes Elba as roughly 28 by 19 kilometres, with Portoferraio as capital, and records Napoleon organizing a small government: Bertrand as secretary of state and interior minister, Drouot as military governor and war minister, and Cambronne as commander of Portoferraio. The same account says he worked on local resources and roads. These are executable limits: a title reaches ports, villages, stores, roads, and guards only through named staff, money, ships, and local cooperation. Elba is neither mere imprisonment nor a recovered continental empire.

**Status:** Source-supported with bounded operational inference. Exile's quality depends on accounts, stores, wages, and repairs, not title alone.

The treaty reserves family revenues in Article VI, returns Napoleon's estates to the French crown in Article IX, and addresses household debts in Article XII; it does not guarantee that every payment arrives promptly. On Elba, the named ministers and commander must convert legal entitlement into food, pay, guard duty, port work, and road maintenance. A ledger may show an amount due while a ship, official, or creditor controls its delivery. Islanders retain their own trades and households and may regard the new court as customer, burden, or risk. The setting therefore permits shortages, repairs, and negotiations but forbids an invented budget or a claim that every inhabitant shared the imperial household's interest.

**Status:** Source-critical. Elba receives information through slow, interested channels, not through an all-seeing imperial staff.

The correspondence chronology records family visits and letters during the exile, including Madame Mère joining Napoleon on 2 August, Marie Walewska and her son seeing him in September, Pauline joining in October, and Marie-Louise's final letter on 3 January 1815. It also records Colonel Campbell, the British commissioner, leaving Elba on 16 February, shortly before Napoleon's departure. Dates establish channels and absence as well as presence: a visitor carries memory and rumour, a letter carries an author's purpose, and a commissioner may observe without sharing every report. A newspaper or ship can be late or curated. The return decision may use estimates of Bourbon weakness and army loyalty, but scenes must show who brought the estimate, when it arrived, and what could still be false.

**Status:** Source-required domain. People around Elba carry different access, loyalties, and verification capacities.

The chronology identifies family visitors, a British commissioner, and the ministers who run the principality, but a named presence does not prove a secret allegiance. A relative may carry household news; a naval officer may report to an allied government; a merchant may care about a profitable port; a former soldier may seek pay or revenge. Each report therefore needs a sender, audience, route, date, and reason to disclose or conceal. A message can be intercepted, delayed, or planted, but those are possibilities to be evidenced rather than automatic plot devices. The setting allows competing loyalty calculations—Napoleon, Bourbon rule, family security, island livelihood, or simple obedience—without turning every visitor into an agent or every rumour into fact.

**Status:** Source-supported with source-critical route detail. The return is a chain of ships, landing, roads, and contacts rather than an instantaneous national welcome.

The direct 1814–1815 chronology records Napoleon leaving Elba on 26 February 1815 and arriving at Golfe-Juan on 1 March; that morning the ships entered the bay without hostile reaction, and men and matériel were unloaded before the afternoon landing. The same account says the 87th at Antibes did not join and the delegation sent there was left prisoner, while Napoleon reached Gap on 5 March by a difficult road. These details make the route executable: harbor control, unloading, provisions, weapons, weather, road capacity, proclamations, and inland receipt all matter. A silent landing is not a public victory, and a regiment that refuses is not evidence that every nearby unit will refuse or join.

**Status:** Source-critical. Arrest authority collapses through a sequence of local encounters, reports, and choices, not through a single national acclamation.

The correspondence chronology records the 87th at Antibes refusing to join, then the 5th of the Line rallying at Laffrey on 7 March with Colonel La Bedoyère, after which Napoleon could enter Grenoble. A regiment's choice changes the next garrison's information and risk, but it does not prove that every soldier loves Napoleon or that every Bourbon official has vanished. Motives can include command obedience, fear of civil war, resentment of royal policy, memory of imperial service, pay, and local safety. An arrest order may be issued but arrive late, meet a different formation, or be abandoned by its officer. The setting keeps each actor's knowledge bounded until contact and communication make a wider political conclusion possible.

**Status:** Work boundary. Public re-entry changes legitimacy, but the restored state remains an institutional process owned by the Hundred Days layer.

The direct chronology dates Napoleon's arrival in Paris to 20 March 1815, while the route account describes a twenty-day return from Golfe-Juan to the Tuileries. Neither date alone proves that ministries, treasury, police, courts, and provincial officials had already been reorganized. A proclamation can promise continuity or reform; implementation requires a signer, printer, courier, office, funds, guards, and a population able to receive it. The next setting may therefore inherit returning veterans, uncertain legitimacy, and coalition mobilization while researching which officials returned, which records survived, and which commands obeyed. This file closes with the mainland's changed political field, not with a claim that the former empire has already regained its former reach.

**Status:** Work boundary grounded in treaty and chronology. Elba ends when the mainland response changes; the Hundred Days begins as an inherited political and military problem.

The treaty establishes a limited principality and guard; the direct chronology establishes departure on 26 February, landing at Golfe-Juan on 1 March, the Laffrey rally on 7 March, and Paris on 20 March. Between those points lie uncertain reports, roads, garrisons, proclamations, and coalition decisions. The next owner inherits a former emperor with returning personnel and family strategy, a French state whose institutions may be reclaimed unevenly, and allied powers reacting to a breach of the settlement. It must not inherit the assumption that Elba's restricted authority became continental authority at the shoreline. The setting boundary is therefore temporal and material: the ship and landing are complete, while restoration, the Waterloo campaign, and the second abdication remain downstream.
