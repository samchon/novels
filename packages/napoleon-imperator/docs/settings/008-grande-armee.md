# The Grande Armée: Organization and Material Culture

This file records the organizational, logistical, and material facts of the French army circa 1805 that any scene involving soldiers, staff, or campaign life must be able to draw on without invention, per `principles/settings.md#constraint-sufficiency`.

## The corps d'armée system {#corps-system}

**Standing: established by research.** A corps d'armée was a self-contained, combined-arms army in miniature — typically two to four infantry divisions each with organic artillery, an attached cavalry division or brigade, a corps-level artillery reserve, and engineers and train — designed to hold its ground against, or fight off, an enemy army for at least a day until neighboring corps converged. Typical corps size ran roughly 20,000–40,000 men. Intellectual roots trace to the pre-Revolutionary theorist Comte de Guibert and to Marshal de Broglie's divisional experiments in the Seven Years' War; Napoleon permanently institutionalized the corps organization by 1800–1805, and the 1805 campaign — the Boulogne army reorganized into seven numbered corps along the Rhine — is its mature debut. The system let Napoleon disperse his army over a wide front (roughly 200 km along the Rhine in 1805, narrowing to some 90 km as it converged on Ulm) and then concentrate rapidly, within about 24 hours, for battle, freeing the army from strict dependence on supply depots and letting corps commanders operate under mission-type orders with real autonomy — directly enabling the encirclement of General Mack at Ulm (`settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-ulm`).

Sources: [napoleon-series.org — "The Development of The Corps D'Armée"](https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/organization/c_armycorps.html) (Guibert's and Broglie's theoretical roots, the corps's self-contained combined-arms design, and its 1805 debut). David Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon* is not cited: this canon has not opened it.

## Rank structure {#rank-structure}

**Standing: established by research.** French army ranks, 1805, infantry line, lowest to highest: soldat (private); caporal (corporal) and caporal-fourrier (corporal-quartermaster); sergent (sergeant); sergent-major (sergeant-major); adjudant sous-officier (senior battalion NCO); sous-lieutenant (second lieutenant); lieutenant; capitaine (captain) and capitaine adjudant-major (staff captain); chef de bataillon (infantry/artillery battalion commander) or chef d'escadron (cavalry equivalent, squadron commander); major and major en second (regimental second-in-command grades); lieutenant-colonel; colonel (regimental commander); général de brigade; général de division; maréchal d'Empire (a dignity of the Empire, not strictly a command grade, created 18–19 May 1804, see below). Cavalry used parallel NCO titles of equal grade: brigadier (=corporal), maréchal des logis (=sergeant), maréchal des logis-chef (=sergeant-major). Officers wore gold or silver epaulettes (color set by each regiment's button metal); NCOs and enlisted wore sleeve chevrons.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Ranks of the French Imperial Army (1804–1815)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranks_of_the_French_Imperial_Army_(1804%E2%80%931815)) (rank hierarchy and epaulette/chevron conventions, cross-checked against [Wikipedia — "French Imperial Army (1804–1815)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Imperial_Army_(1804%E2%80%931815))).

## The marshalate of 1804 {#marshalate-1804}

**Standing: established by research.** The Senate created the dignity of Marshal of the Empire by a decree of 18 May 1804, with names published 19 May 1804. Fourteen were named active field marshals: Berthier, Murat, Moncey, Jourdan, Masséna, Augereau, Bernadotte, Brune, Soult, Lannes, Mortier, Ney, Davout, and Bessières. Four were named honorary marshals, largely retired or senior figures given the dignity: Kellermann, Lefebvre, Pérignon, and Sérurier. Napoleon created twenty-six marshals in total across his reign, never more than roughly twenty simultaneously; the next batch after this founding cohort came in 1807. Individual figure entries for the marshals central to this canon are at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md`.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Marshal of the Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshal_of_the_Empire); [Wikipedia — "List of Marshals of the First French Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Marshals_of_the_First_French_Empire) (the 18 May 1804 sénatus-consulte, the fourteen active and four honorary marshals).

## The Imperial Guard in 1805 {#imperial-guard-1805}

**Standing: established by research, with Mortier's specific 1805 Guard portfolio not yet decided (contradicted between sources) and the Austerlitz-specific origin of the "Grognards" nickname disputed.** In 1805 the Guard was not yet divided into Old, Middle, and Young Guard — that tripartite structure developed from roughly 1806 (Middle Guard, the Fusilier regiments created 19 October and 15 December 1806) and 1809 onward (Young Guard, beginning with the Tirailleurs-Grenadiers); no downstream unit may use those terms for the Guard as it stood at Austerlitz. In 1805 it comprised eight core units: infantry — the 1st Regiment of Foot Grenadiers, the Foot Chasseurs regiment, and a battalion of "sailors of the Guard"; cavalry — Mounted Grenadiers, Mounted Chasseurs, the small Egyptian-recruited Mameluke squadron, and Elite Gendarmes; artillery — a squadron of Guard horse artillery — plus, at Austerlitz specifically, two attached battalions of the Italian Royal Guard, a campaign attachment rather than a ninth core unit. Marshal Jean-Baptiste Bessières was Colonel-General of the Guard Cavalry in 1805 (commanding roughly 5,500 men and 24 guns at Austerlitz) and effectively the field commander of the Guard as a whole; Davout held the Foot Grenadiers and Soult the Foot Chasseurs as honorific senior titles above Bessières's operational command, both solidly convergent across sources. Mortier's specific 1805 Guard portfolio is not yet decided: this canon previously asserted he held Guard artillery, but one source states instead that "in 1805, Mortier was made commander of the infantry of the Imperial Guard," directly conflicting with an artillery assignment, and this canon's research has not resolved which is correct — a downstream scene should not assign Mortier a specific Guard arm without further verification. The Guard functioned as Napoleon's elite tactical reserve and personal bodyguard, committed only sparingly; at Austerlitz it was held back for most of the day and saw limited action, its troops vocally frustrated at being left idle while line troops died — the Guard was engaged only at the crisis point against the Russian Imperial Guard counterattack (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-chronology`). This canon does not tie the nickname "les Grognards" (the Grumblers) specifically to that frustration at Austerlitz: the specialist sources it has read describe the privilege of grumbling in the Emperor's presence as a general, ongoing feature of Guard life rather than dating the coinage to any one battle, and this canon treats the Austerlitz-origin story as disputed rather than established.

Sources: napoleon.org, Jean-François Brun, "La Garde impériale (1/4)," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/la-garde-imperiale/, and "La Garde impériale : un outil de guerre (2/4)," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/la-garde-imperiale-un-outil-de-guerre-2/ (the campaign-by-campaign strength table showing the Guard fielding a single combined-arms division at Austerlitz, including the two attached Italian Royal Guard battalions); The Napoleon Series, Paul Dawson, "Grognards: Grenadier à Pied de la Garde 1799–1815," https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/organization/France/Guard/Infantry/Grenadiers/c_grognards.html; Wikipedia, "Order of battle at the Austerlitz campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_battle_at_the_Austerlitz_campaign (Bessières commanding 5,500 men and 24 guns; the unit list, including the Italian Royal Guard grenadiers); Wikipedia, "Imperial Guard (Napoleon I)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Guard_(Napoleon_I) (the Fusilier regiments of 19 October and 15 December 1806 and the first Young Guard regiment of 1809, supporting the 1806/1809 dating above); Wikipedia, "Édouard Mortier, 1st Duke of Treviso," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Mortier,_1st_Duke_of_Treviso, stating his 1805 command was of the Guard infantry — the source of the conflict with this canon's prior artillery claim, which remains unresolved between sources pending further research.

## Infantry organization {#infantry-organization}

**Standing: established by research.** The Revolutionary government replaced "regiment" with "demi-brigade" in 1794 to strip out monarchist connotations; on 24 September 1803 Napoleon officially restored "régiment" as the formal term. Each infantry regiment had three battalions, with total regimental strength ranging roughly 1,200–3,300 men depending on state of recruitment and campaign attrition. A battalion comprised eight fusilier (line) companies plus one grenadier (elite) company — nine in all. In 1804 Napoleon gave every battalion a voltigeur company of some ninety picked marksmen serving as elite skirmishers, but this was a conversion, not an addition: the voltigeurs replaced one of the existing fusilier companies, so the battalion still stood at nine companies, now seven fusilier, one grenadier, and one voltigeur. This canon states the mechanism explicitly because the commoner summary — that a company was "added" — implies a ten-company battalion and a corresponding rise in paper strength that did not occur. A company was nominally 122 men and officers under a captain. This canon corrects an internal error it previously carried here: the voltigeur company was not "roughly half" that size — its own cited source below gives it as roughly ninety picked marksmen at its 1804 introduction, rising toward near-parity with a full company (121 men) by 1807, so a scene set in this canon's 1805 window should show a voltigeur company under-strength relative to the line companies but not literally half their size. This canon has not independently confirmed a comparative figure for the grenadier company and does not extend the same claim to it without its own source. Line and light infantry (infanterie de ligne vs. légère) were organizationally near-identical — same drill, same weapons — differing chiefly in elite-company naming (light battalions had a carabinier company instead of grenadiers and a chasseur company instead of voltigeurs) and in dress and esprit de corps rather than genuinely different combat function.

Sources: Wikipedia, "French Imperial Army (1804–1815)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Imperial_Army_(1804%E2%80%931815); Wikipedia, "Demi-brigade," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demi-brigade, for the 1803 restoration of "régiment" — this canon has confirmed the year but not the exact day, and the 24 September 1803 date given above should be treated as the commonly repeated figure rather than one verified against the decree itself; Wikipedia, "Voltigeurs," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltigeurs, which states that the voltigeurs "replaced the second company of fusiliers in each existing infantry battalion" and numbered some ninety picked shots in 1804, rising to 121 men by 1807 — the basis for the conversion-not-addition correction recorded above.

## Cavalry organization {#cavalry-organization}

**Standing: established by research, with the 1805 dragoon-division roster corrected against an earlier anachronism.** Cuirassiers were heavy shock cavalry with breastplate armor (a practice Napoleon revived), regarded by him as the decisive battlefield arm. Dragoons were a "medium" cavalry type able in principle to fight mounted or dismounted. Chasseurs à cheval and hussars were light cavalry used for scouting, screening, and pursuit, hussars the most elaborately dressed. Guard cavalry (Mounted Grenadiers and Mounted Chasseurs of the Guard) fought tactically by company-sized units rather than ordinary squadrons. Cavalry regiments nominally had four squadrons of 150–200 men each at full establishment, though field strength typically ran 100–140 men per squadron after campaign attrition. In 1805 Napoleon gave Marshal Murat command of the Cavalry Reserve — all cavalry divisions not organically attached to individual infantry corps — comprising Nansouty's and d'Hautpoul's cuirassier divisions, four dragoon divisions under Klein, Walther, Beaumont, and Bourcier, Lasalle's hussars, and Milhaud's chasseurs. Grouchy and Sahuc are not available as dragoon-division commanders in an 1805 scene: they took the 2nd and 4th Dragoon Divisions only for the War of the Fourth Coalition in 1806–07, replacing Walther and Bourcier, and Sahuc was not promoted général de division until January 1806. Lasalle's attachment to the Reserve belongs to the Ulm phase of the campaign; he had moved to V Corps by the time of Austerlitz.

Sources: [napoleon-series.org — "1 French Cavalry Remounts and Replacements, December 1806 to December 1807"](https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/organization/France/Cavalry/Remounts/c_remounts1806-1807.pdf) (squadron composition: 2 companies per squadron, each with 1 capitaine, 4 lieutenants, NCOs, and roughly 96 privates at establishment); [napoleon-series.org — "The French Cavalry in Austria 1809" by Paul Dawson](https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/organization/France/Cavalry/FrenchCavalryinAustria.pdf) (field-strength attrition below establishment); Wikipedia, "Reserve Cavalry Corps (Grande Armée)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_Cavalry_Corps_(Grande_Arm%C3%A9e), which names the 1805 cuirassier divisions as Nansouty's and d'Hautpoul's and the four dragoon divisions as Klein's, Walther's, Beaumont's, and Bourcier's, and separately records Grouchy and Sahuc taking dragoon divisions only from the War of the Fourth Coalition — the basis for the roster correction recorded above; historyofwar.org, "Marshal Joachim Murat," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/people_murat.html.

## Artillery {#artillery-organization}

**Standing: established by research, with the guns-per-1,000-men ratio flagged as an approximate aggregated figure.** French artillery in 1805 was built on the Gribeauval system, standardized in the 1760s–70s: the 4-pounder (84 mm caliber, crew of 8, 3–4 horses, ~1,200 m max range), the 8-pounder (100 mm, crew of 13, 4 horses, ~1,500 m), the 12-pounder (121 mm, crew of 15, 6 horses, ~1,800 m — Napoleon's favored heavier battlefield gun), and the 6-inch Gribeauval howitzer (166 mm, crew of 13, 4 horses, ~1,200 m for shells, for high-angle explosive fire). Horse artillery (artillerie à cheval) had all crew mounted for rapid movement alongside cavalry; foot artillery (artillerie à pied) crews marched or rode the limbers, generally attached to infantry and slower. At Austerlitz Napoleon fielded roughly 2 guns per 1,000 men, a ratio this canon treats as approximate and rising in later campaigns (roughly 4 per 1,000 at Wagram, 4.5 per 1,000 at Borodino) rather than as a fixed doctrine.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Canon de 4 Gribeauval"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_de_4_Gribeauval); [Wikipedia — "Canon de 8 Gribeauval"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_de_8_Gribeauval); [Wikipedia — "Canon de 12 Gribeauval"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_de_12_Gribeauval); [Wikipedia — "Obusier de 6 pouces Gribeauval"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obusier_de_6_pouces_Gribeauval) (weights, calibers, crew sizes, and ranges for each gun class, cross-checked and internally consistent across the four articles).

## Logistics and march rates {#logistics-marching}

**Standing: established by research, with one popular attribution corrected as false.** A Napoleonic infantry column typically covered about 20–32 km per day under sustained normal conditions (roughly 2.5 mph for 5–8 hours on good roads); a forced march exceeded this, and French infantry could sustain forced marches of up to roughly 56 km (35 miles) in a single day in exceptional cases, at heavy cost in stragglers and cohesion. Davout's III Corps march from Vienna to the Austerlitz battlefield — most commonly cited as roughly 110–112 km in about 48 hours, though sources vary (some cite up to 80 miles/50 hours) — is the standout example this canon treats as decisive to the battle's outcome (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`). The line "an army marches on its stomach" is popularly attributed to Napoleon, but this attribution is almost certainly false; the closest documented precedent traces to Thomas Carlyle's 1858 history of Frederick the Great, explicitly crediting the underlying image to Frederick, who died in 1786 before Napoleon's rise — this canon does not permit the line to be placed in Napoleon's mouth as a direct quotation. Supply used a mixed system: official magasins for baseline provisioning, supplemented heavily by requisitioning and foraging, which the corps system's dispersal made more practical by spreading the burden over a wider area even as it strained relations with local populations. The official daily ration included roughly 750 g of bread (typically three parts rye to one part wheat), a half-pound of meat, and an ounce of rice or two ounces of dried legumes, plus a wine or beer allowance and lard — entitlements frequently unmet in the field, especially on forced marches or in stripped territory. Vivandières (cantinières), often soldiers' wives, sold or distributed food and drink to troops, including under fire on battle days, and nursed wounded.

Sources: [napoleon-series.org — Anthony Dawson, "An Army Marches on Its Stomach?"](https://www.napoleon-series.org) (ration entitlements and the false Frederick-the-Great/Carlyle attribution); Quote Investigator, "Army Marches On Its Stomach"; [Wikipedia — "Vivandière"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivandi%C3%A8re). The march-rate figures are retained as consistent with `settings/010-geography-and-distances.md#geography-distances-table`, established by research.

## Uniforms circa 1805 {#uniforms-1805}

**Standing: established by research, with the exact timing of shako adoption for specific units flagged as needing regiment-level verification if a scene requires it.** Line infantry wore a blue coat with red-piped white collar and cuffs, white-piped red lapels, blue cuff flaps and shoulder straps piped red, white turnbacks piped red, brass buttons; facing colors and trim varied by regiment. The bicorne was still the standard headdress for line infantry at Austerlitz; the shako did not become standard-issue for line infantry until roughly 1806–1808, though light infantry may have transitioned earlier. Grenadier companies wore red epaulettes and red-corded headwear; voltigeur companies (converted from a fusilier company in 1804, per `settings/008-grande-armee.md#infantry-organization`) had yellow collars and green-or-yellow epaulettes and cords. The 1805 campaign is noted as the first in which all participating armies were issued greatcoats as standard cold-weather equipment; the Moravian December cold and the frozen ground and ponds at Austerlitz (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-terrain`) are independently well documented, and a scene may reasonably show soldiers supplementing official issue with informal extra layers, though this canon does not assert as documented fact that greatcoat issue was itself insufficient.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Uniforms of the Grande Armée"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniforms_of_the_Grande_Arm%C3%A9e) (line infantry coat details; the bicorne standard through Austerlitz and Jena, replaced by shako per the 25 February 1806 decree; grenadier and voltigeur company distinctions).

## Weapons {#weapons}

**Standing: established by research, except the cuirassier pistol-compliance figure and the cuirassier carbine-issue date, both of which are not yet decided for the reasons stated below.** The standard infantry musket was the Charleville Model 1777 (as updated in the Year IX and Year XIII patterns), flintlock, smoothbore, .69 caliber (17.53 mm); accurate to roughly 200 yards against a massed column but only 80–100 yards against an individual target, which is why Napoleonic infantry fought in close-order volley lines rather than relying on aimed individual fire. A trained soldier fired roughly 2–3 rounds per minute. Light cavalry (hussars, chasseurs à cheval) carried the An XI light cavalry saber, introduced circa 1802–1803, plus pistols or a carbine depending on unit; cuirassiers carried heavier straight swords of the An IX/An XI lineage plus pistols. Two further claims this canon previously carried are withdrawn to "not yet decided": the figure that only about 20% of a given cuirassier unit actually carried pistols could not be traced to any source this canon has opened, and the date at which cuirassiers received carbines as standard issue is contested in the reachable literature — some accounts give 1812 as the year they first received a long firearm, while others describe carbines being *withdrawn* from cuirassier regiments in November 1812, which implies they already held them. A downstream scene may show a cuirassier with or without a carbine but must not state a date of issue as canon.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Charleville musket"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleville_musket) (Model 1777/An IX/An XIII patterns, .69 caliber, 44¾-inch barrel); [Wikipedia — "Sabre de cuirassier modèle An IX"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabre_de_cuirassier_mod%C3%A8le_An_IX); [Wikipedia — "Pistolet modèle An IX"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pistolet_mod%C3%A8le_An_IX).

## Battlefield medicine {#battlefield-medicine}

**Standing: established by research, with amputation-mortality figures disputed between sources and hedged accordingly rather than reconciled to one number.** Dominique Jean Larrey, surgeon-in-chief to the Consular and then Imperial Guard from 1801, held the senior surgical post in Napoleon's field army by the time of Ulm and Austerlitz in autumn 1805; sources disagree on the exact year (1804 or 1805) his formal title as chief surgeon of the wider Grande Armée specifically dates to, so this canon commits only to his seniority in the 1805 campaign, not a specific appointment date. He was himself wounded at Austerlitz while directing medical operations there, though no source this canon has opened gives the wound's location or severity, and none should be invented. Larrey's central innovation, introduced in 1793 and inspired by the mobility of the newly reorganized horse artillery, was the *ambulance volante* ("flying ambulance"): standard army regulation had kept ordinary heavy ambulance wagons roughly a league (~4 km) behind the fighting line, so a wounded man depended on his regimental surgeon's immediate first aid and then waited 24–36 hours for any further surgical unit to reach him; Larrey's light, sprung two-wheeled (flat ground) or four-wheeled (rough ground) wagons, with padded litters, could go up to and even into the firing line itself, cutting time-to-treatment to roughly one hour. The corps was organized into divisions of roughly 113 personnel apiece — a chief surgeon, some fifteen further surgeons, a trumpeter who carried the surgical instrument case, a drummer who managed dressings, plus drivers and litter-bearers. Larrey's triage doctrine, credited as a root of the modern concept though he never used the word himself, was to treat by severity of wound rather than rank: "we would always start with the most dangerously wounded without regard to rank or distinction." A wounded soldier's actual experience still depended heavily on which sector of the field he fell in: regimental surgeons, one or more per unit, were the routine first responders everywhere, while the flying ambulance corps proper was a Guard/army-level asset under Larrey personally and was not present in equal density across the whole field. There was no anesthesia (ether and chloroform lay decades in the future) and no antisepsis (Lister's work came in the 1860s); the primary "anesthetic" for amputation was surgical speed itself, backed by whatever alcohol was on hand. Larrey himself was renowned for extreme speed — commonly cited at roughly one minute for a leg and as little as seventeen seconds for an arm, and as many as some 200 amputations in 24 hours at Borodino in 1812 (outside this canon's 1805 window, usable only as an order-of-magnitude comparator, on the same convention this canon already applies to later-campaign artillery ratios) — and held that amputation performed within 24 hours of wounding succeeded far more often than one delayed to the third, fourth, or fifth day. Survival odds after amputation are genuinely disputed and this canon does not assert a single figure: one specialist medical-history review credits Larrey's own inverted-cone flap-and-irrigation technique with cutting amputation mortality from upward of 90% to under 25%, while a more generalist secondary account gives a rougher population-wide rule of thumb of roughly one survivor in three; gangrene, sepsis, and tetanus remained the chief post-operative killers regardless of surgical skill, given the period's total absence of germ theory. For Austerlitz specifically, hundreds of wounded were treated at a surgical outpatient department set up in the Blümegen family chateau at Šlapanice, and a French divisional field hospital was established in farm buildings at Jiříkovice; General Valhubert, severely wounded near Sokolnice, was evacuated to Brno (Brünn), where he died a few days later — one of the few individually documented evacuation routes from this battle, and construction-site archaeology near Rohlenka in 1995 recovered remains confirming a field hospital had stood there. At the aggregate level, 11,638 Russian wounded and sick were recorded as treated in Austrian and French hospitals after the battle (`settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-casualties`). Even so, the system's reach was far from complete: Napoleon's own night ride across the field on 2–3 December, ordering fires lit and the wounded gathered together and covered with dead men's coats, is separately documented, but secondary accounts describe hundreds or thousands of wounded on both sides dying in the following days from exposure and lack of care, and a typhoid epidemic subsequently spread through the district, receding only with harder frost around Christmas.

Sources: PMC, "Dominique-Jean Larrey (1766–1842): The Founder of the Modern Triage System," https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11246613/ (flying ambulance division organization and personnel, triage quotation, amputation speed and 24-hour-vs-later timing, the &gt;90%-to-&lt;25% mortality-reduction claim); Hektoen International, "Medical innovations made by doctors during the Napoleonic Wars," https://hekint.org/2019/05/10/medical-innovations-made-by-doctors-during-the-napoleonic-wars/ (corroborating organization and triage detail); Royal College of Surgeons, "Dominique Jean Larrey – Surgeon in Chief of Napoleon's Armies," https://www.rcseng.ac.uk/library-and-publications/library/blog/dominique-jean-larrey-surgeon-in-chief-of-napoleons-armies/ (the pre-Larrey "a league in rear" ambulance-wagon practice; the Borodino amputation figure); Wikipedia, "Dominique Jean Larrey," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique_Jean_Larrey (Guard surgeon-in-chief from 1801; wounded at Austerlitz); napoleonguide.com, "Medical Services During the Napoleonic Wars," https://www.napoleonguide.com/medical.htm (the contrasting, unreconciled roughly-one-in-three-survived figure, cited only as a flagged alternative, not this canon's preferred number); austerlitz.org, "History Trail of the Austerlitz Battlefield," https://www.austerlitz.org/en/history-trail-of-the-austerlitz-battlefield-list-of-locations/ (the Blümegen chateau field hospital at Šlapanice, the Jiříkovice divisional field hospital, General Valhubert's evacuation to and death in Brno, and the 1995 Rohlenka field-hospital archaeology); drticky.eu, "Austerlitz: After the Battle," https://www.drticky.eu/ftp/austerlitz/after.htm (Napoleon's night ride and order to gather the wounded, post-battle mortality from exposure, the subsequent typhoid epidemic). Aggregate hospitalization figures are retained as consistent with `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-casualties`, established by research.

## Command and staff structure {#command-staff}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon's imperial headquarters, the Grand Quartier Général Impérial, included a Military Household with seven aides-de-camp of the Emperor personally, each with subordinate aides of their own — a layered system for rapid, personally trusted message transmission. Marshal Berthier served as Chief of Staff of the Grande Armée from 1805, directing a staff of six generals and eight colonels; his function was not independent command but the precise transmission of Napoleon's orders to the marshals and corps commanders, with all army correspondence addressed to him and personally signed by him, making him the central administrative and communications hub of every operation (`settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-berthier`). Each marshal and corps commander maintained his own subordinate staff and aides beneath this overall structure, forming a hierarchy of message relay suited to a dispersed corps system that depended on fast, accurate written orders to coordinate widely separated formations.

Sources: [napoleon.org (Fondation Napoléon) — "Napoleon's imperial headquarters (1): organization and personnel"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/napoleons-imperial-headquarters-1-organization-and-personnel/) (the Military Household's aides-de-camp, Cabinet/Secretariat, intelligence, and topographical departments; Berthier's role as Chief of Staff 1799–1814 and the central signing/correspondence function); [Wikipedia — "Military Household of the Emperor"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Household_of_the_Emperor).

## Battlefield orders and command transmission {#battlefield-orders}

**Standing: established by research for the documented Austerlitz episodes; the general mechanics of speed, distance, and risk of miscommunication are this canon's own inference from those episodes and hedged where no single source gives a governing figure.** Before a battle, orders moved as formal written documents — Berthier's *Dispositions Générales*, drafted from Napoleon's dictation and circulated to corps commanders the previous evening (`#command-staff` above). Once fighting began, control shifted to a faster and far less reliable channel: mounted aides-de-camp riding at the gallop, carrying either a short written note or, once the battle itself was underway, a purely verbal order memorized and repeated on the ride. At Austerlitz on the morning of 2 December, the corps commanders gathered at Napoleon's Zuran-hill headquarters, received their orders, saluted, and rode off to their own corps; Napoleon held Soult back and asked how long his divisions needed to reach the top of the Pratzen Heights, and Soult answered "no more than twenty minutes" (per General Thiébault's account; Ségur's memoir gives ten, `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-weather`) — the ground distance cited from the Goldbach in front of Kobelnitz, where Saint-Hilaire's division stood, to the top of the Pratzenberg was approximately 2,000 meters, a concrete figure for the scale of ground a mounted order or an advancing division had to cross in this sector. Later in the same battle, an aide-de-camp "arriving in haste" reported that the enemy had begun its attack at Telnitz in force; Napoleon is reported to have replied "Now, then, is the moment," after which — in the version most commonly repeated, tracing to Archibald Alison's 1836 history, a popular nineteenth-century secondary source this canon has not verified against a primary account and flags accordingly — "the marshals set off at the gallop in all directions for their respective corps." Whether or not that exact phrasing is authentic, it is consistent with the mechanics attested elsewhere in the battle: an order given verbally at a headquarters conference and then physically carried back to a corps at speed. The upward channel worked the same way and was no more formalized: Colonel Alexandre de Girardin, aide-de-camp to Berthier, was sent forward with the narrow task of checking Saint-Hilaire's division's condition and reporting back, but instead stayed forward "riding incessantly to and fro from end to end of our lines," personally rallying troops as well as gathering information — evidence that an aide's nominal one-way reporting duty could become open-ended improvisation once he was on the ground and the situation was unclear. Napoleon himself, watching from a sequence of command posts over the day (the Zuran hillock at dawn, Staré Vinohrady from around 1 p.m., and finally the tower of the Chapel of St. Anthony for the battle's last stage, `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-terrain`), is described as "receiving reports from streams of aides-de-camp" throughout — a continuous, informal flow of riders rather than any single formalized signal. Visual signaling existed at the strategic level — the Chappe optical semaphore linked fixed towers over long distances, and Napoleon tried, with only partial success, to use it to direct Berthier's movements from a distance during the 1809 campaign, though even that rear-area strategic message was delayed a full day by ordinary fog — but no source this canon has opened describes flag or semaphore signaling used for tactical control once a battle itself had begun, and this canon does not assert a specific stated reason (such as gunpowder smoke obscuring the signals) that it cannot independently source; it treats mounted riders as the sole practical channel for in-battle order transmission at Austerlitz. Drums and cavalry trumpet or bugle calls at this period served marching cadence, formation commands, and unit-level signals (advance, halt, form square, charge) audible to the men of one's own unit at close range — not the relay of complex orders across a battlefield to a corps commander a kilometer or more away; no source located here describes that latter use, and this canon treats it as outside period practice. No source read for this canon documents a specific instance at Austerlitz of a courier killed, an order lost, or a verbal order garbled in transmission, and this canon does not invent one as established fact. But the structural conditions that make such failures plausible are independently documented: reliance on verbal orders once fighting began, distances on the order of hundreds of meters to a few kilometers between headquarters and a corps, and — on the losing side — General Kutuzov, wounded and swept along in the Russian retreat, later confessing he "could not report on the conduct of his officers" because "my location on that day did not permit me to see in person what was happening elsewhere on the field." A downstream scene may plausibly dramatize a delayed, garbled, or lost order as invented incident consistent with these conditions, but must not present a specific such failure as documented Austerlitz fact, per this canon's governing aim of showing how personally exercised power actually ran day to day (`settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#governing-aim`).
