# Chronology

This file is the dated skeleton of Napoleon Bonaparte's life, 1768–1821, against which every other file's claims must cohere. Each entry gives the governing date(s), Napoleon's age where relevant (computed against his birth date of 15 August 1769), and enough of the event to fix it in sequence; operational, tactical, and figure-level detail for the 1805 campaign lives in `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md`, and for the later campaigns in `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md`, so entries below for those periods are deliberately brief and cross-reference rather than duplicate.

## Corsica before his birth {#chron-corsica-annexation}

**Standing: established by research.** The Republic of Genoa, unable to suppress Pasquale Paoli's Corsican Republic, transferred Corsica to Louis XV's France by the Treaty of Versailles of 15 May 1768. The transfer was not an outright cession, and this canon does not describe it as one: the treaty's own title is *Conservation de l'isle de Corse à la République de Gênes*, and its fourth article binds France to hold the island only until Genoa should ask for it back and reimburse the costs of the expedition and its garrison, leaving Genoese sovereignty nominally intact. What made French possession permanent was conquest, not the treaty: royal forces under the Comte de Vaux crushed the Corsican army at Ponte Novu on 8–9 May 1769 — Paoli was not himself on the field, his second Carlo Salicetti commanding — ending organized independence and driving Paoli into exile in Britain, after which Corsica was constituted a French province in 1770. Napoleon's father, Carlo Buonaparte, had served as Paoli's secretary and aide but chose to reconcile with the new French administration rather than follow Paoli into exile, becoming assessor of the royal jurisdiction at Ajaccio in 1769 and a deputy of the Corsican nobility to the royal court from 1777. Napoleon was born just over three months after Corsican independence collapsed, and so was a subject of the French king from birth — a fact central to his lifelong ambivalent Corsican/French identity.

Sources: Wikipédia (fr), "Traité de Versailles (1768)," https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trait%C3%A9_de_Versailles_(1768), for the treaty's official title and for Article 4's provisional transfer with a Genoese right of redemption; Christian Ambrosi, "Les deux annexions de la Corse (1768 et 1789)," *Annales historiques de la Révolution française* no. 203 (1971), pp. 7–22, https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_1971_num_203_1_4024, for the scholarly reading that 1768 was an Old-Regime diplomatic transfer commanding neither Corsican consent nor completed assimilation; Wikipedia, "French conquest of Corsica," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_conquest_of_Corsica, for Ponte Novu, Salicetti's command in Paoli's absence, Paoli's flight, and Corsica's constitution as a French province in 1770; Wikipedia, "Carlo Buonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_Buonaparte, and Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Carlo-Maria Buonaparte," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/charles_bonaparte.php, for Carlo's assessorship of 1769 and the 1777 deputation. The phrase "a subject of the French king" was previously given here as a Britannica quotation; it does not appear in Britannica's article on Napoleon and the quotation marks and attribution are withdrawn, the statement being this canon's own.

## Birth and family sequence {#chron-birth}

**Standing: established by research, with the total number of pregnancies and the date of the family's noble recognition each disputed between sources.** Napoleon was born 15 August 1769 in Ajaccio, Corsica, the second of eight children to survive infancy of Carlo Buonaparte (b. 29 March 1746 per Britannica, 27 March per other reference works; d. 24 February 1785 at Montpellier) and Maria Letizia Ramolino (b. 24 August 1750, d. 2 February 1836), who had married 2 June 1764, Letizia being thirteen at the marriage. Sources differ on how many pregnancies preceded those eight survivors — the figure commonly given is thirteen, while Napoléon &amp; Empire counts twelve children — and this canon does not commit to one. The surviving order was: Joseph (7 January 1768), Napoleon (15 August 1769), Lucien (21 May 1775), Elisa (3 January 1777), Louis (2 September 1778), Pauline (20 October 1780), Caroline (25 March 1782), and Jérôme (15 November 1784); two earlier daughters and two later sons died in infancy or were stillborn. He was baptized 21 July 1771 at Ajaccio Cathedral. The family's formal recognition as noble is dated 19 August 1771 by Napoléon &amp; Empire and 13 September 1771 — the confirmation by the Conseil supérieur de la Corse — by Wikipedia and French sources; these are plausibly two stages of one process, and this canon records both rather than asserting either as the single act. Full figure entries for each surviving sibling and for both parents are at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md`.

Sources: Britannica, "Letizia Buonaparte," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Letizia-Buonaparte, and Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Maria-Letizia Ramolino-Buonaparte," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/laetitia_ramolino.php, both giving her birth as 24 August 1750 at Ajaccio and her death as 2 February 1836 at Rome, and the marriage as 2 June 1764 — correcting the 1749 birth year this canon previously carried; Britannica, "Carlo Maria Buonaparte," https://www.britannica.com/biography/Carlo-Maria-Buonaparte (29 March 1746 – 24 February 1785, Montpellier); Wikipedia, "Carlo Buonaparte," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_Buonaparte, for the birth dates of the eight surviving children, including Lucien on 21 May 1775 — correcting the 21 March date this canon previously carried — and for the 13 September 1771 confirmation of nobility; Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Carlo-Maria Buonaparte," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/personalities/charles_bonaparte.php, for the 19 August 1771 recognition and the count of twelve children. Full sibling-by-sibling detail and sourcing at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md`.

## Brienne and the École Militaire {#chron-education}

**Standing: established by research, with one minor discrepancy noted.** Napoleon entered the Royal Military School of Brienne-le-Château on 15 May 1779, aged 9, a school for children of impoverished nobility bound for military careers. He left Brienne for the École Militaire in Paris in late October 1784 (sources give either the 22nd or 30th, likely reflecting travel versus formal enrollment; this canon uses 30 October 1784), aged 15. His father's death on 24 February 1785 reduced family income and forced him to compress the normal two-year École Militaire course into one year; artillery examinations were held 6–12 September 1785. His commission as second lieutenant, La Fère artillery regiment, posted to Valence and assigned to the bombardier company, is dated 1 September 1785 and was announced 28 September 1785, aged 16.

Sources: [napoleon.org (Fondation Napoléon) — "1769–1793: Napoleon Bonaparte's early years"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/1769-1793-napoleon-bonapartes-early-years/). This canon has not opened Frédéric Masson's *Napoléon dans sa jeunesse* and therefore does not cite it.

## Early commission and Corsican years {#chron-early-commission}

**Standing: established by research, with the first-lieutenant date disputed between sources for a stated reason.** Napoleon took extended leave in Corsica September 1786 – June 1788 (ages 17–18), then rejoined his regiment at Auxonne, attached to the artillery school, on 15 June 1788. A second Corsica leave from September 1789 (age 20) drew him into revolutionary politics and renewed pursuit of Paoli's favor. He returned to regimental duty at Auxonne in the winter of 1791, aged 21, on a date the sources do not settle: Napoléon &amp; Empire's Auxonne page gives 11 or 12 February, its own chronology page gives 31 January, and the figure this canon previously carried was 10 February. His first-lieutenancy is dated differently by different sources because the commission and the posting are separate acts: his rank as first lieutenant was backdated to 1 April 1791, while his appointment to the 4th Artillery Regiment at Valence is dated 1 June 1791; he left Auxonne on 14 June and entered the Valence garrison on 16 June 1791. This canon does not collapse these into one date, and a downstream scene needing a single moment should use the Valence arrival of 16 June 1791 as the point at which he is serving in the rank. He was promoted captain 6 February 1792 (antedated commission, age 22).

Sources: Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Napoleon Bonaparte in Auxonne," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/napoleon-bonaparte-in-auxonne.php (arrival 15 June 1788; second-stay arrival 11–12 February 1791; appointment as first lieutenant to the 4th Artillery Regiment at Valence dated 1 June 1791, departure from Auxonne 14 June 1791); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Napoléon Bonaparte in Valence," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/napoleon-bonaparte-in-valence.php (entry into the Valence garrison 16 June 1791); Fondation Napoléon, "1769–1793: Napoleon Bonaparte's early years," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/1769-1793-napoleon-bonapartes-early-years/. The 1 April 1791 date widely given for the first-lieutenancy is the backdated rank rather than the appointment or the posting, which is the origin of the discrepancy this entry records rather than resolves.

## Siege of Toulon {#chron-toulon}

**Standing: established by research, with the siege's opening date disputed between sources — Britannica and Napoléon &amp; Empire give 28 August 1793, Wikipedia gives 29 August.** The republican Siege of Toulon ran 28 August – 19 December 1793, the Allied evacuation falling on 18 December and the French entry on the 19th. Captain Bonaparte arrived 16 September and was given command of the republican artillery by Antoine Saliceti after the incumbent artillery commander was wounded; his plan turned on seizing the Caire promontory — the batteries of l'Éguillette and Balaguier, dominated by the Allied redoubt at Fort Mulgrave, nicknamed "Little Gibraltar" — whose guns commanded the roadstead and whose loss forced the withdrawal of the Anglo-Spanish fleet; he was wounded in the thigh in the assault of 16–18 December. On the strength of this performance he was promoted brigadier general on 22 December 1793, aged 24 — the founding act of his military reputation and the earliest event this work treats as canonical background rather than dramatized scene.

Sources: Britannica, "Siege of Toulon," https://www.britannica.com/event/Siege-of-Toulon (28 August – 19 December 1793; Saliceti's appointment of Bonaparte to the artillery after his predecessor was wounded; promotion to brigadier general 22 December, aged 24); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Siege of Toulon (1793)," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/counter-revolution/toulon.php (Bonaparte's arrival at headquarters 16 September 1793; Dommartin's wounding; the Éguillette, Balaguier, and Fort Mulgrave objectives; the assault of 16–18 December and Bonaparte's thigh wound; the Allied evacuation of 18 December); Wikipedia, "Siege of Toulon (1793)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Toulon_(1793), which dates the siege's opening to 29 August and is the dissenting source noted in the standing above.

## Vendémiaire and the path to Italy {#chron-vendemiaire}

**Standing: established by research, with the casualty figure disputed between sources.** On 5 October 1795 (13 Vendémiaire Year IV), a royalist Parisian insurrection variously counted at 25,000–30,000 was dispersed by cannon fire from roughly 6,000 republicans with forty guns. Barras held the Convention's command; Bonaparte, aged 26, accepted a subordinate command on condition of complete freedom of movement and directed the two-hour engagement that decided it, having his horse shot under him. Casualties are not settled: around 300 royalist dead is the figure most often given, alongside some 90 republican dead and wounded, but a figure of 400 civilian dead circulates on the same authority and other reference works run as high as 1,400; this canon keeps the range rather than choosing. The phrase "whiff of grapeshot" describing this action is a coinage of the Scottish historian Thomas Carlyle, who used it as the title of *The French Revolution: A History* (1837), vol. III, book VII, ch. VII, and in his own narrating voice within it — not Napoleon's words, though popularly misattributed to him — this work treats the phrase as narratorial or later commentary, never as a line Napoleon or his contemporaries actually spoke. This victory led directly to his appointment to command the Army of Italy five months later.

Sources: Wikipedia, "13 Vendémiaire," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/13_Vend%C3%A9miaire, for the insurgent range of 25,000–30,000 against roughly 6,000 republicans with forty guns, Barras's command and Bonaparte's conditional acceptance of a subordinate one, and the competing casualty figures; Napoléon &amp; Empire, "13 Vendémiaire Year IV," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/counter-revolution/13_vendemiaire.php. On the Carlyle attribution, the chapter's placement is verified at Project Gutenberg ebook 1301, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1301/1301-h/1301-h.htm (ch. 3.7.VII), and at Wikisource, *The Works of Thomas Carlyle*, vol. 4, *The French Revolution*, vol. 3, book 7, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Works_of_Thomas_Carlyle/Volume_4/The_French_Revolution,_Volume_3/Book_7 — published 42 years after the event and 16 after Napoleon's death; this canon finds no contemporary attestation of Napoleon or his contemporaries using the phrase.

## Marriage to Joséphine {#chron-marriage-josephine}

**Standing: established by research.** Marriage banns were published 7 February 1796; Napoleon married Marie Joséphine Rose de Tascher de la Pagerie, widow of the guillotined Alexandre, Vicomte de Beauharnais, in a civil ceremony at the Mairie of the 2nd Arrondissement, Paris, on 9 March 1796. Joséphine, born 23 June 1763 in Martinique, was 32 at the marriage against Napoleon's 26 — roughly a six-year gap that scandalized his mother and sisters. Both falsified their ages on the marriage certificate (she recorded four years younger, he recorded about eighteen months older); full detail on the marriage's irregularities and the relation between the two figures is at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`.

Sources: full marriage detail, dating, and sourcing at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`, established by research; `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md` naming conventions govern "Joséphine" throughout.

## First Italian campaign {#chron-italian-campaign}

**Standing: established by research.** Bonaparte's Army of Italy campaign against Austria and Sardinia-Piedmont ran 1796–1797, including the Battle of Lodi (10 May 1796), the Battle of Castiglione (5 August 1796, defeating General Wurmser), the Battle of Arcole (15–17 November 1796), and the Battle of Rivoli (14–15 January 1797), which ended Austrian control of northern Italy. The campaign concluded with the Treaty of Campo Formio, 17 October 1797, ending the War of the First Coalition between Austria and France; Napoleon was 26 when he took the Army of Italy in late March 1796 and 28 at Campo Formio, so 26–28 across the campaign.

Sources: historyofwar.org, "Battle of Lodi, 10 May 1796," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_lodi.html; historyofwar.org, "Battle of Castiglione, 5 August 1796," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_castiglione.html (Wurmser defeated; the end of the first Austrian attempt to relieve Mantua); historyofwar.org, "Battle of Arcola, 15–17 November 1796," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_arcola.html; Bill Peterson, "The Campaign in Italy 1796–97: Rivoli, 14–15 January 1797," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/virtual/c_rivoli.html; and, for Campo Formio, the treaty text itself — public and secret articles transcribed from De Clercq, *Traités*, vol. I, pp. 335–343, at The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/diplomatic/c_campoformio1.html, whose Republican dating of 26 Vendémiaire Year VI fixes the signature at 17 October 1797. That page's English header reads "October 27, 1797," which contradicts its own Republican date and every other source consulted; this canon follows the Republican date and does not repeat the header.

## Egyptian expedition {#chron-egypt}

**Standing: established by research, with the expedition's strength and the Siege of Acre's dates disputed between sources.** A fleet of 55 warships and 280 transports — Wikipedia instead counts some 300 requisitioned vessels escorted by thirteen ships of the line and thirteen frigates, historyofwar.org thirteen of the line and seven frigates — carried roughly 36,000 combatants, near 38,000 counting the savants and other civilians, out of Toulon on 19 May 1798 (historyofwar.org gives 20 May). This canon states the range rather than the "roughly 35,000" it previously carried, which sits below every figure consulted. The fleet reached Malta on 9 June and took it 10–12 June, and landed at Alexandria on 1 July, the city falling on the 2nd, having evaded Nelson's pursuing squadron; Napoleon was 28. The Battle of the Pyramids (21 July 1798) was a decisive victory over Mamluk forces using divisional squares against cavalry. On 1–3 August 1798 Nelson annihilated the French fleet at anchor at the Battle of the Nile (Aboukir Bay) — eleven vessels lost or captured, Admiral Brueys killed — stranding Napoleon's army in Egypt though not affecting the concurrent land campaign directly. The Siege of Acre was an unsuccessful French siege of the Ottoman-held city and Napoleon's first major strategic defeat and personal reverse in a decade; its dates are genuinely unsettled, Britannica giving 18 March – 20 May 1799, Wikipedia 20 March – 21 May, and Napoléon &amp; Empire a start of 19 March with the siege lifted on either 11 or 17 May, so no downstream scene may treat a single day as canon. He secretly transferred command to General Kléber and departed by frigate (La Muiron) the night of 22–23 August 1799, aged 30, landing at Fréjus, France, 9 October 1799.

Sources: Napoléon &amp; Empire, "The Egyptian Campaign," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/egyptian-campaign.php (55 warships and 280 transports carrying 38,000 men from Toulon on 19 May 1798; the embarkation aboard *Muiron* on 23 August 1799 and the Ajaccio call of 1 October); Wikipedia, "French campaign in Egypt and Syria," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_campaign_in_Egypt_and_Syria (the alternative reckoning of ~300 vessels, thirteen of the line and thirteen frigates, and 30,800 infantry with 3,475 cavalry and 1,660 artillery); historyofwar.org, "The French Expedition to Egypt," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/wars_french_egypt.html (the third reckoning, and the 20 May and 2 July datings); Napoléon &amp; Empire, "Battle of the Pyramids," https://www.napoleon-empire.org/en/battles/pyramids.php (21 July 1798, 3 Thermidor Year VI, at Embabeh on the Giza plateau — note that the same site's campaign-overview page carries an evident typo of 1799 for this battle, which this canon does not follow); Wikipedia, "Battle of the Nile," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Nile (eleven of thirteen French ships of the line lost or taken; Brueys killed aboard *L'Orient*); Britannica, "Siege of Acre (1799)," https://www.britannica.com/event/Siege-of-Acre-1799, one of the three datings recorded above.

## Brumaire {#chron-brumaire}

**Standing: established by research.** The Coup of 18–19 Brumaire Year VIII (9–10 November 1799) replaced the Directory with a three-man Consulate. Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, a Director seeking a stronger executive, recruited Bonaparte as the coup's military guarantor. On 18 Brumaire the Council of Ancients, under Sieyès's influence, voted to relocate both legislative councils to Saint-Cloud, ostensibly to escape a "Jacobin plot" but actually to isolate them under troops loyal to Bonaparte. On 19 Brumaire, Bonaparte's speech to the Ancients went badly and he was physically confronted and shouted down in the Council of Five Hundred; his brother Lucien Bonaparte, President of the Five Hundred, and troops under Murat forcibly cleared the chamber. The cowed remnant then voted to replace the Directory with the Consulate (Sieyès, Ducos, Bonaparte), the vehicle for Bonaparte's rise to First Consul. This canon uses "Bonaparte" in narration through this date and "Napoleon" thereafter, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#naming-conventions`.

Sources: [napoleon.org (Fondation Napoléon) — "The coup d'État of 18 Brumaire"](https://www.napoleon.org); [Britannica — "Coup of 18–19 Brumaire"](https://www.britannica.com/event/Coup-of-18-19-Brumaire).

## Marengo and the Consulate settlement {#chron-marengo}

**Standing: established by research, with one common conflation corrected.** At the Battle of Marengo (14 June 1800), First Consul Bonaparte, with roughly 28,000 men against some 31,000 Austrians under General Michael Friedrich von Melas, was nearly defeated: Melas left the field believing the day won and delegated the pursuit. General Louis Desaix, recalled at about 1 p.m., reached the field between four and five and launched the infantry counter-attack from San Giuliano Vecchio — and was shot dead almost at its outset, before the outcome was settled. The decisive cavalry charge was not his but François Étienne Kellermann's, which struck the pursuing Austrian column in flank and broke it. This canon states both points explicitly because the popular version credits Desaix with the charge and has him fall at the moment of victory, and neither is accurate. The victory secured French control of northern Italy west of the Mincio and consolidated Napoleon's authority in Paris; he was 30.

Sources: Britannica, "Battle of Marengo," https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Marengo (roughly 28,000 French against some 31,000 Austrians under Michael Friedrich von Melas; Desaix recalled at 1 p.m. and arriving between four and five; "Desaix himself fell almost immediately"; the Austrian cession of northern Italy west of the Mincio); Roberto A. Scattolin, "Clash of Arms: Marengo, 14 June 1800," The Napoleon Series, https://www.napoleon-series.org/military-info/battles-campaigns/clash-of-arms-marengo-14-june-1800/ (Desaix's counter-attack from San Giuliano Vecchio, his death by a bullet through the heart, and Kellermann's following cavalry charge); historyofwar.org, "Battle of Marengo, 14 June 1800," https://www.historyofwar.org/articles/battles_marengo.html (Kellermann's charge into the flank of the pursuing Austrian column as the decisive stroke; the wounded Melas handing command to Kaim; the Convention of Alessandria the following day).

## Amiens and its collapse {#chron-amiens}

**Standing: established by research.** The Treaty of Amiens was signed 25/27 March 1802 (aged 32), giving Europe its only full peace of the period, lasting barely fourteen months. Britain declared war again on 18 May 1803 (aged 33), the proximate cause being Britain's refusal to evacuate Malta as agreed while Napoleon used the truce to annex Piedmont and intervene in Switzerland; this opened the War of the Third Coalition, detailed at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md`.

Sources: Britannica, "Treaty of Amiens," https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Amiens-1802 (27 March 1802 signing date; Britain's undertaking to evacuate Malta within three months); napoleon.org, "The Peace of Amiens," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/the-peace-of-amiens/, which dates the same treaty's signing to 25 March 1802 — the source of this canon's dual "25/27 March" date, retained deliberately rather than resolved to one day; Britannica, "Napoleonic Wars: The Third and Fourth Coalitions, 1803–07," https://www.britannica.com/event/Napoleonic-Wars/The-Third-and-Fourth-Coalitions-1803-07, for the 18 May 1803 declaration of war and Britain's retention of Malta "in defiance of the treaty" as the technical casus belli.

## Napoleonic Code and Empire proclaimed {#chron-empire-proclaimed}

**Standing: established by research.** The Code civil des Français ("Napoleonic Code") was promulgated 21 March 1804, aged 34; full content at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#napoleonic-code`. The Senate proclaimed Napoleon Emperor of the French by *sénatus-consulte* on 18 May 1804, with Joséphine becoming Empress; a nationwide plebiscite followed, its reliability discussed at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#empire-transition`. Also on 11 August 1804, Francis II of the Holy Roman Empire proclaimed himself hereditary Emperor of Austria as Francis I, a direct response to Napoleon's title; see `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Life of Josephine (Marie-Joseph-Rose de Tascher de La Pagerie)" (timeline), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/life-of-josephine-marie-joseph-rose-de-tascher-de-la-pagerie/ ("18 May, 1804: A sénatus-consulte proclaiming Bonaparte 'Emperor of the French' and Josephine 'Empress of the French' ... was approved unanimously (apart from three votes against and two abstentions)"); Wikipedia, "Constitution of the Year XII," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_XII, confirming the sénatus-consulte's date as 28 Floréal Year XII / 18 May 1804 and its effect of establishing the Empire with Napoleon as hereditary Emperor; plebiscite reliability discussed at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#empire-transition`.

## Coronation {#chron-coronation}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon was crowned Emperor at Notre-Dame, Paris, on 2 December 1804, aged 35, with Pope Pius VII present. Full ceremonial detail, including the self-crowning gesture and its historiography, is at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#concordat-1801`. This date recurs exactly one year later as the date of the Battle of Austerlitz — confirmed at `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-chronology` — a coincidence this canon records as fact, not as a scheduled narrative device.

Sources: Wikipedia, "Coronation of Napoleon," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_Napoleon ("December 2, 1804 (11 Frimaire XIII)" at "Notre-Dame de Paris," "in the presence of Pope Pius VII" — the same page already cited for ceremonial detail at `settings/009-institutions-and-economy.md#concordat-1801`); napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Consecration and Coronation" (Peter Hicks), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/napoleons-consecration-and-coronation/, independently confirming 2 December 1804 at Notre-Dame.

## Camp de Boulogne and the march east {#chron-boulogne}

**Standing: established by research.** The Armée des côtes de l'Océan (~150,000–200,000 men across camps) assembled at Boulogne and neighboring Channel ports from 1803, intended for a cross-Channel invasion of Britain. The camp was struck and the army, redesignated the Grande Armée, marched east toward the Rhine beginning 26–27 August 1805, aged 36, as the Third Coalition formed. Full detail at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-boulogne-camp`.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Bullet Point #32 — Did Napoleon really intend to invade Britain?," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/articles/bullet-point-32-did-napoleon-really-intend-to-invade-britain/ (confirms "more than 150,000 men, 10,000 to 15,000 horses and 500 artillery pieces" in the camps by 1805); Wikipedia, "Napoleon's planned invasion of the United Kingdom," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon%27s_planned_invasion_of_the_United_Kingdom ("a new army of 200,000 men... was gathered and trained," and "on 27 August 1805, Napoleon... had it break camp and march eastwards" — this canon's "26–27 August" is not pinned to a single day by either source and should be read as approximate rather than independently verified to both days).

## Ulm and Austerlitz {#chron-ulm-austerlitz}

**Standing: established by research.** General Mack's Austrian army capitulated at Ulm on 20 October 1805, surrendering an initial 23,000–27,000 troops (with total campaign losses in the theater reaching roughly 50,000); the day after, 21 October 1805, Nelson destroyed the Franco-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar, an action with no bearing on the unfolding land campaign. The Battle of Austerlitz, the "Battle of the Three Emperors," was fought 2 December 1805, aged 36 — exactly one year after the coronation — and ended the War of the Third Coalition with a decisive French victory. Full operational detail is at `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md`.

Sources: cross-reference to `settings/011-third-coalition-and-1805-campaign.md#coalition-ulm` and `settings/012-austerlitz.md#austerlitz-oob-french`, `#austerlitz-oob-allied`, each established by research with full sourcing there; [Wikipedia — "The Death of Nelson, 21 October 1805"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_of_Nelson,_21_October_1805) for Trafalgar's date.

## Jena–Auerstedt to Tilsit {#chron-jena-tilsit}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon crushed the Prussian army at the twin battles of Jena and Auerstedt on 14 October 1806, aged 37 (Berlin capitulated 24 October; Napoleon's own triumphal entry through the Brandenburg Gate followed 27 October); the Berlin Decree establishing the Continental System followed 21 November 1806, its legal architecture, administration, and economic effects detailed at `settings/021-the-continental-system.md`. The Battle of Eylau (7–8 February 1807) was a bloody tactical stalemate and Napoleon's first real check, though not a defeat. The Battle of Friedland (14 June 1807) was a decisive victory over Russia. The Treaties of Tilsit — with Russia signed 7 July 1807, with Prussia 9 July 1807, both negotiated in person with Tsar Alexander I on a raft on the Neman River — ended the War of the Fourth Coalition, made Russia a nominal French ally, and severely truncated Prussia.

Sources: [napoleon.org (Fondation Napoléon) — "The twin battles of Jena and Auerstedt, 14 October 1806"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/the-twin-battles-of-jena-and-auerstedt-14-october-1806/); [napoleon.org — "A close-up on: Napoleon's entry into Berlin, 27 October 1806"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/a-close-up-on-napoleons-entry-into-berlin-27-october-1806/) (Berlin's capitulation 24 October distinguished from Napoleon's entry 27 October); [napoleon.org — "A close-up on: the Polish campaign: Eylau (8 February 1807)"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/a-close-up-on-the-polish-campaign-eylau-8-february-1807/); [napoleon.org — "A close-up on: Tilsit (July 1807)"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/a-close-up-on-tilsit-july-1807/) (Russian treaty 7 July, Prussian treaty 9 July 1807).

## Peninsular War begins {#chron-peninsular-begins}

**Standing: established by research.** The Tumult of Aranjuez (17–19 March 1808) forced Charles IV of Spain's first abdication in favor of his son Ferdinand VII; the Madrid uprising against French troops followed 2 May 1808. At the Abdications of Bayonne (5–7 May 1808), Napoleon, aged 38, coerced both Charles IV and Ferdinand VII into successively renouncing the Spanish throne to him, installing his brother Joseph Bonaparte as Joseph I, King of Spain (proclaimed 7 July 1808, arrived Madrid late July). This ignited the Peninsular War, 1808–1814, detailed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-peninsular-war`.

Sources: [Wikipedia — "Abdications of Bayonne"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdications_of_Bayonne); full campaign detail and sourcing (Esdaile, napoleon.org) at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-peninsular-war`, established by research.

## Aspern-Essling and Wagram {#chron-wagram}

**Standing: established by research.** Preliminary Danube campaign victories at Abensberg, Landshut, Eggmühl, and Regensburg (20–23 April 1809) preceded the Battle of Aspern-Essling (21–22 May 1809, aged 39), commonly called Napoleon's first personal battlefield reverse — though whether it counts as an outright defeat is itself contested, per `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-fifth-coalition` — in which Marshal Jean Lannes was mortally wounded (died 31 May; full detail at `settings/005-marshals-and-command.md#marshal-lannes`). The Battle of Wagram (5–6 July 1809) — roughly 172,000 French and allied troops against roughly 136,000 Austrians under Archduke Charles, correcting a near-parity figure this canon previously carried — forced an armistice and the Treaty of Schönbrunn of 14 October 1809, also known as the Treaty of Vienna and not to be confused with the Prussian Treaty of Schönbrunn of 15 December 1805; both are detailed at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-fifth-coalition`.

Sources: cross-reference to `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-fifth-coalition`, established by research, where the corrected Wagram strengths, the Aspern-Essling losses, and the Treaty of Schönbrunn's articles are each given with their own locators — historyofwar.org and Napoléon &amp; Empire for the battles, and the treaty text at Wikisource.

## Divorce, remarriage, and an heir {#chron-divorce-remarriage}

**Standing: established by research.** Napoleon's civil divorce from Joséphine was formalized 15 December 1809, aged 40, both reading statements of continued devotion; a separate canonical annulment, needed for a Catholic remarriage, followed from the Diocesan Officiality of Paris in January 1810 and was announced in the Moniteur — this canon states the two steps distinctly rather than collapsing the religious dissolution into the December civil date. She became the first divorced Empress of France. A proxy marriage to Archduchess Marie-Louise of Austria (aged 18, grandniece of Marie-Antoinette) was conducted in Vienna 11 March 1810, Archduke Charles standing in for Napoleon; the civil ceremony followed at Saint-Cloud 1 April 1810 and the religious ceremony in the Louvre's Salon Carré 2 April 1810. Their son, Napoleon-François-Charles-Joseph Bonaparte ("the King of Rome," later Napoleon II/Duke of Reichstadt), was born at the Tuileries 20 March 1811, aged 41 — Napoleon's only legitimate child. Full relational detail at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#relation-napoleon-josephine`; the decision's own build-up, the civil ceremony's exact words, and the annulment's specific canonical grounds are at `settings/024-the-divorce.md`; the Russian alternative Napoleon weighed first, the proxy wedding, the journey and informal first meeting, and the April ceremonies are at `settings/025-the-austrian-marriage.md`; the difficult delivery, the salute, the title's legal basis, and the regency this marriage was ultimately meant to secure are at `settings/026-the-king-of-rome.md`.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Napoleon's Divorce" (timeline), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/napoleons-divorce/ ("15 December, 1809, 9pm: at the Assemblée de famille... the official dissolution of the civil marriage was announced," and the subsequent January 1810 canonical annulment); napoleon.org, "The marriage of Napoleon I and Marie-Louise of Austria" (timeline), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/the-marriage-of-napoleon-i-and-marie-louise-of-austria/ (the 11 March 1810 Vienna proxy marriage with Archduke Charles standing in; "1 April, 1810: the civil marriage... took place"; "2 April, 1810: the religious ceremony was held in the chapel at the Louvre"); napoleon.org, "The marriage ceremony (civil)," https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/images/the-marriage-ceremony-civil/, for the Saint-Cloud location of the 1 April ceremony; Wikipedia, "Napoleon II," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_II ("Napoleon II was born on 20 March 1811, at the Tuileries Palace").

## Russian campaign {#chron-russia-1812}

**Standing: established by research, with force and casualty figures disputed between sources.** The Grande Armée crossed the Niemen River into Russia 24 June 1812, aged 42. Sources disagree substantially on total force size — commonly cited figures range from roughly 450,000 (the initial invading spearhead most narrowly counted) to 600,000–680,000 (counting reserves and flank/support corps across the full theater); this canon treats both figures as established-but-disputed and does not silently collapse them, per `settings/001-aim-and-conventions.md#measurement-conventions`. The Battle of Borodino (7 September 1812) was fought roughly 110–125 km from Moscow (sources vary by measurement point, some citing up to 150 km) between some 130,000+ French and 120,000 Russians; single-day combined casualties are widely agreed to have exceeded 70,000, the bloodiest day of the Napoleonic Wars, though exact figures vary by source. Napoleon occupied Moscow 14 September 1812, abandoned the night before by Kutuzov's withdrawing army; the Great Fire of Moscow that followed is most often attributed to a deliberate order by Governor-General Rostopchin, though this is not universally accepted. The retreat began 19 October 1812 after Tsar Alexander I rejected peace overtures, and the catastrophic crossing of the Berezina River (26–29 November 1812) saw the Grande Armée's remnants force a passage under attack via improvised pontoon bridges. Of the invading force, only a small fraction returned in fighting condition — among the worst military disasters in history by scale, though precise totals are disputed for the reasons given at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-russia-1812`.

Sources: cross-reference to `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-russia-1812`, established by research (Lieven, *Russia Against Napoleon*, corroborated via David A. Bell's H-France review; Mikaberidze, *The Burning of Moscow*, corroborated via [napoleon.org's own review of the book](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-burning-of-moscow-napoleons-trail-by-fire-1812/), content verified via search-indexed excerpt), for the disputed force-size figures and the Barclay/Kutuzov strategic analysis; [napoleon.org — "The Battle of Borodino: Napoleon Against Kutuzov"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-battle-of-borodino-napoleon-against-kutuzov/) and Britannica, "French invasion of Russia," for the Borodino and Berezina narrative facts and the Moscow-distance source split.

## Leipzig and first abdication {#chron-1813-1814}

**Standing: established by research, with both armies' strengths disputed between sources and two previously carried figures withdrawn.** The Battle of Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations" (16–19 October 1813, aged 44), was the largest battle in European history before the First World War. Both armies grew across the four days: Napoleon fielded roughly 177,000 rising to roughly 190,000–195,000, against a coalition force (Russia, Prussia, Austria, Sweden) of roughly 257,000 rising to roughly 320,000–360,000. This canon withdraws the figures of 191,500 and 394,000 it previously carried, neither of which it can locate in any source it has read; full ranges and their sources are at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-leipzig`. French losses are commonly cited near 38,000 casualties plus some 30,000 more troops trapped and lost in the city, against an official Allied return of 53,804. The defeat ended French control of Germany. Napoleon's first abdication was signed at Fontainebleau 6 April 1814 (Treaty of Fontainebleau concluded that day, signed by Napoleon 11 April), negotiated by Marshals Ney and Macdonald and General Caulaincourt with the Austrian, Russian, and Prussian plenipotentiaries; terms gave him sovereignty over Elba, retention of the imperial title, and an annual revenue of 2 million francs in rentes. Whether that revenue was actually paid is not settled by anything this canon has read, and the claim that it was "never fully paid" is not asserted here; see `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-first-abdication-elba`. He departed Saint-Raphaël aboard HMS *Undaunted* 29 April 1814 and disembarked at Portoferraio, Elba, 3–4 May 1814, aged 44.

Sources: cross-reference to `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-leipzig`, established by research, for the disputed troop-strength ranges; [NEH — "Napoleon Went Into Exile Aboard a Ship Called the Undaunted"](https://www.neh.gov/article/napoleon-went-exile-aboard-ship-called-undaunted) and [Wikipedia — "Napoleon's first abdication"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon's_first_abdication) for the Saint-Raphaël departure (29 April 1814) and Portoferraio arrival (3 May, disembarked 4 May 1814).

## Waterloo and second abdication {#chron-waterloo}

**Standing: established by research, with the single-battle casualty split (as opposed to full-campaign totals) disputed between sources and not fully reconciled.** Napoleon won secondary engagements at Ligny (against Blücher's Prussians) and Quatre-Bras (against Wellington) on 16 June 1815, then was defeated at the Battle of Waterloo on 18 June 1815, aged 45: his roughly 72,000 troops against Wellington's roughly 68,000, reinforced during the battle by some 45,000 Prussians under Blücher — figures Britannica confirms exactly. Napoleon delayed his main assault until midday to let the ground dry, a delay that allowed Blücher's army to escape earlier French pursuit and reach the field; repeated French assaults on the Allied center failed, and the Prussian arrival on the French flank combined with the repulse of the Imperial Guard's final assault produced a French collapse. Single-battle casualty figures vary by source and this canon does not silently pick one: this canon's own prior figures (near 25,000 French killed/wounded plus 9,000 captured; near 15,000 Wellington; near 8,000 Prussian) could not be traced to a single source in this research pass; Wikipedia's infobox instead gives French total losses of 26,000–27,000 (25,000 killed/wounded plus 8,000 captured, with 6,000–7,000 overlap since many captured were also wounded), Wellington's Allied losses at 17,000, and Prussian losses at 6,604–7,000, while napoleon.org's own Blücher timeline states Prussian losses plainly as "7,000 men." The full-campaign totals (Ligny, Quatre-Bras, and Waterloo combined) are separately given at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-hundred-days-waterloo` and should not be conflated with the single-battle figures above. Napoleon returned to Paris 21 June 1815; the Chamber of Representatives, refusing to back continued resistance, pressed for his abdication, which he signed 22 June 1815, formally in favor of his son Napoleon II — rejected by the provisional government and the Coalition. Full detail at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-hundred-days-waterloo`.

Sources: Britannica, "Battle of Waterloo," https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Waterloo ("Napoleon's 72,000 French troops," Wellington's "68,000," "about 45,000 Prussians under Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher," and the abdication "June 22, 1815, four days after"); napoleon.org, "Blücher and the Prussians at Waterloo" (timeline), https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/timelines/blucher-and-the-prussians-at-waterloo/ (16 June Ligny/Quatre-Bras; the Wellington–Blücher meeting near Belle-Alliance 9–10 p.m. on 18 June; Napoleon's 22 June abdication; Prussian losses of "7,000 men"); Wikipedia, "Waterloo campaign," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_campaign ("Napoleon arrived in Paris, three days after Waterloo (21 June)"; "On 22 June 1815 he abdicated"); Wikipedia, "Battle of Waterloo," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo, for the single-battle casualty figures disputed above.

## Saint Helena and death {#chron-st-helena-death}

**Standing: established by research, with the cause of death disputed between the mainstream position and two named minority scholarly positions.** The *Northumberland* brought Saint Helena into view 14 October 1815 and anchored off Jamestown 15 October 1815, aged 46; Napoleon actually disembarked and set foot on the island 17 October 1815, taking temporary residence at The Briars, with an entourage of roughly 24, before moving to Longwood House on 10 December 1815. He died at Longwood 5 May 1821, aged 51 (1821 − 1769 = 52, minus one because 5 May precedes his 15 August birthday). The autopsy performed the next day by Dr. Francesco Antommarchi, before 16 witnesses including seven British medical observers, found the stomach almost entirely diseased with a tumor over 10 cm extending from cardia to pylorus, consistent with gastric cancer — the same disease that killed his father. A minority theory proposing chronic arsenic poisoning, based on 1960s hair-strand analysis, gained wide popular attention but has been substantially undermined by more recent comparative analysis (arsenic levels in Napoleon's hair are comparable to contemporaries', reflecting period-wide environmental exposure rather than deliberate poisoning). A second, separate minority theory argues for death by drug-interaction-induced cardiac arrhythmia rather than either cancer or poisoning. Modern scholarly consensus favors gastric cancer as cause of death, with both alternatives treated as named minority positions, not established fact. Full detail on the exile, the household, and the historiographical dispute is at `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-st-helena` and `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`.

Sources: cross-reference to `settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-death-dispute`, established by research, for full citations including the arsenic and cardiac-arrhythmia minority theories; [napoleon.org — "Saint Helena, an island under siege (15 to 17 October 1815)"](https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/saint-helena-island-siege-15-17-october-1815/), for the *Northumberland*'s 15 October anchoring distinguished from the 17 October disembarkation; PMC8572813 / PMID 33661330, "The gastric disease of Napoleon Bonaparte" (2021), [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572813/).
