**Status:** Source-supported work canon. Tax is a distributed process, not cash in one imperial vault.

The Banque de France history records the Caisse d'amortissement and the new receiver-generals of finance in each department as part of the institution's capitalization, showing how fiscal collection, guarantees, and credit intersected. A tax claim still requires assessment, register, collector, receipt, transport, remittance, and enforcement before it can pay a distant army. A ministry may count expected revenue while a collector lacks coin, a road is occupied, or a court delays seizure. The setting therefore identifies who assessed, who collected, who verified, who transported, and who bore the local consequence. A delayed payment can become arrears, a military shortage, or resistance; it never becomes immediate cash merely because a budget lists it.

**Status:** Source-required domain. War finance is experienced as goods, coin, debt, carts, and lost household capacity.

The Treaty of Lunéville provides a legal example: Article VII requires indemnity for dispossessed princes, Article VIII leaves debts attached to ceded territories under specified conditions, Articles IX–X address sequestration and repayment, and Article XVIII ends contributions and war impositions only after ratification exchange. A field requisition is a different object: an officer demands food, horses, carts, or lodging now, often before a treaty accountant can settle value. A merchant may be paid later or not at all; a village may lose seed grain while a headquarters records supply. Settings identify beneficiary, intermediary, quantity, authority, transport route, and household consequence rather than translating extraction into abstract victory.

**Status:** Source-supported and source-critical. Credit moves through confidence and instruments before it can become state spending.

The Banque de France history records its official creation on 18 February 1800, operations beginning two days later, administration by fifteen regents and three censors, discount of commercial bills carrying three signatures, and notes payable in specie at sight. It also records police suspicion that government influence could undermine confidence and a public assurance that the bank would not simply advance money to the state. These details establish a boundary: a bank balance is not an army's cash, and a government announcement cannot force merchants to accept paper without risk. Financial scenes specify currency, document, collateral, institution, maturity, rumor, and the distance between Paris credit and a campaigning paymaster.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Fiscal authority depends on records whose writing, checking, storage, and movement take time.

The Lunéville text repeatedly makes effects depend on ratification exchange, such as the ending of contributions and the movement of armies, demonstrating that legal and fiscal state changes have a clock. A receiver's register, a ministry's copy, a sealed order, a courier's receipt, and a later audit are separate records. A missing page may hide a payment, while a later ledger can summarize an earlier month without giving contemporaries its knowledge. The setting therefore names who writes, copies, verifies, stores, and transports each fiscal record. A forged receipt, delayed remittance, or unexamined deficit may alter what an official can prove; none should be resolved by giving the character a later archive.

**Status:** Work canon with bounded inference. A nominal wage is not the same as coin, rations, remittance, or household security.

The bank's capacity to discount private bills and the Treasury's separate payment obligations establish why a soldier's pay can be authorized without arriving at a barracks. A paymaster needs an order, funds, transport, and a functioning local exchange; a family needs the remittance to cross roads and borders. Deductions, arrears, ration substitution, merchant credit, illness, and lost boots translate fiscal delay into bodies and choices. One soldier may remain from loyalty, another from debt, and another leave because his household cannot survive. The setting records nominal amount, currency, delivery date, deductions, food issue, merchant substitute, remittance route, and family labor rather than inventing a single army-wide experience.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. Indemnity is simultaneously legal claim, political theatre, and material burden.

Lunéville Article VII assigns indemnity for dispossessed princes to the Germanic Empire collectively, while Articles VIII–X specify debts, sequestration, and repayment conditions; Article XVIII ends contributions only at ratification exchange. These clauses show why a treaty sum cannot be narrated as cash already collected. A signatory, payer, creditor, collector, treasury, and household may experience different dates and currencies. Public compliance may coexist with private delay, dispute over collateral, or local resistance. Later narratives must name the legal instrument and the people who bear its cost rather than turning “indemnity” into a universal moral label or assuming sovereignty changes when the first coin moves.

**Status:** Source-supported and source-critical. Imperial grants bind beneficiaries to a state whose legal change can remove liquidity overnight.

The Fondation's treasury analysis records a 25-million-franc civil list and, in 1810, an extraordinary Crown domain of property acquired through conquest or treaty. It also records that after the 1814 abdication Napoleon took about 3.98 million francs from the civil list while a larger private treasury was placed under sequestration, and that the annual 2.5-million-franc Elba revenue promised by Fontainebleau was not paid. These facts separate title, estate, pension, legal entitlement, and cash. A court beneficiary may possess a domain but lack liquid funds; a soldier's family may be poor while a minister is solvent; a grant can create obligations and a target for seizure. Household owners must preserve this scale difference.

**Status:** Work boundary. This file supplies fiscal mechanics; local taxes, estates, dowries, and household debts belong to dedicated owners.

Every later transaction must identify its scale, currency, institution, document, payer, recipient, time of payment, route, and person bearing risk. A national budget is not a village ledger; a bank note is not an army ration; a treaty indemnity is not a cart of grain; a civil-list grant is not household cash. The next storylines inherit a state able to assess, borrow, requisition, record, and promise, but unable to make money, transport, confidence, or trust instantaneous. Fiscal evidence must remain attached to its material and administrative chain.
