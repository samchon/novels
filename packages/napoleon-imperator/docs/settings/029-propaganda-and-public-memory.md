# Propaganda and Public Memory

This file owns the regime's public information systems and their limits.

## Bulletins and official victory {#bulletins-and-official-victory}

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Bulletins record what the regime wanted readers to believe as well as what headquarters knew.

A bulletin has an audience, delay, editor, and political purpose. It may identify commanders and broad outcomes reliably while inflating enemy losses or compressing uncertainty. A manuscript may quote it as a document, never as an omniscient narrator.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The correspondence of Napoleon,” https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/; Gallica, https://gallica.bnf.fr/html/und/histoire/napoleon-bonaparte.

## Censorship newspapers and theatre {#censorship-newspapers-and-theatre}

**Status:** Work canon. Newspapers, theatre, paintings, medals, ceremonies, and controlled printing create a public Napoleon while readers and spectators interpret the message unevenly.

The state can close a paper or authorize an image, but cannot control every private conversation. A proclamation reaches a market, barracks, salon, or village through different channels. Scenes must show circulation and reception rather than treating propaganda as automatic belief.

Sources: Archives nationales, https://www.archives-nationales.culture.gouv.fr/; Fondation Napoléon, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/biographies/napoleon-bonaparte/.

## Eagle standards and imperial ritual {#eagle-standards-and-imperial-ritual}

**Status:** Source-supported for symbols and ceremony; the emotional effect on every soldier is not measurable and remains bounded.

An eagle, uniform, oath, medal, or parade binds state, army, and memory through repeated performance. The object has material custody and political meaning. A ceremony may inspire some participants, embarrass others, and consume money and time.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon chronology, https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/chronologies/.

## Memoir and later legend {#memoir-and-later-legend}

**Status:** Historical principle. St Helena memoirs, aide-mémoires, enemy histories, and restoration-era accounts are retrospective constructions with valuable but uneven testimony.

Later memory can preserve a scene while relocating a clock, improving a line, or turning a collective act into one hero's decision. Each later source must be dated and compared with orders, letters, and independent reports.

Sources: Fondation Napoléon, “The correspondence of Napoleon,” https://fondationnapoleon.org/en/activities-and-services/telling-history/correspondence-napoleon/.
