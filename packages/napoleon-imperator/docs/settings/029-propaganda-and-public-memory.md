# Propaganda and Public Memory

This file owns the regime's public information systems and their limits.

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Bulletins record what the regime wanted readers to believe as well as what headquarters knew.

A bulletin has an audience, delay, editor, and political purpose. It may identify commanders and broad outcomes reliably while inflating enemy losses or compressing uncertainty. A manuscript may quote it as a document, never as an omniscient narrator. The setting must preserve the difference between a dispatch written under fire, a staff summary, a printed bulletin, and the civilian reader's later recollection.

**Status:** Work canon. Newspapers, theatre, paintings, medals, ceremonies, and controlled printing create a public Napoleon while readers and spectators interpret the message unevenly.

The state can close a paper or authorize an image, but cannot control every private conversation. A proclamation reaches a market, barracks, salon, or village through different channels. Scenes must show circulation and reception rather than treating propaganda as automatic belief. Paper, printers, couriers, licenses, and police determine what can be seen and when.

**Status:** Source-supported for symbols and ceremony; the emotional effect on every soldier is not measurable and remains bounded.

An eagle, uniform, oath, medal, or parade binds state, army, and memory through repeated performance. The object has material custody and political meaning. A ceremony may inspire some participants, embarrass others, and consume money and time. Settings must name who carries, guards, funds, and interprets the symbol instead of treating it as weightless inspiration.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Public silence may indicate agreement, fear, opportunism, lack of access, or a message that never arrived.

Police reports, informers, letters, sermons, jokes, and family conversation carry different risks and evidentiary limits. A newspaper reader can reject a claim without leaving a record. The novel must not infer universal belief from an official circulation figure or universal dissent from one private complaint; it must identify the setting, audience, and channel.

**Status:** Historical principle. St Helena memoirs, aide-mémoires, enemy histories, and restoration-era accounts are retrospective constructions with valuable but uneven testimony.

Later memory can preserve a scene while relocating a clock, improving a line, or turning a collective act into one hero's decision. Each later source must be dated and compared with orders, letters, and independent reports. A compelling anecdote is not self-authenticating merely because it appears in several memoirs copied from one another.

A monument records a decision about land, money, inscription, and audience, not only a battle. Different regimes can preserve, remove, or reinterpret the same object. Later narratives must distinguish a memory practice from the event remembered and must give communities agency in accepting, contesting, or repurposing public symbols.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. The surviving record is shaped by preservation, censorship, literacy, institutional power, and the interests of later collectors.

An absent letter is not proof that an event did not happen, while a preserved official file is not proof that its claims are true. Settings must mark silence, damaged chains, and unequal access to writing. The manuscript can dramatize an archival gap only as a character's uncertainty, not as a license to invent a hidden fact.
