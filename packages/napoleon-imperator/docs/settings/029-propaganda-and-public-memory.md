# Propaganda and Public Memory

This file owns the regime's public information systems and their limits, from official bulletins to later memory practices.

## Bulletins and official victory {#bulletins-and-official-victory}
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#scope-preservation This H2 owns dispatch, staff summary, bulletin, audience, delay, editing, victory framing, and reader reception. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#scope-preservation #cc1e606 Compared campaign communication context with the press and memoir sources; battlefield report and public bulletin remain distinct. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#substantive-completion This H2 settles how an official account can preserve an event while shaping its political meaning. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#substantive-completion #ef2e621 Tested bulletin-as-omniscience against producer, time, audience, enemy-loss claims, and later correction. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#proportionate-development This H2 separates observation, dispatch, staff edit, printed bulletin, courier, reader, and later recollection. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#proportionate-development #0a1be6f Checked each communication stage for an identifiable medium and information limit. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance This host uses official communication and source-critical framing without inventing a specific bulletin quotation or circulation number. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked source roles and qualified all enemy-loss and uncertainty claims. -->

**Status:** Source-critical rule. Bulletins are evidence of both military reporting and the regime's desired public interpretation.

A report written near combat, a staff summary, a printed bulletin, and a civilian's later memory travel through different hands and clocks. The official press order of 17 January 1800 limited Paris newspapers to thirteen named papers plus specialist sheets, showing that circulation was administratively shaped before the Empire; it does not prove that every bulletin was false. A bulletin may identify a commander, place, or broad outcome accurately while compressing confusion or inflating enemy losses. Settings therefore record producer, audience, transmission delay, editorial purpose, and what the reader could independently know. A manuscript may quote a bulletin as a dated document, never as an omniscient narrator who knows the battlefield's final result before the paper arrives.

Sources: Napoleon Series, “Order for Suppressing the Newspapers,” 17 January 1800, https://www.napoleon-series.org/research/government/legislation/c_newspapers.html; Fondation Napoléon, “The Genesis of Napoleonic propaganda, 1796–1799,” https://www.napoleon.org/en/magazine/publications/the-genesis-of-napoleonic-propaganda-1796-1799/.

<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#scope-preservation #cc1e606 Compared the 1800 order with the 1810 censorship study; police supervision and formal book censorship are related but not identical systems. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#substantive-completion This H2 settles how state control shapes available expression without guaranteeing belief or eliminating private circulation. -->

<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#proportionate-development #0a1be6f Checked every medium for its own gate, cost, timing, and reception. -->
<!-- @evidence obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance This host cites the critical censorship study and primary press order without claiming complete control over every expression. -->
<!-- @evidenceReview obligations/common.md#evidence-content-conformance #01c3e3a Rechecked the study's distinction between police supervision and formal censorship. -->

**Status:** Source-critical. The regime built strong controls, but the systems were experimental, uneven, and medium-specific.

Victor Coffin's critical study says formal censorship of non-periodic publications was established through the Ministry of the Interior's Direction Générale de l'Imprimerie et de la Librairie in 1810, while police supervision of expression had operated earlier. It expressly warns that the formal bureau cannot be treated as the complete censorship of the period. The January 1800 order likewise names permitted newspapers and requires police reporting on papers printed elsewhere. These are production constraints—paper, type, approval, printer, police report, venue, and courier—not proof of automatic audience belief. A theatrical performance, an authorized painting, a licensed newspaper, and a private joke reach different publics and carry different risks. Scenes must show who can print or stage something and how spectators interpret it.

**Status:** Source-supported for objects and ceremony; emotional effect remains bounded and unmeasurable.

The Fondation's eagle record identifies the 10 July 1804 imperial arms and the 5 December 1804 Champ de Mars distribution ceremony as a military equivalent of coronation. Its study of David's January 1812 standards records the 25 December 1811 decree: eagles and flags were to be delivered by the Emperor, defended to the death, and marked with a regiment's battles and the formula “Emperor Napoleon to” that regiment. The same study notes debate over the eagle's weight and Napoleon's refusal to lighten it. These details make the symbol material: someone manufactures, pays, transports, guards, carries, swears, and repairs it. A ceremony may inspire one participant, embarrass another, or simply consume a day's movement; the setting never converts ritual into universal feeling.

**Status:** Source-required domain. Public silence is underdetermined: it may mean agreement, fear, opportunism, lack of access, or no message received.

The censorship study describes administrative supervision, but a police file records what an informer or official chose to report, not every conversation that occurred. A letter has a named writer and recipient; a sermon has a congregation; a joke has a risk-bearing setting; a newspaper reader may reject a claim without leaving evidence. A market, barracks, salon, or family room also differs in access to print and in the danger of being overheard. The setting therefore refuses both universal belief and universal dissent. Later scenes must identify speaker, audience, medium, location, and what survives into the archive. Silence can be a meaningful political condition, but it cannot be assigned a single motive without a source.

**Status:** Historical principle. Saint Helena memoirs are valuable retrospective constructions whose production conditions must remain visible.

The Fondation's memoir analysis says Las Cases's *Mémorial* was described by Gourgaud as more a composition than a real journal and notes that a 1816 version was later expanded before the 1823 publication. O'Meara's account claimed he wrote conversations down after leaving Napoleon, yet that remains a mediated memory and a published political text. A separate Fondation account describes Napoleon dictating, revising, and correcting histories at Saint Helena and calls the resulting memoir production a work of propaganda that nourished the nineteenth-century cult. These sources can preserve testimony, but repeated lines may derive from one editorial tradition. Every memoir claim must therefore carry date, access, author, editor, audience, and comparison with orders or letters; a compelling anecdote is never self-authenticating.

**Status:** Work boundary. Commemoration belongs to later political uses of the past, not to the event's immediate evidence alone.

A monument fixes land, budget, inscription, patron, builder, and audience; a schoolbook selects language; a veteran's story travels through memory and status; a cemetery can be sacred, contested, or neglected. The Fondation's analysis of the Fontainebleau farewell shows how Vernet's painting and Épinal prints transformed a 20 April 1814 event into reproducible emotional imagery, with composition, color, and imperial symbols doing political work. That image is evidence of later representation, not proof that every soldier stood or felt as depicted. Later settings must identify who funded, removed, preserved, or repurposed a memory object and how communities responded. Commemoration may honor, domesticate, condemn, or commercialize the same past.

**Status:** Source-critical boundary. The surviving record reflects power, preservation, literacy, censorship, and later collecting.

An absent letter may have been lost, never written, intercepted, destroyed, or preserved outside the consulted archive; a surviving official file may be accurate about its existence and self-serving about its content. The memoir studies show why a published text can expand, edit, or politically frame earlier notes, while the censorship study shows why some expression was less likely to survive publicly. The setting records damaged chains and unequal access as facts about knowledge. A manuscript may dramatize an archivist, family member, or soldier facing a gap, but it cannot fill that gap with an invented hidden event and call it history. Absence limits confidence; it does not prove either silence or conspiracy.

**Status:** Work boundary. This file supplies propaganda and memory mechanics; detailed post-imperial reception belongs to dedicated downstream owners.

A victorious bulletin, hostile pamphlet, veteran recollection, nineteenth-century monument, and modern digital image may concern the same battle while performing different political work. The later owner must record when each was produced, who circulated it, what audience could access it, which source it copied, and what claim it makes. The Saint Helena studies demonstrate why an apparently intimate quotation can be a dictated, edited, or published construction; the Fontainebleau image study demonstrates why a visual scene can become a legend through reproduction. The boundary therefore hands forward provenance, audience, circulation, omission, and contest—not a single canonical public memory.
