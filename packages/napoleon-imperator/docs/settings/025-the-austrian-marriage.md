# The Austrian Marriage

This file gives the Austrian marriage negotiation and its ceremonies the granular, scene-level detail `settings/002-chronology.md#chron-divorce-remarriage` dates but does not carry, and does not repeat what is already fixed at `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-marie-louise` and `#relation-napoleon-marie-louise` or at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#francis-ii-i`. Metternich's figure-level unit is at `settings/007-foreign-crowns-and-commanders.md#metternich`. This file states facts and constraints, not a scene schedule, per `principles/settings.md#execution-neutrality`.

## Negotiation and alternatives: the Russian match and its collapse {#marriage-negotiation}

**Standing: established by research, with the degree to which Alexander I's own reluctance versus his mother's opposition actually drove the Russian match's collapse flagged as disputed among historians.** Napoleon first raised, through Champagny's instruction to ambassador Caulaincourt on 22 November 1809, a marriage to Grand Duchess Anna Pavlovna, Tsar Alexander I's sister, then aged 14; on 13 December 1809 Napoleon told the Russian government a response was needed by the end of January 1810. Alexander's response was consistently dilatory: he professed personal honor at the prospect but said the decision rested with his mother, the Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna, whose hostility to Napoleon was well known — Baron Ernouf's biography of Maret records "her aversion for Napoleon and for France was so well known that the cabinet was well aware of her invincible resistance." A more skeptical historical reading, from Albert Vandal's *Napoléon et Alexandre Ier*, holds Alexander may himself have used his mother's known opposition as cover for his own reluctance, running the Russian and Austrian options in parallel without foreclosing either. Caulaincourt's own memoirs show him relaying Napoleon's direct questions about the Grand Duchesses and warning that the earlier failed Swedish match (Grand Duchess Marie/Gustav IV Adolphe) showed religious conversion would again be a sticking point — a conduit executing and reporting the negotiation rather than an active advocate pushing Napoleon toward Russia specifically. Austria's side of the eventual pivot began well before the French decision to abandon Russia: as early as 29 November 1809, before the divorce was even final, Metternich told French chargé d'affaires Laborde he had "véritablement offert l'archiduchesse Marie-Louise en holocauste à l'Ogre Corse" (genuinely offered the Archduchess Marie-Louise as a sacrifice to the Corsican Ogre), calling such a union "un véritable bonheur pour les Autrichiens et une gloire pour son ministère" while insisting he spoke only for himself; Emperor Francis separately hinted the same idea to Comte de Narbonne around the same time, quoting the Habsburg motto "Bella gerant alii, tu, felix Austria, nube" (let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry). At a 16 December 1809 Tuileries reception — Joséphine's last public appearance beside Napoleon — an Austrian embassy secretary reassured a French senator that Emperor Francis and his family, including the reputedly anti-French Empress Maria-Ludovica, were open to a "union de famille." Most strikingly, Joséphine herself actively brokered her own successor: on 2 January 1810, at Malmaison, she told Countess Metternich directly, "J'ai un projet... c'est que l'Empereur épouse votre archiduchesse. Je lui en ai parlé hier et il m'a dit que son choix n'était point encore fixé. Mais je crois qu'il le serait s'il était sûr d'être accepté de vous" (I have a plan: that the Emperor marry your archduchess; I spoke to him of it yesterday and he told me his choice was not yet fixed, but I believe it would be if he were certain of being accepted by you) — a remark independently confirmed both in a French-language napoleon.org quotation and in the archive.org English translation of Countess Metternich's own letter reporting it to her husband, 3 January 1810. On 15 February 1810 Metternich informed Marie-Louise herself that Napoleon wished to marry her; her reply, per biographer Paul Ganière: "Je ne veux que ce que mon devoir me commande de vouloir" (I want only what my duty commands me to want). The formal French pivot itself came fast: on 6 February 1810, Napoleon had Prince Eugène ask Austrian ambassador Schwarzenberg, without warning, whether he was prepared to sign the marriage contract the next day; Schwarzenberg, with no authority to answer, gave a positive answer within hours regardless. Couriers sent that day or the next to Caulaincourt in St. Petersburg to abandon the Russian project crossed paths mid-route with a courier from Caulaincourt reporting Alexander had "with great regret" bowed to his mother's will — this canon records the French pivot itself as a matter of one to two days once Schwarzenberg's answer was in hand, while the underlying Austrian diplomatic groundwork ran roughly ten weeks longer, from Metternich's late-November 1809 opening through the February 1810 signature, and does not collapse the two timescales into one. Napoleon's formal letter requesting the marriage reached Emperor Francis I on 23 February 1810; Berthier formally asked for Marie-Louise's hand in Vienna on 8 March.

## The proxy wedding in Vienna, 11 March 1810 {#marriage-proxy-wedding}

**Standing: established by research.** On 9 March 1810, Marie-Louise renounced under oath on the Gospels all rights to the Austrian crown, once the marriage contract, already ratified in Paris, was ratified in Vienna. The religious proxy marriage was celebrated "en grande pompe" on 11 March in the Augustinerkirche, with Archduke Charles — Napoleon's own defeated adversary at Aspern-Essling (`settings/013-later-campaigns-and-death.md#later-fifth-coalition`) — standing in as proxy groom, a choice this canon treats as deliberate on Napoleon's part, a gesture of homage to his unfortunate opponent rather than an incidental assignment. Berthier, as Napoleon's ambassadeur extraordinaire, had arrived in Vienna on 6 March "in great pomp," lodging at the Imperial Chancellery, and formally requested Marie-Louise's hand at the Hofburg on 8 March, presenting a medallion portrait of Napoleon set with twelve large diamonds. That evening, Marie-Louise, now titled Empress of the French, presided over a state banquet. The near-crisis noted at `settings/024-the-divorce.md#divorce-annulment` belongs chronologically here: the Archbishop of Vienna's doubts about the validity of Napoleon's canonical annulment nearly disrupted the ceremony's run-up in the days immediately before 11 March, and had to be personally resolved by Metternich together with the French ambassador Otto. Marie-Louise left Vienna two days later, on 13 March; a contemporary near-riot broke out among Viennese crowds reproaching Emperor Francis for "sacrificing" his daughter — a genuine popular ambivalence this canon records rather than presenting the marriage as universally welcomed in Austria.

Sources: Paul Ganière, "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 300 (1978), read in full via the fetch helper — the source for the 9-11 March sequence, the Augustinerkirche location, Archduke Charles as proxy, Berthier's 6-8 March mission and the diamond medallion, the state banquet, and the 13 March Viennese near-riot.

## Journey to France and the informal first meeting {#marriage-journey-meeting}

**Standing: established by research, correcting the commonly repeated claim that the informal first meeting took place at Compiègne itself.** The bridal cortège comprised 83 carriages, 454 horses, and roughly 300 people. Route and dates: Vienna departure 13 March; St. Pölten, Enns, Ried, then Braunau-sur-Inn on 16 March, where the formal "remise" (handover) at the Franco-Bavarian symbolic border stripped Marie-Louise of every Austrian attendant and possession, down to her pet canary and dog, before she re-emerged "French"; Munich (18th), Ulm (19th), Stuttgart (20th), Karlsruhe (21st), Strasbourg (22nd, where she was first noticed, unremarked, by the Austrian officer Adam-Albert von Neipperg — her future husband after Napoleon's fall, `settings/004-family-and-relations.md#family-marie-louise`), Lunéville (24th), Nancy (25th), Vitry-le-François (26th), and the Braine/Courcelles-sur-Vesle area on 27 March, en route to a planned overnight reception at Soissons before the formal welcome at Compiègne. The informal meeting happened that day, 27 March, at Courcelles-sur-Vesle on the Soissons-Reims road — not at Compiègne itself, though that is the commonly repeated shorthand. Napoleon, unable to wait, left Compiègne at noon with Murat in an unmarked carriage, broke a wheel near Braine, and sheltered under the porch of the village church at Courcelles-sur-Vesle. When the imperial cortège stopped there to change horses around 4:00 p.m., Napoleon strode to the carriage, entered it, and embraced Marie-Louise: "Madame, j'éprouve grand plaisir à vous voir" (Madame, I am very glad to see you). She, once she understood who he was, replied: "Sire, vous êtes bien mieux que sur vos portraits" (Sire, you are much handsomer than your portraits). He then sent a courier ahead to announce their arrival at Compiègne that same night around 10:00 p.m., and the party proceeded directly through Soissons — skipping the planned overnight reception and banquet there entirely — arriving at Compiègne that evening. Marie-Louise, per her own 27 March letter to her father — "Depuis mon arrivée, je suis presque continuellement avec lui, et il m'aime extrêmement" (since my arrival I have been almost continuously with him, and he loves me extremely) — was relieved rather than offended by the breach of protocol, since it spared her the rehearsed formal genuflection and speech she had been coached to deliver. That night at Compiègne, per Constant's memoirs (`settings/029-eyewitness-memoirs.md#source-constant`, this canon's own access to which is via Ganière's synthesis rather than an independently opened edition) and Napoleon's own later recollections at St. Helena, Napoleon asked Cardinal Fesch, "Suis-je marié?" (Am I married?), to which Fesch answered, "Oui, Sire, selon la loi civile, mais peut-être pas tout à fait selon le droit canonique" (Yes, Sire, according to civil law, but perhaps not entirely according to canon law) — Napoleon proceeded regardless, citing the historical precedent of Henri IV and Marie de Médicis.

Sources: Paul Ganière, "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," *Revue du Souvenir Napoléonien* no. 300 (1978), pp. 7-16, read in full via the fetch helper — the sole source for this file's journey itinerary, the Braunau handover, the Strasbourg sighting of Neipperg, the Courcelles-sur-Vesle meeting and its dialogue, Marie-Louise's 27 March letter to Francis I, and the Compiègne wedding-night exchange with Fesch; this canon treats Ganière's 1978 article, published in the Fondation Napoléon's own specialist journal and drawing on court memoirs and Marie-Louise's own correspondence, as a scholarly secondary synthesis — the interpretive narration is Ganière's own voice, while the directly quoted letters and dialogue are primary material he reproduces.

## The April 1810 ceremonies {#marriage-april-ceremonies}

**Standing: established by research, with Parisian street-level public reaction to these specific ceremonies (as opposed to court reaction at Compiègne) not located in this canon's research and flagged as a gap.** The civil ceremony, 1 April, Saint-Cloud: per the official *Description des cérémonies et des fêtes... par Charles Percier et P.F.L. Fontaine* (1810), Cambacérès, as Prince Archichancelier, officiated the civil declarations, asking Napoleon, "Sire, votre Majesté Impériale et Royale déclare-t-elle prendre en mariage S.A.I. et R. Marie-Louise..." (Sire, does your Imperial and Royal Majesty declare that you take in marriage her Imperial and Royal Highness Marie-Louise), with both parties answering, "Je déclare prendre en mariage," before Cambacérès pronounced them married. All of Napoleon's siblings and their spouses attended and signed the register in strict rank order; artillery salutes were echoed by cannon at the Invalides in Paris; the evening included theater, illumination of the park and grounds, fountains lit in colored fire, dancing, and multiple orchestras, described in the period account as producing "les effets les plus brillants" and "un spectacle vraiment magique." The religious ceremony followed 2 April in the Louvre's Salon Carré, officiated by Cardinal Fesch as Grand Aumônier of the Empire (`settings/006-court-and-ministers.md`); the Arc de Triomphe de l'Étoile, then under construction, was specially completed in wood and canvas for the day's procession. Thirteen cardinals, including Ercole Consalvi, boycotted this religious ceremony in protest — the "cardinaux noirs" episode recorded at `settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md#church-excommunicate-empire`, tied specifically to this 2 April date rather than to the January annulment. This canon did not locate a source describing Parisian street reaction to the April ceremonies specifically, as distinct from the well-documented court reaction at Compiègne (where courtiers, per Ganière, "en arrivent à regretter Joséphine," come to miss Joséphine, on witnessing Marie-Louise's cooler formality); a downstream scene needing that texture should treat it as invented rather than documented.

Sources: napoleon.org, "Déroulement du mariage civil de Napoléon Ier et Marie-Louise, 1er avril 1810," verbatim extract of Percier and Fontaine's *Description des cérémonies et des fêtes* (1810), read in full via the fetch helper — the sole source for the civil-ceremony dialogue and evening festivities quoted above; Wikipedia, "Pope Pius VII," and "Ercole Consalvi," both opened directly and cross-checked, for the "cardinaux noirs" boycott's tie to the 2 April ceremony specifically, both citing the Catholic Encyclopedia's Napoleon I entry as their ultimate source, unreachable in this research pass and flagged accordingly at `settings/022-the-church-and-the-papacy.md#church-excommunicate-empire`; Paul Ganière, "Marie-Louise et Napoléon à Compiègne," as above, for the courtiers' comparative-regret remark.
