# Identity and Language

This file sets the English novel’s treatment of identity, names, code-switching, disability, migration, class, faith, and self-description. It is a representation contract, not a catalogue of labels and not a plot engine.

## Self-description before category

**Status:** Work decision — representation canon.

Characters may describe themselves, decline a label, use different words with different audiences, or change a label over time. The narrator does not assign an identity from appearance, accent, paperwork, clothing, disability aid, family origin, or relationship. A category used by an institution may be necessary for a form while remaining incomplete as lived description.

The prose gives a person ordinary preferences, expertise, humour, and contradiction. No identity is a shorthand for virtue, danger, poverty, foreignness, or access wisdom. When a label matters to a decision, the text states who uses it, for what purpose, and whether the person has corrected it.

## Names, order, and honorifics

**Status:** Work decision — English-language canon.

Korean names remain in the order chosen by the character or document. A family name, given name, nickname, romanization, and school register entry may differ. The English prose supplies context rather than forcing every name into one global convention. A person may ask peers to use a shortened or English name without that choice erasing the Korean name.

Honorifics and titles are translated by relationship and register where possible. *Seonsaengnim* may be retained when its sound or social work matters, with context showing that it is a teacher address. A title is not proof of legal authority; a name without a title is not proof of disrespect.

## Code-switching as social action

**Status:** Work decision — language rule.

Korean, English, Spanish, and mixed speech can signal audience, intimacy, precision, humour, exclusion, urgency, or a search for an untranslatable phrase. The novel renders dialogue in English while marking a meaningful switch through syntax, retained term, register change, or a translation note within action.

Nonstandard grammar is not used as a comic defect. Recurrent code-switching remains intelligible without a glossary. If a speaker changes language to exclude or protect someone, the focal character can observe the effect without automatically knowing the private intention.

## Translation and untranslatable remainder

**Status:** Work decision — linguistic evidence rule.

A translation records source, audience, purpose, and uncertainty. Words such as *nunchi* or *jeong* are retained only when their cultural work cannot be carried by a flat synonym; surrounding action conveys enough meaning for an English reader. A translator may provide alternatives and ask the speaker to choose.

The setting forbids “perfect translation” claims when register, joke, legal force, or emotional implication changes. An official translation may have a responsible service; a peer paraphrase is labelled as such. Misunderstanding is repaired through clarification, not by making the non-English speaker magically fluent in the narrator’s needs.

## Migration and belonging

**Status:** Work decision — social canon.

A migrant or diasporic character can belong to Haneul while retaining other places, languages, foods, memories, and obligations. Immigration status is private and never inferred from accent or surname. A school or civic form may ask a legally relevant question; a peer has no right to ask for documentary proof.

Migration is not an automatic explanation for every cultural difference. A family may have lived in Haneul for generations and still use multiple languages; a newly arrived person may prefer local slang. The group asks what help is wanted instead of turning a person into a tour guide or translation machine.

## Disability and body variation

**Status:** Work decision — access and dignity canon.

Disability is treated as an interaction between body, mind, environment, design, and social response. A person can have changing energy, use different aids, mask a need, or refuse to explain it. Do-yun’s measurements cannot stand in for another person’s lived access.

The prose names barriers concretely—slope, glare, crowd, noise, reach, timing, or inaccessible information—and preserves the person’s agency. Disability is not a metaphor for moral purity, tragic destiny, or inspirational achievement. Assistance is offered, not grabbed; a refusal is accepted unless immediate danger requires intervention.

## Gender, family, and social expectation

**Status:** Work decision — representation canon.

Gender expression, pronouns, family role, school uniform, and public expectation may align or conflict. Characters identify themselves and correct others. The novel does not make a person’s gender a puzzle for the reader or use misnaming as a cheap joke.

Care, leadership, technical skill, translation, and emotional labour are distributed rather than assigned by gender. A character may conform in one setting and resist in another without the narrative declaring one performance the true self. Safety concerns are taken seriously without making gendered fear the only story available.

## Faith, food, and observance

**Status:** Work decision — pluralism canon.

Characters may be religious, nonreligious, questioning, privately observant, or part of a family with mixed practice. Food, clothing, prayer, holy days, and silence are handled as lived choices rather than exotic props. A schedule request names the practical need and does not demand a theological essay.

An association or family member may explain a practice, but no person represents every believer. The club checks ingredients, rest space, timing, and privacy when relevant. A refusal to participate in a ritual is not disrespect; an invitation is not consent.

## Class, work, and dignity

**Status:** Work decision — economic representation.

Class appears through time, housing, transport, tools, debt, work, food, and institutional confidence, not through caricature or a single brand. A student who works after school may be skilled, tired, ambitious, resentful, generous, or all at once. A family receiving aid is not morally indebted to a club.

The founders do not use a poorer peer’s hardship to make a grant application vivid. Costs and unpaid labour are made visible so that redesign is possible. Wealth also has constraints—privacy, pressure, family authority, or mobility—and is not treated as proof of happiness.

## Accent, dialect, and linguistic prejudice

**Status:** Work decision — dialogue canon.

Accent and dialect convey history and context but do not determine intelligence, honesty, or education. The prose may show a listener misunderstanding a word or registering a register change, then preserve the speaker’s competence. A character can code-switch toward formal institutional speech without the narrative declaring their home speech inferior.

Mockery of language is shown as a social action with an affected person and repair cost, not as neutral banter. Translation access is designed rather than demanded from the person with the least institutional power. A public notice is assessed for clarity, not for closeness to one prestige variety.

## Identity records and correction

**Status:** Work decision — administrative boundary.

A school or civic record may contain a name, contact, category, or accommodation field for a defined purpose. The record owner, correction route, retention period, and disclosure scope are identified. A club notebook does not copy a sensitive field merely because an official form contains it.

When a record misnames or misclassifies someone, the founders help route a correction without publishing the error. A corrected field does not require the person to perform gratitude. Historical copies may be retained under an authority’s rule but are not used as a current identity without checking.

## Reader-facing context

**Status:** Work decision — English access contract.

The novel gives English-language readers enough action and context to understand Korean school terms, public institutions, and social registers without turning characters into lectures. A retained Korean term earns its place through sound, relationship, or institutional precision. A footnote, document gloss, or conversational clarification is used when ambiguity would block comprehension.

The text does not translate away power. “Please” can still be a command in context; a formal title can coexist with disagreement. Readers receive a stable distinction between the language of narration, the language spoken, and the authority of a document.

## Refusal, privacy, and unfinished identity

**Status:** Work decision — dignity rule.

A person may decline a label, translation, photograph, interview, pronoun explanation, family history, or public correction. The founders may record that a request was declined without recording the private reason. Some identity questions remain unresolved because the person has not chosen to answer.

Unfinished knowledge is not a narrative defect. The setting protects a person's right to be known through ordinary action rather than mined for a secret. Any later change in self-description is handled as current truth with a documented transition where an institution needs one, not as evidence that an earlier self was fraudulent.
